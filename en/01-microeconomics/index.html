Track 01 · Core theory

Microeconomics

微观经济学

The study of how households and firms make choices under scarce resources, and how millions of those choices are coordinated through prices. It is the shared language of nearly every branch of economics—the macroeconomics, finance, labor, and industrial organization that follow all build on the tools laid down here.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • Analyze real decisions using opportunity cost, and draw the production possibilities frontier to explain increasing marginal opportunity cost
  • Use the supply-and-demand model to predict the direction of price and quantity changes, and distinguish a "shift of the curve" from a "movement along the curve"
  • Draw budget lines and indifference curves, and state the consumer's optimality condition MRS = the price ratio
  • Explain the relationship between marginal cost and average cost, and why sunk costs never enter a decision
  • Compare output, price, and welfare loss (DWL) under perfect competition versus monopoly, and compute a simple deadweight-loss triangle
  • Identify the four market failures—externalities, public goods, and asymmetric information among them—and explain the corrective logic of Pigouvian taxes and property rights
  • Evaluate a resource allocation or a policy with the Pareto efficiency criterion, and explain clearly why efficiency and equity can be handled separately
Prerequisites: None (entry-level track)

Chapter 1Scarcity, Choice, and Opportunity Cost

Opening question

In 2023, about 4.74 million people in China sat the national postgraduate entrance exam (kaoyan). Suppose you are holding a job offer with an annual salary of 150,000 yuan, and a three-year master's program would cost about 100,000 yuan in tuition and housing. Is graduate school really that expensive? And why would economists say the 100,000 yuan on the bill is far from the main cost of this decision?

The core intuition

The core intuition

Economics studies choice under scarcity: time, money, and attention are never sufficient, so every choice means giving up others. The true cost of a decision is the value of the best option you forgo—that is its opportunity cost. It often appears on no bill at all: forgone wages, missed work experience, and lost room for trial and error are all implicit costs.

The second intuition is to think at the margin, not in totals: whether to do one more thing depends on the extra benefit and the extra cost of that step (marginal analysis), not on how much has already been sunk into it. Put these two intuitions together and you have economics' most fundamental way of thinking.

Key concepts

Scarcity
稀缺性
Resources are always limited relative to human wants. Scarcity is not a "shortage"—it is a permanent constraint, and it forces people to choose.
Opportunity Cost
机会成本
The value of the highest-valued option given up to obtain something. Note: count only the best forgone option, not the sum of all forgone options.
Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)
生产可能性边界
The set of all maximum output combinations of two goods, given resources and technology. Points beyond the frontier are unattainable; points inside it leave resources unused.
Marginal Analysis
边际分析
Comparing the extra benefit of "one more unit" with its extra cost. The rule of rational choice: keep going as long as marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost.
Positive vs. Normative Analysis
实证分析与规范分析
Positive statements answer "what is and what will happen" and can be tested against evidence; normative statements answer "what should be" and involve value judgments. Both matter, but never conflate the two.

The real world

The real world · The opportunity cost of the graduate-exam boom

For a graduate who gives up a job paying 150,000 yuan a year to attend a three-year master's program, the explicit costs (tuition and housing) come to about 100,000 yuan, while the forgone wages and promotion prospects exceed 450,000 yuan—the opportunity cost is several times the bill. This explains two patterns. First, in years when the job market is strong and wages rise quickly, growth in graduate-exam applications tends to slow (the opportunity cost has gone up). Second, many working professionals prefer part-time programs with weekend classes—cutting the "forgone income" to cut the opportunity cost. Whenever you evaluate a "should I or shouldn't I" decision, first ask: what is the best alternative you are giving up worth?

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

✗ Treating opportunity cost as "the sum of the values of all forgone options." The right approach: count only the single most valuable one—you cannot work at two companies at once.
✗ Counting only explicit costs while ignoring implicit ones: your own shop pays you "no wage," but your time still has a market price.
✗ Passing positive claims off as normative ones or vice versa: "a minimum wage reduces low-skill employment" is a testable positive proposition; "the government should raise the minimum wage" is a normative claim that requires a separate value argument.

Check yourself

Quiz 1.1Mr. Zhang earns 12,000 yuan a month and quits to attend a two-year master's program with total tuition of 40,000 yuan. The most complete measure of the economic cost of those two years (from the opportunity-cost perspective) is:
Explanation: Economic cost = explicit cost + implicit cost. Tuition is the explicit cost; the wages forgone while studying (along with promotion prospects) are the implicit cost—and precisely the largest part. A badly understates the cost; C confuses "cost" with "net benefit"—it is precisely because there are costs that a decision requires weighing costs against benefits. Living expenses usually do not count as an incremental cost, since you would eat whether or not you attended.
Quiz 1.2The production possibilities frontier is concave to the origin (bowed outward). This reflects:
Explanation: Resources are specialized—the first resources drawn into car production are those best at building cars; to keep expanding output, the economy must pull in resources that are ever better at growing grain, so ever more grain is forgone and the frontier bows outward. If A held, the frontier would be a straight line (constant opportunity cost); B is unrelated to the PPF's shape (technological regress would shift the entire frontier inward).

Chapter summary

  • Scarcity is where every economics question begins: wants are unlimited, resources are limited, so choices must be made.
  • Opportunity cost = the value of the best option forgone; beyond explicit costs, do not forget implicit ones.
  • The PPF shows the maximum output combinations under resource constraints; its outward bow captures increasing opportunity cost.
  • Rational decisions look at the margin: compare additional benefit with additional cost, not totals already spent.
  • Distinguishing positive statements (what is) from normative ones (what should be) is the first discipline of discussing economic questions.

Chapter 2Supply, Demand, and Market Equilibrium

Opening question

In 2019, African swine fever sharply cut China's hog capacity and pork prices briefly doubled in 2020; the high prices drove farmers to restock frantically, and in 2021 hog prices fell back below cost. No one commands the millions of hog farmers and over a billion consumers—so who "sets" the price? Why does it rise and fall with such precision, yet so violently?

The core intuition

The core intuition

No one sets the price: it is where the demand and supply curves "collide." The law of demand: the lower the price, the more people buy. The law of supply: the higher the price, the more firms sell. Above the equilibrium price there is a surplus and sellers undercut one another; below it there is a shortage and buyers outbid one another—bargaining pushes the price back to the intersection of the two curves: the market equilibrium.

The price's real job is to transmit information and coordinate action: a rise in pork prices simultaneously tells consumers "eat less," farmers "raise more," and capital "enter this industry"—with no central planning at all. To analyze any market event, answer just two questions: which curve did it shift? After the shift, where does the new intersection sit?

Key concepts

Law of Demand
需求定律
Holding everything else constant, a higher price means a lower quantity demanded, and a lower price a higher quantity demanded. Note the difference between a change in quantity demanded (a movement along the curve) and a change in demand (a shift of the entire curve).
Market Equilibrium
市场均衡
The price and quantity at which quantity supplied exactly equals quantity demanded. There is neither surplus nor shortage, and the price faces no further pressure to move.
Price Elasticity of Demand
需求价格弹性
The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price; it measures how sensitive buyers are to price. More substitutes, a longer time horizon, and a larger share of the budget all mean greater elasticity.
Price Control
价格管制
A government-set maximum price (price ceiling) or minimum price (price floor). Controls change how prices ration goods; they do not abolish scarcity itself.
Price elasticity of demand Ed = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price |Ed| greater than 1 means elastic (a price increase lowers total revenue); less than 1 means inelastic (a price increase raises total revenue)

The real world

The real world · The hog cycle: a roller coaster built on lagged supply

From restocking breeding sows to finished hogs leaving the farm takes roughly 10–12 months, so supply inevitably reacts to price with a lag: the swine-fever shock shifted the supply curve left and prices soared; farmers restocked on the strength of today's high prices, but by the time those hogs reached market, supply had surged, prices turned down, and the herd was culled again—and so the hog cycle of "dear meat hurts eaters, cheap meat hurts farmers" repeats. The great boom and bust around 2020 showed it once more: understanding which curve is moving, and when, is more useful than forecasting exact price numbers. Pork carries a sizable weight in China's CPI basket, which makes this chapter the first stepping-stone to the next track, macroeconomics.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

✗ "Rising prices mean demand has increased." Not necessarily—a supply contraction (swine fever, output cuts) also pushes prices up, but quantity moves in the opposite direction: higher demand raises both price and quantity, while lower supply raises price and cuts quantity. Check quantity to tell them apart.
✗ Confusing a "change in quantity demanded" with a "change in demand": the good's own price only moves the point along the curve; changes in income, tastes, or the prices of substitutes shift the entire curve.
✗ Believing a price cap makes the good affordable for everyone. A cap does not increase supply; it merely rations by queues, scalpers, lotteries, or quality cuts instead—scarcity has not disappeared, only the allocation rule has changed.

Check yourself

Quiz 2.1African swine fever kills large numbers of hogs and cuts capacity. In the short run, the pork market will see:
Explanation: Swine fever hits the selling side (capacity), so the supply curve shifts left and the new intersection sits up and to the left of the original equilibrium—higher price, smaller quantity. A misplaces the shock on demand (that would drop both price and quantity). C's error: consumers moving along the curve is the result, not the cause; until supply recovers, the price will not fall back on its own.
Quiz 2.2Demand for cigarettes is highly inelastic (addiction), while supply is relatively elastic. If the government raises the tobacco tax, who bears most of the burden?
Explanation: Tax incidence depends on elasticities, not on "who writes the check" or "who has more money": the side with less escape bears more. Smokers lack substitutes and find quitting hard (inelastic demand), so the great majority of the tax is passed through to consumers in higher prices; if sellers tried to hold prices down, the cigarette companies could simply cut output or switch products (elastic supply), so they bear less. This is also why tobacco taxes make reliable revenue yet are criticized as regressive (low-income smokers carry a relatively heavier burden).

Chapter summary

  • The equilibrium price forms at the intersection of demand and supply: quantities match exactly, and the price has no pressure to move.
  • The two-step method for analyzing shocks: decide which curve shifts → find the new intersection and read off the directions of price and quantity.
  • Higher demand: price and quantity both rise; higher supply: price falls, quantity rises—the two combinations point differently; don't mix them up.
  • Elasticity measures sensitivity and governs both how a price change affects total revenue and who bears a tax: the less elastic side bears more.
  • Price controls change the allocation rule without removing scarcity: a ceiling creates shortage, a floor creates surplus.

Chapter 3Consumer Theory: How to Make the Optimal Choice

Opening question

Reviewing your bills at month's end: 22 takeout meals, 4 movies, 8 workouts—why exactly that combination, and not "15 takeout meals and 8 movies"? If your employer suddenly added a 500-yuan monthly allowance, how would your bundle change? And why is "spend it all on one thing" almost nobody's answer?

The core intuition

The core intuition

A consumer's choice is pinched between two things: preferences (which bundles you want) and the budget (which bundles you can afford). Preferences are described by indifference curves: every bundle on the same curve is equally satisfying. The budget is described by the budget line: the bundles that spend all your money. The optimal choice is the highest indifference curve the budget line can touch—the tangency point.

The tangency has an elegant meaning: the rate at which you are privately willing to "trade takeout meals for a movie" (the marginal rate of substitution, MRS) exactly equals the rate the market asks you to pay (the ratio of the two prices). If what you'd willingly give exceeds what you must pay, trade a bit more; otherwise trade back—adjust until the two are equal and no room for improvement remains.

Key concepts

Utility
效用
An ordinal measure of satisfaction, used only to rank bundles; it cannot be compared in size from one person to another.
Indifference Curve
无差异曲线
The curve through all bundles that give the consumer the same utility. Usually convex to the origin: the less of a good you have, the more reluctantly you trade it away.
Budget Line
预算约束线
All the bundles that spend the entire budget. Its slope is set by the ratio of the two prices, its position by income.
Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS)
边际替代率
The absolute value of the indifference curve's slope: the amount of Y you are willing to give up for one more unit of X. The consumer's optimality condition: MRS = Px/Py.
Math box (optional)

Let utility be U = xy (taking logs of both goods gives the same result), with budget Pxx + Pyy = m. On the indifference curve xy = U, MRS = y/x. At the optimum y/x = Px/Py, and solving together with the budget equation gives x* = m/2Px and y* = m/2Py: double income and both consumptions double in the same proportion (the classic property of Cobb-Douglas preferences—verify it with the income slider in Figure 3-1 below). A price change works in two steps: the substitution effect swaps the bundle along the original indifference curve, while the income effect shifts it as real purchasing power changes—the two can point in opposite directions (as with inferior goods).

The real world

The real world · Consumer theory inside threshold discounts

Open any food-delivery or e-commerce app and "15 yuan off 60" or "4 yuan off 25" deals are everywhere. A threshold discount simply turns your budget line into a kinked one: below 60 yuan, each yuan buys one yuan's worth; cross the threshold and the last few yuan suddenly "buy more." The optimum that sat on the old budget line is abruptly pushed to a new bundle on the far side of the threshold—by reshaping your budget set, the platform nudges you to "add one more item to qualify." Once you see this, you can tell the two apart: what genuinely makes you better off is the discount itself, while the snacks bought just to clear the threshold mostly move your optimum without adding any utility.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

✗ Drawing indifference curves that cross. Two crossing indifference curves would give one bundle two different utility levels—a contradiction in preferences.
✗ Comparing utility across people: "he is 30% happier than you" is not a scientific statement; utility is a tool for ranking bundles within one person.
✗ Assuming the optimum must be at a tangency and must spend every yuan. With extreme preferences (say, eating almost exclusively at the canteen), the optimum can sit at an endpoint of the budget line (a corner solution); saving is a legitimate "third use" for the money.

Check yourself

Quiz 3.1A consumer's optimal choice between two goods (an interior solution) satisfies:
Explanation: The optimum lies at the tangency of the budget line and an indifference curve: your private exchange rate (MRS) exactly equals the market's exchange rate (the price ratio). If MRS exceeds the price ratio, you value X more than the market does and buying more X still yields a net gain—and vice versa—only when they are equal is there no room for improvement. B has no reason whatsoever to hold; C has both slopes nonzero.
Quiz 3.2Income falls while the prices of both goods stay the same. The budget line will:
Explanation: The budget line's slope is −Px/Py and depends only on prices; with prices fixed, the slope is fixed. Income only sets the size of the budget set: when income falls, both intercepts (m/Py and m/Px) shrink in the same proportion and the line shifts inward in parallel. A is the case of one good's price changing; B has the direction reversed.

Chapter summary

  • Preferences (indifference curves) plus constraints (the budget line) jointly determine choice; the optimum sits at the tangency.
  • Optimality condition: MRS = the price ratio—your private exchange rate equals the market's.
  • An income change shifts the budget line; a price change pivots it.
  • A price change decomposes into a substitution effect (a different bundle) and an income effect (different purchasing power).
  • Utility can only rank, never compare across people; the optimum can also be a corner solution.

Chapter 4Producer Theory and Costs

Opening question

Every year before "Double Eleven," China's giant shopping festival, parcel carriers would rather hire temporary workers at double wages and rent trucks on the open market at premium rates than "simply build a few more automated sorting hubs" while volume is enormous. If the peak season is that profitable, why not make a once-and-for-all investment? And conversely, once a sorting hub is built, why does it keep running even at a loss in the slow season?

The core intuition

The core intuition

The answer lies in the distinction between the short run and the long run. In the short run at least one input is fixed (plant, equipment): as you keep piling variable inputs (people, trucks) onto fixed inputs, each addition yields less and less extra output—diminishing marginal returns. That is why adding people and trucks in peak season gets ever more expensive, but with no time to build, firms swallow it. In the long run all inputs are variable and the firm can replicate its entire scale: if output doubles while cost less than doubles, that is economies of scale—precisely the reason automated sorting hubs exist, and precisely why they must wait for the long run.

The discipline of decision-making has only three rules: marginal cost decides how much to produce, average cost decides whether to stay in the business, and sunk costs never enter any decision.

Key concepts

Production Function
生产函数
The technical relationship between input combinations and maximum output: Q = f(L, K). Getting less output from the same inputs means a regression in technology or efficiency.
Diminishing Marginal Returns
边际报酬递减
When one variable input is increased while other inputs are held fixed, its marginal product eventually falls. A short-run concept whose premise is "other inputs unchanged."
Fixed vs. Variable Cost
固定成本与可变成本
Fixed costs do not vary with output (rent, depreciation) and must be paid in the short run whether or not you produce; variable costs rise and fall with output (materials, piece-rate wages).
Economies of Scale
规模经济
In the long run, output expands proportionally more than cost, so average cost falls with scale. Sources: spreading fixed inputs, specialization, bulk purchasing. Past some size, rising coordination costs produce diseconomies of scale.
Sunk Cost
沉没成本
A cost already paid and unrecoverable. It affects no marginal comparison, and rational decision-making should ignore it.

The real world

The real world · Short run vs. long run in parcel delivery

When warehouses overflow in peak season, a parcel carrier's plants and automated sorting lines are fixed inputs; in the short run the peak can only be absorbed by adding people, overtime, and rented vehicles—variable inputs piled onto fixed sorting capacity, with marginal returns diminishing fast. Hence the news stories about "double wages for temporary sorters." In the long run, the leading firms keep building large transfer hubs and automated sorting centers: handling cost per parcel falls with scale (economies of scale)—the micro foundation for a decade of falling parcel prices alongside continued profitability. The same chapter's logic explains the slow season too: once a sorting center is built, depreciation is a sunk/fixed cost, and as long as shipping rates cover the variable costs, running is more profitable than shutting down.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

✗ The sunk cost fallacy: "I paid 60 yuan for this ticket, so however bad the movie is, I'm staying." The ticket price cannot be recovered; the only relevant comparison is whether the next 90 minutes have a better use.
✗ Making production decisions with average cost. Whether to produce one more unit depends only on marginal cost versus marginal benefit; average cost is for judging long-run staying power and profit.
✗ Confusing "diminishing marginal returns" with "diseconomies of scale." The first is a short-run story about a single variable input; the second is a long-run story about all inputs expanding in proportion—they can occur together or separately.

Check yourself

Quiz 4.1"Diminishing marginal returns" refers to:
Explanation: Diminishing marginal returns means the extra output from each added unit of input (the marginal product) falls—total output is still growing, just ever more slowly. Picture a restaurant kitchen that keeps adding cooks: output keeps rising, but each extra cook adds fewer extra dishes. A describes the extreme case after marginal product turns negative; C describes diseconomies of scale, a long-run concept.
Quiz 4.2You paid 60 yuan for a movie ticket and confirm within half an hour that it is a dud. The economic advice is:
Explanation: The ticket price is a sunk cost: whether you stay or leave, it cannot be recovered, so it is irrelevant to "should I walk out now?" The rational move is to compare marginal quantities: the disutility of 90 more minutes of a bad movie versus the value of leaving and doing something else. B is the classic sunk cost fallacy; C overcorrects—one bad call does not change the expected value of movies; the right lesson is to read more reviews before buying the next ticket.

Chapter summary

  • Short run: at least one input fixed → diminishing marginal returns; long run: everything variable → economies or diseconomies of scale.
  • Marginal cost sets output, average cost sets whether to stay, sunk costs stay out of decisions.
  • MC crosses ATC at its minimum from below: the marginal value pulls the average toward itself.
  • Fixed costs are unavoidable in the short run but all costs are variable in the long run; shutting down requires only comparing price with average variable cost.
  • Economies of scale come from spreading and specialization and are a key determinant of industry structure (next chapter).

Chapter 5Market Structure: From Competition to Monopoly

Opening question

On the same flight, the passenger next to you may have paid three times your fare; for the same movie, students pay half price; for the same software, the enterprise edition costs ten times the personal one. Sellers clearly hold pricing power—so why not simply charge everyone the highest price? What is a firm that can price discriminate really playing at?

The core intuition

The core intuition

Picture the market as a spectrum. At one end is perfect competition—many firms, homogeneous products: any firm that raises its price loses all its customers, so price equals marginal cost and no one earns excess profit. At the other end is monopoly—a single seller facing the entire downward-sloping demand curve: it chooses output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, then pushes the price up onto the demand curve. Between the two poles, monopolistic competition (differentiated products, free entry) and oligopoly (a few firms watching one another) each have their own fascination; oligopoly's strategic interaction is developed in Track 04.

Monopoly's key cost: output is squeezed below the competitive level, and the transactions for which buyers are willing to pay more than the cost simply vanish—deadweight loss (DWL). Price discrimination, in essence, is the monopolist abandoning uniform pricing to charge according to willingness to pay, pocketing part of the DWL and the consumer surplus. To pull this off, it must be able to identify customers and to prevent low-price buyers from reselling.

Key concepts

Perfect Competition
完全竞争
A market structure with many buyers and sellers, homogeneous products, and free entry and exit. Each firm is a price taker facing a horizontal demand curve, and long-run economic profit is zero.
Monopoly & Natural Monopoly
垄断与自然垄断
A single seller with no close substitutes. When one firm can supply the whole market at lower cost than several sharing it (cost subadditivity), the monopoly is natural—think power grids or rail networks.
Price Discrimination
价格歧视
Charging different buyers different prices for the same good. Three degrees: by person (first-degree/perfect), by quantity (second-degree), and by group identity (third-degree, e.g. student tickets). Preconditions: identifiable, separable, no resale.
Oligopoly
寡头垄断
A few large firms dominate the market, and each must price with an eye on rivals' reactions—game theory's natural habitat; see Track 04.

The real world

The real world · Natural monopoly and third-degree price discrimination

Power grids, railways, and urban water supply are textbook natural monopolies: one network serving everyone costs far less than everyone building their own, and duplicating the network would be pure waste. Governments everywhere, China included, respond not by splitting them into competitors but with entry regulation plus price (rate-of-return) regulation—accepting the monopoly structure while restraining monopoly conduct. Meanwhile, airlines, cinemas, and software vendors are masters of third-degree price discrimination: student fares, early-bird tickets, senior discounts, "new-user deals"—all use observable identity tags to sort willingness to pay, pulling in customers who could not or would not buy at the list price (output moves closer to the competitive level) while preserving profits from the high-price groups. The recent controversy over "big-data price discrimination" against loyal customers is the backlash, on fairness and privacy grounds, once price discrimination reaches fully personalized pricing—China's E-Commerce Law and Personal Information Protection Law both respond to it.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

✗ "A monopolist can charge whatever it wants." Pricing is constrained by the demand curve: the higher the price, the lower the sales, and the monopolist must stop where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. In the inelastic range, raising price even increases revenue—so it would never stop there (see Quiz 5.2).
✗ "Monopoly = huge profits." Monopoly guarantees a higher profit ceiling, not positive profit—with high costs or weak demand a monopolist loses money too.
✗ "Price discrimination is always bad." Perfect price discrimination eliminates DWL and reaches competitive output—total social surplus is actually maximized (it just all goes to the seller). Judge discrimination on both efficiency and distribution, not on gut feeling.

Check yourself

Quiz 5.1In a perfectly competitive market, the demand curve facing a single firm is:
Explanation: Under perfect competition there are many firms with homogeneous products, and any single firm is negligible relative to the market: at the market price it sells as much as it wants (a price taker), and one cent higher sends every customer to identical substitutes. So the firm faces a horizontal demand curve, and P = MR. A is the demand curve facing the whole market or a monopolist; C has no economic meaning.
Quiz 5.2A monopolist finds itself at an output where demand is inelastic (|ε| < 1), so marginal revenue MR < 0. Its rational choice is to:
Explanation: Inelastic demand means a 1% price rise cuts quantity by less than 1%, so total revenue rises rather than falls; cutting output also saves marginal cost. Cutting back thus improves revenue and cost simultaneously, until MR again equals MC—which also proves a monopolist always prices on the elastic range of demand (with linear demand, above the midpoint). B points in exactly the wrong direction; C forgets that "monopoly" merely grants the power to choose—the pricing itself must still obey the logic of optimization.

Chapter summary

  • The market-structure spectrum: perfect competition → monopolistic competition → oligopoly → monopoly; the dividing lines are substitutes and barriers to entry.
  • Competitive firm: P = MC, zero long-run profit; monopolist: MR = MC sets output, price rides up the demand curve.
  • Monopoly output is lower and price higher than the competitive level; the difference forms the DWL triangle.
  • Lerner index (P − MC)/P = 1/|ε|: the less elastic demand, the more markup a monopolist can charge.
  • Price discrimination converts consumer surplus into producer surplus; under perfect discrimination output matches competition.

Chapter 6Market Failure and Government Intervention

Opening question

An old residential compound wants streetlights, and the property manager says "those who benefit should pay." The outcome is predictable: everyone hopes the lights come on, everyone waits for someone else to pay—in the end no streetlights are installed, and everyone keeps stumbling around in the dark. Why can't the market deliver something that benefits everyone? And by the same token, why can't it stop factories from polluting when that harms everyone?

The core intuition

The core intuition

The price system is efficient because it forces decision-makers to bear their own costs and enjoy their own benefits. Market failure is precisely where that chain breaks: an externality lets your actions affect others without ever entering your bill—the damage from pollution is borne by society, the benefit of streetlights enjoyed by the neighbors; public goods are non-excludable and non-rival, so there is no way to charge users, and free riding follows; asymmetric information lets bad products drive out good ones (the market for lemons); and market power (last chapter's monopoly) directly distorts prices.

The basic corrective idea is not to abolish the market but to fill in the missing price: tax external damage (a Pigouvian tax) or subsidize positive externalities, define property rights and let the parties bargain (the Coasean approach), or cap quantity and let tradable quotas find the price. But keep this in mind too: government also has limits of information and incentive—government failure and market failure must be weighed on the same scale.

Key concepts

Externality
外部性
The effect of an action on third parties that is never settled through a price. Harming others is a negative externality (pollution); benefiting them is a positive one (vaccination, streetlights).
Public Goods
公共品
Goods that are simultaneously non-excludable (non-payers cannot be kept from using them) and non-rival (one person's use does not reduce what is left for others), such as national defense, streetlights, and basic research.
Free Riding
搭便车
Enjoying the fruits of a collective effort without paying for it. Free riding is why public goods are underprovided by private actors—the main justification for government provision.
Asymmetric Information
信息不对称
One party to a transaction knows something the other does not. Hidden information before the deal leads to adverse selection (the market for lemons); hidden action after it leads to moral hazard. See Track 04.
Pigouvian Tax
庇古税
A tax on a negative externality set equal to the marginal external damage, raising private marginal cost to the social level and returning output to the social optimum.

The real world

The real world · Carbon markets and the market for lemons

In July 2021, China's national carbon emissions trading market officially launched, with the power-generation sector in the first batch: the government caps total emissions and firms buy and sell emission allowances—essentially the "tradable quota" version of the Pigouvian tax, letting the market rather than officials decide who can abate cheapest. As a contrast, the used-car market is the classic site of asymmetric information: buyers afraid of getting a "lemon" (a car with hidden defects) will pay only an average price; owners of good cars, offended by the low offers, exit, and the market's average quality slides further—adverse selection shrinks the market ever smaller. The platforms' countermeasures (official inspection reports, warranties, no-questions-asked returns) are precisely manufacturing information to repair the market, in the same lineage as signaling and screening, developed further in Track 04.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

✗ "The market failed, so the government should take over." Government faces its own incomplete information and distorted incentives (rent-seeking, bureaucratic inefficiency, regulatory capture). The right question is: market, government, or community and property-rights arrangements—which side governs at the lowest total cost?
✗ Treating the Pigouvian tax as a revenue tool. The optimal tax equals the marginal external damage; taxing further just to collect more pushes output below the social optimum and manufactures a new DWL.
✗ "Public goods = goods produced by government." The test is the good's attributes (non-excludable, non-rival), not the producer's identity—governments also produce private goods (state-owned firms' products), and markets and NGOs can supply public goods (open-source software, Wikipedia).

Check yourself

Quiz 6.1The streetlights in a compound never get installed. The economic root cause is:
Explanation: Once a streetlight is on, residents who did not pay cannot be excluded from the light (non-excludable), and one person's enjoyment does not dim anyone else's (non-rival). So each person's best strategy is "wait for someone else to pay"—free riding leaves voluntary provision far below what society needs, which is exactly the reason public goods are provided collectively out of tax revenue. A and C are beside the point: the technology is mature and the demand real; what jams is the payment mechanism.
Quiz 6.2A product's production causes marginal external damage of 4 yuan per unit. Setting the Pigouvian tax at 4 yuan per unit will:
Explanation: The Pigouvian tax's job is to "write" the external damage into firms' cost schedules: when the tax exactly equals the marginal damage, each firm's private optimization (finding the demand intersection on its own cost line) replicates society's optimization (finding it on MSC), and output reaches the social optimum. A wildly overstates it—output shrinks, it does not vanish; B has the goal backwards: the Pigouvian tax corrects an externality, and revenue is a by-product; a revenue-maximizing rate would push output far too low.

Chapter summary

  • The four roots of market failure: externalities, public goods, asymmetric information, market power.
  • Externalities drive private cost ≠ social cost: negative externalities mean overproduction, positive ones underprovision.
  • Public goods invite free riding through non-excludability plus non-rivalry, leaving private provision short.
  • The corrective idea is to fill in the missing price: Pigouvian taxes/subsidies, property rights and Coasean bargaining, tradable quotas.
  • Policy evaluation weighs market failure against government failure and picks the governance with the lowest total cost.

Chapter 7General Equilibrium and Welfare Economics

Opening question

World oil prices rise: first queues at the gas station, then electric-vehicle sales climb and lithium prices soar, then electricity-rate hearings convene—even the charging station downstairs starts charging more. The first six chapters looked at one market at a time, but in the real world all markets move together. Is there a toolkit that can see "all markets clearing at once"—and answer a bigger question: is such an economy a "good" one?

The core intuition

The core intuition

Partial equilibrium looks at one market at a time; general equilibrium makes all markets clear simultaneously, with prices and quantities determined jointly by the entire network of markets—an oil-price change travels along chains of substitutes, complements, and factor demand in every direction. To analyze pure exchange between two people and two goods, use the Edgeworth box: rotate B's indifference map 180 degrees and superimpose it on A's; every point in the box is an allocation.

The criterion for judging allocations is Pareto efficiency: no change can make at least one person better off without making anyone worse off. Efficiency in exchange requires everyone's MRS to be equal—the allocations satisfying this form the contract curve. The two Welfare Theorems then divide the labor: the First Theorem says a perfectly competitive equilibrium is necessarily Pareto efficient (markets are good at efficiency); the Second Theorem says any efficient allocation can in principle be reached by suitably redistributing initial endowments and then letting markets trade (for equity, redistribute first, then let the market do the work).

Key concepts

General Equilibrium
一般均衡
The state in which all markets clear at the same time. The "other things equal" of partial-equilibrium analysis becomes jointly determined in general equilibrium—a shock in one market propagates throughout.
Pareto Efficiency / Improvement
帕累托效率与帕累托改进
If at least one person can be made better off with no one worse off, that is a Pareto improvement; when no room for one remains, the allocation is Pareto efficient. The criterion avoids interpersonal comparisons—and is therefore silent about distribution.
Contract Curve
契约曲线
The locus of all allocations in the Edgeworth box where the two parties' MRS are equal—the Pareto-efficient set for exchange; the final landing point of bargaining (the competitive equilibrium) is one of its points.
Two Fundamental Theorems of Welfare Economics
福利经济学两大定理
First Theorem: a competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient. Second Theorem: any efficient allocation can be achieved by lump-sum redistribution of endowments followed by competitive exchange. Together: leave efficiency to the market, equity to redistribution.

The real world

The real world · Incremental reform: a Pareto improvement in practice

The household contract responsibility system of around 1978 is a textbook near-Pareto improvement: land remained collectively owned while farmers contracted to farm it—deliver enough to the state, set aside enough for the collective, keep the rest—and almost no one lost, while grain output surged within a few years. The dual-track pricing system that followed carried the same welfare-economics wisdom: prices and quotas on the planned track stayed put (the stock untouched, creating no losers), while the market track outside the plan was liberalized (the increment moved first, letting the improvers gain). The sequencing won broad support for reform—echoing the spirit of the Second Theorem: efficiency and distribution (who is harmed, who is compensated) are two things that can be designed separately; "growing the pie" and "dividing the pie well" call for different tools.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

✗ "Pareto efficient = fair." The contract curve contains both the equal-split point and corners where one person holds everything—they are all equally efficient. Efficiency asks only "is the pie as big as possible," never "how the pie is divided."
✗ Treating the Welfare Theorems as unconditional truths. Both rest on strong premises—perfect competition, complete markets, no externalities—and Chapters 5 and 6's monopoly and externalities are exactly where the premises fail, and competitive equilibrium no longer guarantees efficiency.
✗ "If markets are efficient, no redistribution is needed." The First Theorem only guarantees landing somewhere on the contract curve; where you land depends on initial endowments. The legitimate tool for moving that point is lump-sum redistribution, not price distortion.

Check yourself

Quiz 7.1A policy change is called a "Pareto improvement" if and only if:
Explanation: A Pareto improvement is by definition a change that makes someone better off and no one worse off; it deliberately avoids interpersonal utility comparisons, which makes it the weakest and least contentious standard. B and C can both accompany losses for some—GDP rises while workers in one industry lose their jobs, or distribution becomes more equal while the rich are made worse off—so neither is sufficient nor necessary; judging such changes requires additional value judgments (normative analysis).
Quiz 7.2The most important policy implication of the Second Welfare Theorem is:
Explanation: The Second Theorem splits "efficiency" and "distribution" into two separate tasks: the market is the machine that produces efficiency, and any efficient allocation you want (including fairer ones) can be reached by adjusting the starting point (lump-sum redistribution) and then handing over to the market. A is wrong that they "cannot both be had"; C mistakes efficiency for fairness—where the competitive equilibrium lands is determined by initial endowments and can be deeply unequal. Of course, in practice lump-sum redistribution (like taxing endowments) runs into incentive and enforcement problems—the province of optimal tax theory (see Track 08).

Chapter summary

  • General equilibrium has all markets clearing at once; shocks propagate throughout via substitution and factor chains.
  • The Edgeworth box analyzes exchange: crossing indifference curves mean mutually beneficial trades remain; tangency means Pareto efficiency.
  • The contract curve = all allocations where MRS are equal; the competitive equilibrium lies on it.
  • First Theorem: competitive equilibrium is efficient; Second Theorem: efficient plus equitable can be reached via redistribution of endowments.
  • Efficiency is not equity; the theorems' premises (perfect competition, no externalities) are exactly the focus of policy debate.
