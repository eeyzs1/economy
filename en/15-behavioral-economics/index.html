Behavioral Economics

行为经济学

Standard economics says people are cool-headed calculators; psychology experiments keep discovering instead that people are predictably "irrational" — they fear losses more than they love gains, today's self bullies tomorrow's self, and they tear up free money to make a point. Behavioral economics welcomes these systematic quirks of human nature back into the model, and uses them to explain market anomalies and design better policy.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • Use the System 1 / System 2 framework to judge when a class of decisions is effortless and when it is error-prone
  • Identify the three heuristics — anchoring, availability, and representativeness — and the systematic biases they produce
  • Use prospect theory (reference points, loss aversion, probability weighting) to explain the disposition effect and the person who buys both insurance and lottery tickets
  • Explain inconsistent discounting and preference reversals, and design commitment devices accordingly
  • Evaluate models of social preferences against ultimatum-game and dictator-game evidence, and discuss the design and ethical limits of nudges
Prerequisites: 01 Microeconomics (utility & expected utility) 04 Game Theory (ultimatum game, optional)

Chapter 1Bounded Rationality and Heuristics

Opening question: A bat and a ball together cost 1.10 yuan; the bat costs 1 yuan more than the ball. How much does the ball cost? More than half of students at top universities blurt out "0.10 yuan" — wrong; it is 0.05 yuan. Why do smart people stumble on grade-school arithmetic?

The core intuition

The brain runs in two modes. System 1 is fast, automatic, and effortless — recognizing faces, reading tone of voice, completing "2+2". System 2 is slow, deliberate, and effortful — multiplying 17×24 in your head, checking contract clauses. System 2 is a lazy gatekeeper that waves through System 1's first answer most of the time. The trouble is that System 1's answer looks right, so we stop checking. Nobel laureate Herbert Simon proposed bounded rationality: cognitive resources and time are scarce, so people aim not for "the best" but for "good enough" (to satisfice). The brain therefore takes shortcuts — heuristics. Shortcuts are usually efficient, but in particular environments they produce systematic, predictable biases — and that is exactly where behavioral economics begins.

A short history of the field

Simon won the Nobel Prize in 1978 for bounded rationality; Kahneman (with the late Tversky) won in 2002 for bringing psychological experiments into judgment and decision-making under uncertainty; Thaler won in 2017 for pushing behavioral ideas into saving, markets, and policy. It took roughly forty years for behavioral economics to go from heresy to a standard tool in the mainstream toolbox.

Key concepts

Bounded Rationality
有限理性
With limited cognitive capacity, time, and information, people stop searching once an option is "good enough" rather than exhausting the optimum. The master assumption of behavioral economics.
System 1 / System 2
系统 1 / 系统 2
Kahneman's dual-process metaphor: System 1 is intuitive, fast, and automatic; System 2 is reflective, slow, and effortful. They are modes of processing, not two literal regions of the brain.
Anchoring
锚定效应
An irrelevant initial number pulls subsequent numerical estimates toward itself. Even when the number comes from a roulette wheel, estimates of the share of African countries in the UN are dragged along by it.
Availability Heuristic
可得性启发式
The more easily an example comes to mind, the more common it is judged to be. After a plane crash makes headlines, people overestimate the risk of flying — even though driving is statistically more dangerous.
Representativeness Heuristic
代表性启发式
Judging probability by how much something resembles a typical case, ignoring base rates and sample size. The "Linda problem" and the gambler's fallacy (betting on black after five reds) both spring from it.

Each of the three classic heuristics has a signature crash scene:

  • Anchoring: first ask "Did Gandhi die before or after age 114?" then ask "How old do you think he was when he died?" — estimates come out more than a decade higher than without the leading question. The commercial "strikethrough original price" is someone handing you an anchor.
  • Availability: after a crash makes the news, flight bookings fall and sales of air-travel accident insurance rise; kitchen falls, which are genuinely frequent, never make headlines, and so get underestimated.
  • Representativeness: describe "Linda: 31, philosophy graduate, concerned with discrimination and social justice," then ask which is more likely — (A) Linda is a bank teller, or (B) Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement. Most people pick B, but "teller and feminist" is a subset of "teller" and cannot be more probable. This conjunction fallacy is representativeness overriding logic.
The real world · Strikethrough prices and purchase-limit anchors

In an e-commerce mega-sale's "was 999 yuan, now 399 yuan," the 999 usually works not as information but as an anchor: it lifts your estimate of the product's worth and makes 399 look like a bargain — which is why regulators require that the "original price" have actually changed hands. Another classic field study: a supermarket posted "Limit 12 cans per person" on soup, and average purchases per customer rose significantly versus no limit — the anchor "12" quietly answered "how many cans should I buy," a question you had never thought to ask.

Common pitfalls

"Heuristics are fallacies and should be eliminated." Quite the opposite: most of the time heuristics are efficient and robust. Gigerenzer's work on ecological rationality shows that simple rules matched to their environment (such as the equal-weighted 1/N portfolio) can rival complex models. The point of behavioral economics is not to sneer at shortcuts but to identify the environments in which they systematically fail — and then improve those environments. A second pitfall: treating System 1 and System 2 as two organs in the brain — they are only a convenient metaphor.

Quiz 1.1Which of the following tasks relies mainly on System 2 (slow thinking)?
Explanation: Face recognition, reading emotion, and answering "2+2" are System 1's automatic operations — fast and effortless. Multi-digit multiplication has no automatic answer; it must occupy working memory step by step, and that is precisely System 2's signature: slow, effortful, interruptible. Rule of thumb: anything that makes you "pause and think" is probably System 2 at work.
Quiz 1.2After a local plane crash is covered in the news for a month straight, passenger numbers fall noticeably, even though driving is statistically more dangerous. Which heuristic most likely drives the overestimation of crash risk?
Explanation: The more easily a scene can be "retrieved" from memory (the higher its availability), the more common the event is estimated to be — and replaying crash footage on the news artificially inflates availability. Representativeness judges by resemblance to a typical case (the Linda problem); anchoring needs an initial number pulling an estimate. Here there is neither a typicality comparison nor an anchor — only the vividness of memory.

Chapter summary

  • Bounded rationality: cognitive resources are scarce, so people aim for "good enough," not the optimum.
  • System 1 is fast and automatic; System 2 is slow and effortful — and a lazy System 2 often waves through System 1's first response.
  • The three shortcuts — anchoring, availability, representativeness — are usually efficient but produce systematic, predictable biases in particular environments.
  • The goal of behavioral economics is to identify the failing environments and improve them, not to mock the shortcuts.

Chapter 2Prospect Theory: Reference Points and Loss Aversion

Opening question: Why do investors rush to sell their winning stocks while clinging to their losers forever? Taking the loss — "cutting the flesh," as Chinese traders put it — is only a click away.

The core intuition

Standard theory says utility depends on final wealth; Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 prospect theory says people feel gains and losses relative to a reference point. The reference point is usually the status quo (the purchase price, last year's bonus, the neighbor's salary), and three things follow. First, loss aversion — the pain of losing 100 yuan is roughly equal to the pleasure of gaining more than 200 yuan. Second, diminishing sensitivity — the jump from 0 to 100 yuan feels better than the jump from 1,000 to 1,100 yuan, though the difference is identical. Third, probability weighting — tiny probabilities are overweighted (a one-in-ten-thousand chance is felt as at least one in a thousand), and near-certain options get an extra boost (the certainty effect). Together, these three are the behavioral map of how people decide under risk.

Key concepts

Prospect Theory
前景理论
Kahneman & Tversky (1979): utility is defined over gains and losses relative to a reference point; the loss side is steeper (loss aversion), sensitivity diminishes on both sides, and probabilities are weighted nonlinearly.
Reference Point
参照点
The dividing line between gains and losses, usually the status quo or an expectation (the purchase price, last year's bonus). Move the reference point and the same money flips from "gain" to "loss" — preferences flip with it.
Loss Aversion
损失厌恶
Losses hurt more than equal-sized gains please; the ratio λ is usually estimated around 2 (a typical value is 2.25).
Probability Weighting Function
概率权重函数
Subjective probability π(p) ≠ p: small probabilities are overweighted, moderate-to-large ones underweighted. Explains the certainty effect and the "fourfold pattern."
Disposition Effect
处置效应
Investors tend to sell winning positions too early and hold losing ones too long — "sell winners, hold losers," the standard fingerprint of loss aversion in capital markets.
v(x) = xα (x ≥ 0); v(x) = −λ·(−x)β (x < 0) Math box: the value function. Empirical estimates α, β ≈ 0.88 (diminishing sensitivity); loss-aversion coefficient λ ≈ 2.25 (Kahneman & Tversky, 1992)

The fourfold pattern (gains/losses × high/low probability) delivers a beautiful set of predictions. Facing a high-probability gain (900 for sure vs. a 91% chance of 1,000), people are risk-averse — take the money and run. Facing a high-probability loss (lose 900 for sure vs. a 90% chance of losing 1,000), people are risk-seeking — "one more bet might win it back." Facing a low-probability jackpot they buy lottery tickets (risk-seeking); facing a low-probability catastrophe they buy insurance (risk-averse). The same person buying both insurance and lottery tickets is a contradiction in expected-utility theory — and two sides of the same coin in prospect theory.

The real world · The disposition effect hurts — and it costs

Using 10,000 brokerage accounts, Odean (1998) found that retail investors are significantly more prone to sell winning stocks than losing ones — "sell winners, hold losers" follows directly from a reference point (the purchase price) plus loss aversion. The stinger: the winners that were sold went on, on average, to outperform the losers that were kept. Studies of Chinese A-share accounts find the same pattern: high-turnover retail investors are especially slow to cut their losses, and some even buy more to average down as the losses deepen. A behavioral bias is not just psychological discomfort — it has a price in real money.

Common pitfalls

"Loss aversion is just risk aversion." No. Risk aversion in standard theory is concavity of the utility function, a statement about final wealth; loss aversion is asymmetry of gains and losses around a reference point, and it predicts exactly what a concave utility function cannot: locking gains in quickly (risk aversion) while holding losers and even doubling down (risk-seeking) — an asymmetry the concave form cannot deliver. Another mistake: assuming the reference point is fixed. Last year's three-month bonus makes this year's two-month bonus feel like a loss; money won at the casino is booked into a different mental account, so losing it back barely hurts (the house-money effect). Reference points are moved by experience and by wording.

Quiz 2.1Mr. Wang refuses to sell a stock that has fallen 20%, yet quickly sells one that has risen 10%. Which two elements of prospect theory are the key to explaining this "disposition effect"?
Explanation: Utility is defined over gains and losses relative to the purchase price (the reference point). Selling a loser cashes in the psychological loss, instantly amplifying the pain (λ≈2), so he delays; selling a winner locks in "happiness he can see." Standard risk aversion (B) cannot explain the asymmetry of the same person selling winners in a hurry while holding losers forever — a concave utility function would only say he dislikes volatility on both sides.
Quiz 2.2The same person buys insurance and lottery tickets. The most fitting explanation is:
Explanation: Prospect theory's probability weighting function gives tiny probabilities more than proportional weight (π(p)>p): a small-probability, large loss like a crash is magnified → willing to buy insurance even at a high price (risk-averse on the loss side); a small-probability, large gain like a jackpot is magnified just the same → also willing to buy lottery tickets (risk-seeking on the gain side). This is the diagonal combination of the "fourfold pattern," a pattern that a single unified risk-aversion parameter in expected-utility theory cannot produce.

Chapter summary

  • Utility depends on gains and losses relative to a reference point, not on final wealth; the reference point can be moved by the status quo, expectations, and wording.
  • Loss aversion λ≈2.25: losses hurt more than equal-sized gains; risk-seeking in the loss domain, risk aversion in the gain domain.
  • Diminishing sensitivity (α<1) flattens the curve on both sides.
  • Probability weighting overweights small probabilities and adds a premium to certainty → buying insurance and lottery tickets at once, the fourfold pattern.
  • The disposition effect (sell winners, hold losers) is one of prospect theory's most robust fingerprints in capital markets.

Chapter 3Intertemporal Choice and Self-Control

Opening question: You have been promising yourself "early night tonight" for three weeks; the annual gym membership is a year old and you have been four times. Why do the you who plans and the you who execute seem to be two different people?

The core intuition

The standard model uses exponential discounting: each period, the future shrinks by the same proportion δ. It has an elegant property — time consistency: a plan made today for next month is still executed next month. Real people look more like hyperbolic discounters: extremely sensitive to the gap between "now and soon," yet numb to the gap between "far and farther" — colloquially, the temptation right in front of you gets an extra boost. Economists usually capture this with quasi-hyperbolic discounting (the β-δ model): everything outside "now" first takes a β discount (β<1) — and that is present bias. Preference reversal (time inconsistency) then appears: asked today, you choose "salad next month"; when next month actually arrives at your lips, you choose "fried chicken today." The planning self and the executing self have conflicting interests, and the executing self always wins.

Key concepts

Exponential Discounting
指数贴现
The future shrinks by a fixed proportion δ each period; the curve declines at a constant rate, implying time consistency: plans and execution never conflict.
Quasi-hyperbolic Discounting (β-δ)
准双曲贴现
All future periods first take a β discount and then decline by δ. The smaller β is, the greater the privilege of "now" over the future.
Present Bias
现时偏好
Overweighting immediate rewards and costs: pleasant little tasks get done today, hard ones are pushed onto tomorrow's self.
Commitment Device
承诺机制
Voluntarily restricting your future choice set — deciding on behalf of "your future self" in advance: automatic transfers, deadlines, refundable-but-unusable deposits.
U = u(c₀) + β · Σt≥1 δt u(ct), β < 1 Math box: quasi-hyperbolic discounting. β penalizes all of "the future's" consumption at once (creating the now-versus-later asymmetry); δ is the per-period discount factor; at β=1 the model reverts to exponential discounting
The real world · Gym memberships and outsourced self-control

Studying bills from a US gym chain, DellaVigna and Malmendier found that members on monthly or annual plans averaged fewer than 5 visits a month, making their cost per visit far higher than pay-per-visit — "I'll come every day from now on," promised at the moment of signing up, is textbook over-optimism plus present bias (pay the cost now, push the workouts onto the future). The antidote is a commitment device: retirement contributions auto-transferred the day after payday, Christmas savings clubs where you pay first and consume later, deposit apps that keep you offline while you write (StickK: miss the goal and your deposit goes to the organization you hate most). Behavioral policy uses the same principle: scheduling the annual checkup by default in birthday week works far better than any "remember to get checked" slogan.

Common pitfalls

"Hyperbolic discounting just means impatience." No. A very low δ is consistent impatience: today's plan is carried out tomorrow exactly as made. The behavioral signature is the inconsistency: the ranking of small and large rewards flips as the moment approaches. The policy implications are completely different — for consistent impatience, raise the rate of return or lower the cost of waiting; for present bias, use commitment devices to rearrange the timing structure of choice. A second mistake is treating present bias as a character flaw of weak will: it is a measurable, modelable preference parameter — firms use it to design "buy now, pay later," and smart people should use it to design their own environments.

Quiz 3.1"I'll definitely sleep early tonight" — said for three weeks running. In terms of quasi-hyperbolic (β-δ) discounting, the most accurate diagnosis is:
Explanation: When planning, both "scrolling tonight" and "sleeping early tonight" lie in the future, both take the β discount, and health's comparative advantage wins; when "tonight" actually arrives, scrolling becomes "now," escapes the β discount, and the immediate temptation is instantly magnified — the choice flips. This is precisely the preference reversal of Figure 3-1: "the small reward today, the large reward a month from today." C is wrong: a low δ is only consistent impatience, and it never changes its mind as the moment approaches.
Quiz 3.2Scheduling the automatic investment deduction for the day after payday works far better than counting on "manually saving" at month's end, mainly because it:
Explanation: The present-biased person's predicament is that the "self of the moment" always wins. Automatic transfer hands the decision back to the self that set up the commitment: the money is gone before it ever reaches the spendable account, so the self of the moment never gets a vote — this is the standard posture of a commitment device: constrain tomorrow's choice set rather than persuade tomorrow's self. Interest rates (A) are usually no different, and trust (C) is hardly the point.

Chapter summary

  • Exponential discounting implies time consistency: plans and execution never conflict; a low δ is only consistent impatience.
  • Quasi-hyperbolic discounting (β-δ) captures present bias: the whole future takes the β discount, and "now" holds a privilege.
  • Present bias produces preference reversal: the large reward is chosen today, the small temptation when the moment arrives.
  • The antidote is a commitment device: automatic transfers, deposits, deadlines — deciding in advance for your future self.

Chapter 4Social Preferences and Fairness Games

Opening question: Splitting 100 yuan with a stranger, one shot only: you offer 1 yuan and keep 99 for yourself, and the stranger will most likely refuse — walking away with nothing just to leave you empty-handed too. In standard theory this is an "irrational" rejection; yet it happens reliably in every laboratory in the world. Who exactly is being irrational?

The core intuition

Perhaps people are not wrong — the model is too narrow. Write fairness, reciprocity, and altruism into the utility function and the rejection makes immediate sense: punishing a greedy proposer delivers a psychological payoff in itself. Ultimatum game: a proposer splits 100 yuan, and the responder can accept or reject (rejection leaves both with 0). The subgame-perfect equilibrium is "offer the smallest unit; the responder accepts." In experiments the average offer is 30–40%, and offers below 20% are rejected about half the time. The dictator game deletes the rejection stage, yet allocators still give away two to three tenths on average — so it is not only fear of punishment; there is also pure altruism and fairness preference. The trust game and the public-goods game go further: people will pay out of their own pockets to punish free riders (altruistic punishment), and precisely this punishment sustains cooperation. The sense of fairness is not noise; it is the lubricant that keeps society running.

Key concepts

Social Preferences
社会偏好
Utility depends not only on one's own payoff but also on others' payoffs and intentions: fairness, reciprocity, altruism, inequality aversion.
Ultimatum Game
最后通牒博弈
A proposer offers a split; the responder accepts and the split is implemented, or rejects and both get zero. Standard prediction s≈0; in experiments the average offer is 30–40%, and low offers are often rejected.
Dictator Game
独裁者博弈
The rejection stage is removed and one side simply allocates. Nonzero giving points to altruism or fairness; giving falls under double-blind conditions, showing that "being watched" also matters.
Negative Reciprocity
负互惠
Retaliating against those who treat you badly, even at a cost to yourself. Rejecting a low offer is the classic case; altruistic punishment is its public-goods version.
Inequality Aversion
不平等厌恶
The Fehr–Schmidt model: utility falls whether you are behind or ahead of others — a way to parameterize "fairness enters the utility function."

The real world · Fairness grows with the mode of production

Henrich and colleagues repeated the ultimatum game in 15 small-scale societies (hunters, herders, whalers, slash-and-burn farmers): the deeper a society's market integration and the more important cooperative production, the more generous the offers (the Indonesian whaling village of Lamalera averaged about 58% — collective whaling cannot function without fair sharing), while the nearly self-sufficient Machiguenga farmers of Peru offered only about 26%, with low rejection rates as well. Fairness norms are not a universal constant but an adaptation to the local way of making a living — echoing Chapter 1's ecological-rationality theme of heuristics matching their environment. In high-stakes experiments (pots of several hundred dollars) low offers are still rejected, so this is not "the money is too small to matter."

Common pitfalls

"People who reject low offers are irrational." That holds only under the narrow definition "maximize monetary payoff in this round." In repeated, reputation-bearing social settings, rejecting a greedy offer is an optimal investment in future cooperation: tolerate today's 1-yuan humiliation and you will face more 1-yuan offers tomorrow. Experimental economics adds a second caution: dictator giving drops markedly under double-blind conditions (where not even the experimenter knows who gave what) — part of that "altruism" is really social-image management. Before concluding, separate "preference" from "being observed."

Quiz 4.1In an ultimatum game, the responder rejects a 15% split. Which economic explanation best holds up?
Explanation: With fairness written into the utility function, rejection is rational utility maximization — spending 15 yuan to buy the psychological payoff of "punishing the greedy" plus a reputational signal. In high-stakes games (pots of several hundred dollars) low shares are still rejected, ruling out C; A promotes the model assumption "maximize monetary payoff" into the definition of rationality — when the model fails, fix the model rather than declare the human being wrong.
Quiz 4.2In a dictator game, allocators still give away about 20% on average, although the other side has no power whatsoever to punish. This shows that:
Explanation: The dictator game removes the rejection stage, so every cent given away is real money out of the giver's pocket (whether from altruism, fairness, or image concerns). The bare fact that giving is nonzero is already enough to reject the narrow "everyone maximizes only their own money" model; at the same time, giving falls in double-blind experiments, so "being observed" contributes part of the story — the model should accommodate both forces rather than choose between them.

Chapter summary

  • Ultimatum: average offers 30–40%, low offers rejected about half the time; dictators still give 20–30%.
  • Fairness, reciprocity, altruism, and inequality aversion can enter the utility function — the rejection is therefore rational behavior.
  • Altruistic punishment can sustain cooperation in public goods — social preferences are the lubricant that keeps society running.
  • The strength of fairness norms varies with livelihood and culture (cross-cultural experiments); when interpreting, distinguish preference from image management.

Chapter 5Nudges and Choice Architecture

Opening question: Change nothing but a form's default checkbox and organ-donation consent can jump from 12% to over 99% — not a cent of subsidy added, not a single option removed. Since when does a printing detail carry that much power?

The core intuition

Since people are swayed by inertia, procrastination, and defaults, how the options are arranged is itself an influence on outcomes — Thaler and Sunstein call this choice architecture. A nudge, by definition, bans no option and does not meaningfully change economic incentives; it only rearranges the choice environment, gently pushing people's behavior in the direction they themselves say they want, and they can always change their mind ("libertarian paternalism"). The most powerful weapon is the default option: most people "want to join the pension plan" and "want to donate organs" but are forever one form short; set the default to "enrolled," and the people who do nothing move from outside to inside. The framing effect is the other face: the same fact stated as a "90% survival rate" versus a "10% mortality rate" leads patients to different choices. Wording, like defaults, is choice architecture.

Key concepts

Nudge
助推
An intervention that bans no option and does not meaningfully change incentives, only rearranging the choice environment to improve behavioral outcomes; full freedom of choice is preserved.
Choice Architecture
选择架构
How options are presented: defaults, ordering, grouping, wording, feedback. Every "interface" nudges; the only question is in which direction.
Default Option / Status Quo Bias
默认选项 / 现状偏差
People tend to stick with the status quo and avoid the cost of acting; the default thus becomes "the choice of those who do not choose" — the highest-leverage detail in policy.
Framing Effect
框架效应
Different wording of the same information (survival rate vs. mortality rate, gains vs. losses) systematically changes choices — a direct extension of how the reference point is set.
The real world · Two defaults that changed a nation's course

Organ donation: Johnson and Goldstein's (2003) classic comparison — Austria, with consent as the default, has a 99.98% consent rate; next-door Germany, with non-consent as the default, only 12%, though the two publics express nearly identical attitudes in surveys. UK pension "automatic enrollment" (from 2012): no added subsidy, only a changed default, and private-sector employees' saving participation rose from about 55% to 88%. Alongside it sits Thaler and Benartzi's Save More Tomorrow: employees commit to saving a little more at their next raise, postponing the sacrifice into the future and tying it to rising income — participation and retention both climb sharply. Present bias from Chapter 3, turned directly into a policy component.

Common pitfalls

"With nudges, who needs price incentives?" A default changes the cost of action, not true preferences or budget constraints. A smoker facing "cigarettes no longer at the checkout" will simply go find the shelf — the tobacco tax is still the most powerful tool. Conversely, if a subsidy points the wrong way, the smoothest default merely helps people do the wrong thing faster. The nudge is a new wrench in the toolbox, not a replacement for the hammer. A second mistake is "defaults equal deception" — a good nudge is transparent, reversible, and pushes toward the chooser's own stated goals; whether a line has been crossed is exactly the subject of the next chapter.

Quiz 5.1The UK pension "automatic enrollment" reform added not a cent of subsidy, yet participation rose from about 55% to 88%. The most fitting mechanism is:
Explanation: Most people "wanted to join but never got around to it"; the cost of acting (finding the form, making a decision) is indefinitely postponed by present bias. Once enrollment is the default, the path of least effort lands exactly in the direction they themselves say they want — that is a nudge: no options changed, no incentives added, only a rearranged choice architecture. A overstates: anyone who wants out can opt out at any time, and a few did; C contradicts the facts — automatic enrollment is precisely a "no subsidy, no penalty" intervention.
Quiz 5.2The same operation described as "90% survival rate at one month" or "10% mortality rate at one month" leads patients to different choices. This is:
Explanation: The two messages are perfectly equivalent; the only difference is whether the reference is set to "survival" (gain frame) or "death" (loss frame). Under the loss frame patients are markedly more willing to refuse the surgery and gamble — the framing effect, a direct descendant of Chapter 2's reference point. Anchoring requires an initial number pulling an estimate; the endowment effect requires valuation to rise once you own something. Here there is neither anchor nor ownership.

Chapter summary

  • Choice architecture: defaults, ordering, wording, and feedback all influence choice; "not designing" is itself a design.
  • A nudge bans no option, barely changes incentives, and can be undone — libertarian paternalism.
  • The default has the greatest leverage: organ donation 12% vs. 99.98%, UK pensions 55% → 88%.
  • Framing effect: gain-versus-loss wording of the same information changes choices; Save More Tomorrow turns present bias into a policy component.
  • Nudges do not replace price incentives or information; they only lower the cost of acting on the right choice.

Chapter 6The Limits and Ethics of Behavioral Economics

Opening question: If governments and firms knew for certain that a single default checkbox can steer your pension, your organs, and your data consent — then who should hold the power to set the default?

The core intuition

Behavioral economics has three boundaries. The ethical boundary: the line between a nudge and manipulation lies in transparency, reversibility, and "in whose interest" — pushing toward the chooser's own stated goals is a nudge; harvesting people by exploiting their weaknesses is manipulation ("sludge": burying entitlements under red tape so people give them up — a nudge in reverse). Who nudges the nudgers is a question public policy cannot dodge. The empirical boundary: around 2011 psychology erupted into the replication crisis — in the 2015 large-scale replication project, only about a third of one hundred studies replicated successfully, and several famous effects (including some social-priming effects) shrank dramatically. Behavioral economics must stand on preregistration, large samples, and direct replication. The boundary of applicability: markets with experienced actors, immediate feedback, large stakes, and arbitrage "train" behavior toward the standard model — List's studies of sports-card markets found the endowment effect weakening sharply with trading experience. So the right question is not "which model is true" but "which model is the better first approximation in this environment".

Key concepts

Libertarian Paternalism
自由家长主义
Thaler and Sunstein's rule for nudges: preserve complete freedom of choice (libertarian) while steering defaults toward the chooser's long-run interest (paternalism). Critics ask: who gets to define "your interest"?
Replication Crisis
复制性危机
Many published psychology and behavioral findings fail to replicate: small samples, publication bias, flexible analysis (p-hacking). The fix: preregistration, large samples, direct replication, reporting effect sizes.
Ecological Rationality
生态理性
Gigerenzer: a rule's quality depends on its match with the environment. Simple heuristics are robust and effective in matching environments — market experience can "correct" many biases.
The real world · The Behavioural Insights Team: nudging and experimenting

In 2010 the UK created the "Behavioural Insights Team" (BIT, nicknamed the "Nudge Unit"). Its famous experiment: adding one line to overdue-tax reminder letters — "9 out of 10 taxpayers in your area pay on time" (a social norm) — raised on-time payment by about 5 percentage points according to its reports, outperforming threatening language at almost zero cost. Equally important is the methodology: BIT made randomized controlled trials and preregistration its standard workflow, so nudge policies are themselves tested first. The World Bank's World Development Report 2015: Mind, Society, and Behavior carried the approach worldwide; China's recent default and process design for "personal pension" accounts and organ-donation registration belongs to the same lineage of debate.

Common pitfalls

"Behavioral economics has replaced neoclassical economics." The mainstream is fusion, not replacement: loss aversion, present bias, and social preferences are embedded as parameters in standard models (behavioral IO, behavioral labor, behavioral public finance) to answer "when, and by how much, do people deviate?" Individual-level biases are often weakened by competition and arbitrage as markets aggregate — the mispricing a bias creates is somebody else's profit. Nor can a few failed replications bankrupt an entire field: loss aversion, default effects, and present bias have oceans of replicated evidence. Sort by environment, look at effect sizes — that is the grown-up attitude.

Quiz 6.1Regarding the "replication crisis" in behavioral science, which statement is most apt?
Explanation: The Open Science Collaboration (2015) replicated 100 psychology studies; only about a third yielded statistically significant successful replications, and several famous effects shrank or vanished. The right lesson is methodological: preregistration, large samples, direct replication, reporting effect sizes. But "all false" (B) and "not our problem" (C) are both wrong — core findings such as loss aversion and default effects have extensive evidence across labs and settings, and they have indeed been directly replicated again and again.
Quiz 6.2In which of these settings is the standard "rational agent" model likely still the better predictive tool?
Explanation: Experience + immediate feedback + large stakes + competitive arbitrage "trains" behavior toward the optimum: List's sports-card market studies show the endowment effect weakening sharply with trading experience, and professional market makers' quoting biases are far smaller than novices'. Conversely, one-shot, emotional, unfamiliar, feedback-free choices (A, C) are the home turf of behavioral models. The mnemonic: the novice's first time is behavioral; the veteran's routine is rational.

Chapter summary

  • The ethical boundary: transparent, reversible, in the chooser's interest; "sludge" that weaponizes friction in reverse is manipulation.
  • The empirical boundary: the replication crisis → preregistration, large samples, direct replication, effect sizes.
  • The boundary of applicability: in environments with experience + feedback + stakes + arbitrage, the standard model is often more accurate.
  • The mainstream is fusion: behavioral ingredients are parameterized into standard models, and the first approximation is chosen by environment.
