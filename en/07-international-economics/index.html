Track 07 · Sectors & international

International Economics

国际经济学

No country can produce everything it needs — and even if one could, it shouldn't. International economics explains why trade happens, who wins and who loses, how exchange rates are set, and why "pegging the exchange rate, opening the capital account, and raising interest rates on your own" cannot all be done at once.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • Define comparative advantage through opportunity cost, and explain why a country that lags at everything can still gain from trade
  • Show how the gains from trade are distributed, and predict which groups will oppose openness
  • Compute the welfare cost of a tariff on a supply-and-demand diagram (producers, consumers, government, deadweight loss)
  • Explain exchange-rate movements with the supply and demand for foreign exchange, and distinguish nominal from real exchange rates and from purchasing power parity
  • Write the balance-of-payments identity and explain the mechanics of the policy trilemma and currency crises
Prerequisites: 01 Microeconomics (supply, demand, surplus) 02 Macroeconomics (interest rates, aggregate demand)

Chapter 1Why trade: comparative advantage

Opening question: A country is less productive than its neighbor at making every single product — does it still have a reason to take part in international trade? Ricardo's 1817 answer changed the world: yes — and it profits by doing so.

The core intuition: the contest is over what you give up, not who is best

Absolute advantage asks "who produces more"; comparative advantage asks "who produces it at lower opportunity cost". A lawyer types faster than the secretary (a double absolute advantage), but one hour of legal work earns the lawyer 2,000 yuan while an hour of typing is worth only 100 — the lawyer's opportunity cost of typing is enormous, and the secretary holds the comparative advantage. Trade works the same way: China builds aircraft less efficiently than the United States, but every aircraft China builds means more clothing given up, so "America builds aircraft, China makes clothing" lets both countries consume beyond their own capacity to produce. Trade is not a zero-sum contest; it lets each side concentrate on what it gives up least to do.

Key concepts

Comparative Advantage
比较优势
The ability to produce something at a lower opportunity cost. Comparative advantage always exists (one country's opportunity cost is lower in one good precisely where the other's is lower in the other), and that is the root of trade's universality.
Terms of Trade
贸易条件
The ratio of export prices to import prices. When it falls between the two countries' domestic opportunity costs, both sides win; the closer it sits to one side's opportunity cost, the more the gains tilt toward the other side.
Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)
生产可能性边界
The set of output combinations achievable with all resources employed. What trade adds: consumption can break through the nation's own PPF — the "expanded circle".
The real world · A T-shirt's journey around the world

Cotton starts in Texas → spun into yarn in Pakistan or Vietnam → woven, dyed, and finished in Bangladesh → sewn into shirts → sold worldwide. No step "must" happen in the same country: each stop is decided by local comparative advantage (resources, labor costs, accumulated technology, industrial clusters). In The Travels of a T-Shirt in the Global Economy, Pietra Rivoli uses a single supply chain to lay bare how comparative advantage, trade policy, and political games interact.

Common pitfalls

"Comparative advantage comes from technological leadership." — Technological leadership delivers absolute advantage; the key to comparative advantage is relative opportunity cost. Even a country that leads across the board has limited time and resources, so it puts them where its lead is greatest and leaves the rest to others — which is why trade benefits the strong and the weak alike. Another pitfall: "free trade benefits the nation as a whole, so everyone should support it" — no; next chapter: trade creates winners and losers at home.

Quiz 1.1Country A can produce 6 tons of wheat or 3 tons of steel per day; country B can produce 1 ton of wheat or 1 ton of steel per day. Which statement is correct?
Explanation: Country A holds the absolute advantage in both goods, but compare opportunity costs: in A, 1 ton of steel = 2 tons of wheat; in B, 1 ton of steel = 1 ton of wheat → B produces steel "more cheaply", and A's opportunity cost of wheat (0.5 steel) is below B's (1 steel). So A exports wheat, B exports steel, and both gain — provided the terms of trade fall between 1 steel = 1 wheat and 1 steel = 2 wheat. Country B, "behind at everything", still gains from trade through comparative advantage.
Quiz 1.2How are the gains from trade split between the two countries?
Explanation: In the example above, a price close to 1 steel = 2 wheat sends the bulk of the gain to country B (country A, the steel buyer, saves almost nothing); close to 1:1, the bulk goes to country A. Bargaining, market power, and the volume of trade all move the final price — "win-win" does not mean "split down the middle", and that is exactly where the politics of trade begins.

Chapter summary

  • Comparative advantage = lower opportunity cost, unrelated to absolute advantage.
  • Terms of trade between the two opportunity costs → both sides win; where they land decides the split.
  • Trade lets consumption move beyond the national PPF — the "expanded circle" is the geometry of the gains.

Chapter 2Winners and losers from trade

Opening question: Economics proves that trade benefits "the nation" as a whole — so why do mass anti-trade movements keep erupting? Because "the nation benefits" is an illusion created by aggregation: group by group, fortunes diverge completely.

The core intuition: the scarce factor pays the bill

Trade pushes down the price of import-competing goods → home producers of those goods (firms and workers) lose, consumers gain. One layer deeper (the intuition of Heckscher–Ohlin plus the Stolper–Samuelson theorem): the country's relatively scarce factor is the loser — when a capital-abundant country opens up, capital gains and labor loses; in a labor-abundant country the roles reverse. Consumers win "a little per person"; producers lose "a lot, concentrated among a few" — concentrated losses versus diffuse gains, which is why organized political energy against trade is always stronger (the logic of collective action; see track 16).

Key concepts

Stolper–Samuelson Theorem
斯托尔珀-萨缪尔森定理
Opening to trade raises the real return of a country's abundant factor and lowers the real return of its scarce factor — the mirror image in which the winners' side must contain losers.
Factor Endowment
要素禀赋
The relative abundance of capital, labor, land, and other production factors a country owns; it sets the direction of its comparative advantage (the Heckscher–Ohlin model).
Trade Adjustment Assistance
贸易调整援助
Retraining and compensation for those hurt by trade. The theoretical basis: winners' gains > losers' losses, so in principle the winners can compensate the losers and the nation still comes out ahead.
The real world · China's WTO entry and the American Rust Belt

Studies of the "China Shock" that followed China's 2001 WTO entry (Autor, Dorn, Hanson) show that US manufacturing towns exposed to import competition suffered long-lasting damage to employment, wages, marriage stability, and public health — and labor did not "smoothly reallocate" as the textbooks promised. On the whole, American consumers and China both gained enormously, but the costs of adjustment were highly concentrated in particular regions and groups of people, and far more persistent than the models assumed — the economic foundation for understanding today's trade politics.

Quiz 2.1If capital-abundant, labor-scarce America opens its markets further to labor-abundant countries, who is most likely to lose in the long run?
Explanation: The Stolper–Samuelson theorem: trade raises the real return of the abundant factor used intensively in export sectors (American capital and skill) and lowers the real return of the scarce factor used intensively in import-competing sectors (unskilled labor). Consumers do enjoy lower prices overall, but for low-skill groups who live on their wages, the wage loss can outweigh the consumer gains.
Quiz 2.2What policy conclusion follows from "the aggregate gains from trade are positive"?
Explanation: This is the standard meaning of a "potential Pareto improvement": trade creates room for compensation. The failure of real-world politics is precisely that compensation often never arrives or falls short (retraining programs are small and thinly covered). Anti-trade sentiment is not "ignorance of economics"; it is often a rational response to missing compensation — and economics that says this plainly is mature economics.

Chapter summary

  • Trade improves the aggregate, but distribution necessarily creates losers: the scarce factor plus import-competing sectors.
  • Diffuse gains, concentrated losses → protectionism carries inherently greater political energy.
  • Compensation mechanisms are part of the plumbing of free trade, not charity.

Chapter 3Tariffs and trade policy

Opening question: The tariff is the oldest trade policy and a politician's favorite: "make foreign sellers pay to protect domestic jobs." The questions are — who really pays a tariff, and how large is the bill?

The core intuition: a tariff is a wedge

For a small country (powerless to move the world price), a tariff t lifts the domestic price from the world price Pw to Pw+t. The extra money consumers pay splits three ways: (1) government revenue (imports × t); (2) extra profit for home producers; (3) deadweight loss (DWL) — on the production side, resources are pulled out of more efficient sectors into inefficient production; on the consumption side, some buyers give up the good. Those two triangles are the true social cost of "protection". And "foreign sellers pay", in the textbook case, is almost zero: they set the price, and the post-tax increase lands on domestic consumers.

Key concepts

Deadweight Loss
无谓损失
The net loss of total social surplus after an intervention: value that the destroyed trades would have created.
Quota
配额
A ceiling on import quantity. Like a tariff it raises the price, but the revenue becomes "rent" for the holders of import licenses (or is left to foreign exporters) — usually worse than a tariff.
Nominal vs Effective Protection
名义保护率与有效保护
A tariff on finished goods protects "value added": if raw materials are imported too and enter duty-free, the effective rate of protection can far exceed the nominal tariff — a hidden amplifier inside industrial policy.
The real world · The bill for the 2018–19 tariff war

After the United States and China raised tariffs on each other, a series of empirical studies (Amiti, Itskhoki, and Konings, among others) found that US import prices rose by almost exactly the full amount of the tariffs — the burden fell almost entirely on American importers and consumers, not on Chinese exporters through price cuts. Protected industries did see net job gains, but rising costs in downstream industries and the damage from retaliatory tariffs matched or outweighed them. The tariff is a real political weapon — and a real economic cost.

Quiz 3.1After a small country imposes a tariff, its domestic price rises by…
Explanation: That is the definition of "small": a price taker on the world market — buying more or less does not change Pw. So the tariff t lands entirely in the domestic price, paid by consumers and downstream firms. Only an economy large enough can shift part of the burden by pushing Pw down (the large-country case), and it pays for that with the risk of retaliation from its trading partners.
Quiz 3.2Compared with a tariff, an import quota is often worse because…
Explanation: A quota manufactures scarcity → the license itself becomes valuable (a rent). Whoever holds the license collects it: domestic importers (a breeding ground for rent-seeking and corruption) or — under a "voluntary export restraint" — foreign exporters (equivalent to handing tariff revenue to foreigners). A double warning from economics and public choice: opaque quantity controls are more easily captured than transparent taxes.

Chapter summary

  • A small country's tariff lands fully on the domestic price: consumers pay.
  • Welfare decomposition: government revenue + producer surplus − consumer loss = −DWL.
  • A quota's rents go to license holders — usually less transparent and more rent-prone than a tariff.

Chapter 4Exchange rates and the foreign-exchange market

Opening question: One dollar bought 7.2 yuan today, 6.7 last year, 7.0 the year before. Exchange rates flicker across the news every day — what forces are actually setting them?

The core intuition: an exchange rate is also just a price

The foreign-exchange market is the largest market in the world (average daily turnover above $7 trillion), yet the basic analysis of an exchange rate is still supply and demand: the demand for foreign currency comes from imports, outbound travel, outward investment, and speculation; the supply comes from export receipts and foreign investment inflows, among others. The Fed hikes → yields on dollar assets rise → global funds pour into dollars → the demand curve shifts right → the dollar appreciates. Over the long run, purchasing power parity (PPP) works like gravity: the same basket of goods should cost the same once converted at the exchange rate, and the currency of a high-inflation country tends to depreciate (the principle behind the Big Mac index). Over the short run, interest differentials and capital flows dominate — an exchange rate is an "asset price", and it is naturally volatile.

Key concepts

Purchasing Power Parity
购买力平价
The macro version of the law of one price: in the long run the exchange rate converges to "equal purchasing power". It deviates sharply in the short run, but it is the long-run anchor of exchange rates.
Real Exchange Rate
实际汇率
The nominal rate × relative price levels — the exchange rate that measures "competitiveness". A real depreciation = a gain in competitiveness; not the same thing as nominal swings.
Interest Rate Parity
利率平价
The interest differential ≈ the expected change in the exchange rate (+ a risk premium): high-yield currencies are expected to depreciate enough to offset the differential, or else there is an arbitrage. It ties the pricing of the two markets together.
Common pitfalls

"A weaker currency means the country is losing." — Depreciation cuts both ways: export and tourism competitiveness rise (the mirror image of Greece's pain at being unable to devalue during its crisis), while foreign-currency debt gets heavier (Argentina; the property developers' dollar bonds). Appreciation cuts both ways too. There is no simple honor or shame in an exchange rate, only the degree of fit with the economy's structure and policy goals. Also, the yuan "breaking 7" in the news has no magic — 7 is just a round number, and its psychological meaning far outweighs its economic meaning.

Quiz 4.1The Fed raises rates while China holds steady; all else equal, the renminbi tends to… against the dollar
Explanation: Interest differentials driving capital flows are the first mover of short-run exchange rates (interest rate parity). When the Fed hiked aggressively in 2022, the dollar index climbed to a twenty-year high and major currencies slid across the board — this mechanism on live display. Trade flows set the medium- and long-run fundamentals, but capital flows dwarf trade in scale.
Quiz 4.2A country runs 20% annual inflation, its trading partners 2%. By purchasing power parity, in the long run its currency should…
Explanation: The relative version of PPP: the inflation differential between two countries ≈ the currency's rate of depreciation. The high-inflation country's goods become expensive, and only depreciation keeps "the same basket equally priced" on both sides. Look at the exchange-rate path of any country with long-run high inflation (Turkey, Argentina) — it hugs the textbook line. Short-run deviations happen; the long run never grants an exemption.

Chapter summary

  • An exchange rate is the price of foreign exchange: in the short run it is driven by capital flows and interest differentials.
  • The long-run anchor is purchasing power parity: the inflation differential ≈ the depreciation rate.
  • No honor or shame in appreciation or depreciation — what matters is the currency of your debts and the structure of your exports.

Chapter 5The balance of payments: a nation's ledger

Opening question: The news reports "China's current-account surplus" and "America's record trade deficit". Is a surplus a medal for saving, or a medical record of imbalance?

The core intuition: a deficit = spending more than you earn, with the gap covered by IOUs

The balance of payments (BOP) is the nation's complete cash-flow statement with the rest of the world, in two great categories: the current account (trade in goods and services + investment income + transfers) and the capital and financial account (purchases and sales of assets). The accounting identity: current account + capital and financial account ≡ 0 (errors aside). A trade deficit necessarily comes with a net capital inflow — once foreigners hold the domestic currency, they necessarily buy domestic assets (government bonds, stocks, property): that is the "IOU". A deeper identity: current account = saving − investment. America's deficit means national saving is insufficient (government and private sectors borrowing too much); China's surplus was the natural outlet for its high saving. The exchange rate and the interest rate are precisely the prices that keep these two accounts in balance.

Key concepts

Current Account
经常账户
The trade balance + net income + net transfers. A surplus = net lending to the world; a deficit = net borrowing from the world.
Foreign Exchange Reserves
外汇储备
Foreign-currency assets held by the central bank. They serve a dual function — exchange-rate intervention (selling reserves when the account is in deficit) and insurance against crisis; too large a stock carries the costs of low returns and imported inflation.
Global Imbalances
全球失衡
A pattern of large-scale cross-border imbalances (e.g., the 2000s: Asian and oil-exporter surpluses vs the US deficit). Bernanke's "savings glut" hypothesis: the surplus countries' reserves poured into US Treasuries, depressing interest rates and inflating the housing bubble.
The real world · The other side of the surplus

China's "twin surpluses" (current plus capital) in the 2000s brought reserve accumulation on the order of $4 trillion: on one side, the export engine and its jobs; on the other, passively issued base money (the pressure of sterilization) and low-yield exposure to US Treasuries. The mercantilist instinct "exports good, imports bad" sees only half the ledger — a surplus is equally money lent to foreigners to spend, while households at home cut back their current consumption. The optimal imbalance is not "the bigger the better", but the one that matches saving-investment fundamentals.

Quiz 5.1An American trade deficit necessarily means…
Explanation: The BOP identity makes a "deficit" automatically mean a "capital inflow": foreigners sell us goods, then use the dollars they earn to buy American assets. Causality can run either way (low saving pulls in the deficit; a flood of foreign saving depresses rates and feeds spending), but the books always balance. Moralizing the deficit as "losing" wrongly applies household bookkeeping to a nation.

Chapter summary

  • BOP ≡ 0: a current-account deficit ⇔ a capital inflow.
  • One level deeper: CA = S − I — the essence of a deficit is a saving shortfall.
  • Surplus and deficit carry no moral valence; healthy means matched to fundamentals.

Chapter 6The trilemma and currency crises

Opening question: The 1997 Asian financial crisis, the 2015 Swiss franc "de-peg" flash crash, the 2022 plunges of the pound and the yen — the crisis scripts look strikingly alike: capital flees en masse, and the central bank cannot hold the exchange rate. Why is defending a rate so hard?

The core intuition: pick two of three

The impossible trinity (the policy trilemma): (1) a fixed exchange rate, (2) free capital movement, (3) an independent monetary policy — a country can have at most two of the three. The logic: with a fixed rate and free capital movement, arbitrage forces domestic and foreign interest rates to line up (otherwise funds flood without limit across the border and batter the exchange rate), and monetary policy is neutralized; to keep both a fixed rate and the freedom to raise rates on its own, a country must control the capital account. Hong Kong (gives up (3): a currency board), mainland China (partially restricts (2)), and the US and Japan (give up (1): floating rates) have each picked their side. The classic crisis script: an economy that chose "fixed + free" sees its fundamentals deteriorate → speculators bet on devaluation → the central bank hikes and sells reserves to defend the rate → the hikes crush the domestic economy and the reserves run dry → the peg is abandoned and the currency collapses overnight. Soros's 1992 raid on the pound was simply spotting this indefensible position early.

Key concepts

Currency Board
货币局制度
The strictest fixed exchange rate: base money 100% backed by foreign reserves, interest rates fully following the anchor currency. Hong Kong's Linked Exchange Rate system, 1983 to today.
Self-fulfilling Attack
自我实现的投机攻击
Speculators collectively bet on devaluation → the central bank is forced to hike and hurt the economy → the devaluation "proves" justified. A crisis can be ignited by expectations alone (game theory's bad equilibrium again).
Original Sin
原罪问题
Emerging markets cannot borrow long term in their own currency → depreciation instantly magnifies the foreign-debt burden → depreciation reinforces itself. The core amplifier of the Asian crisis.
The real world · The 1997 Asian crisis in brief

Thailand: a fixed exchange rate + free capital movement + heavy short-term dollar debt. Speculators borrowed baht and sold it for dollars → the Bank of Thailand hiked and sold reserves to fight back → the reserves ran dry within a month → on July 2 the peg was abandoned and the baht lost ~15% in a day, with contagion then spreading to Korea and Indonesia (currencies cut in half, GDP down by double digits). The checklist of lessons: the impossible triangle is not negotiable; short-term foreign debt is the gunpowder; reserve "adequacy" depends on the measure; and the controversy over the IMF's austerity programs continues to this day.

Quiz 6.1Hong Kong runs a linked exchange rate (fixed + free capital). This means the HKMA…
Explanation: The trilemma is a logical constraint, not a resource constraint — no pile of reserves buys back the third corner. When the Fed hiked in 2022, Hong Kong rates were forced up in lockstep (the HK-US rate gap approached zero), and the local property and stock markets absorbed the imported tightening. The price of a fixed exchange rate is pawning away monetary sovereignty.
Quiz 6.2A speculative currency attack can be "self-fulfilling". The best-fitting game structure is…
Explanation: A currency attack and a bank run (track 05, chapter 6) are isomorphic coordination failures: if nobody attacks, the rate holds; if everyone attacks, defending it (recession-inducing hikes) becomes unsustainably costly. Second-generation crisis models (Obstfeld) are built exactly on this: in the gray zone of fundamentals, expectations decide fate. The defense: never run a balance sheet that can be run on.

Chapter summary

  • The trilemma: fixed rate, free capital, independent money — pick two of three.
  • Crisis script: fundamentals worsen → speculative attack → the cost of defending explodes → the peg breaks and the currency plunges.
  • Currency attacks are isomorphic to bank runs: the bad equilibrium of a coordination failure.

Wrap-upNext steps & further reading

International economics roots downward into 01 Microeconomics (supply-and-demand analysis of trade) and 02 Macroeconomics (open-economy models), and connects upward to 11 Development and Innovation (trade and growth), 13 Urban and Regional Economics (economic geography and new trade theory), and 16 Political Economy (the political economy of trade).

  • Krugman, International Economics: Theory and Policy — the Krugman–Obstfeld edition; the standard textbook.
  • Pietra Rivoli, The Travels of a T-Shirt in the Global Economy — trade theory brought to life through a single supply chain.
  • Krugman, Pop Internationalism / The Economics of Peddling Anxiety — classic popular writing that debunks trade panic.
  • Crisis histories such as The Plaza and the Bridge of Helplessness: The Asian Financial Crisis — reads the trilemma as a story.
