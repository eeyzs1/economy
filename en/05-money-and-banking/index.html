Track 05 · Money & finance

Money & Banking

货币经济学

Money is the economy's blood, banks are its heart, and the central bank is its pacemaker. This track answers three questions: where money comes from (far more of it comes from bank ledgers than you think), how the central bank steers the economy, and why inflation and crises happen.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • State the three functions of money and explain why fiat money, worthless in itself, is accepted by everyone
  • Use fractional reserves and the money multiplier to explain how banks "create" money
  • Describe the central bank's three conventional tools and the operating logic of the interest-rate corridor
  • Trace how one rate hike travels the transmission chain to prices and employment, and why the "real interest rate" is the one to watch
  • Explain the causes and costs of inflation, and the mechanics of — and defenses against — bank runs
Prerequisites: 02 Macroeconomics 01 Microeconomics (supply & demand)

Chapter 1What is money

Opening question: A World War II prisoner-of-war camp had no government and no central bank, yet the economist Radford observed that Red Cross cigarettes gradually became universal "money" — tea, coffee, and shirts were all priced in cigarettes. Why cigarettes of all things? And what makes a piece of paper worth a hundred yuan?

The core intuition

The essence of money is not "something valuable" but something everyone believes everyone else will accept. It does three jobs: serve as a medium of exchange (sparing us barter's double coincidence of wants), a unit of account (one yardstick for all prices), and a store of value (banking today's labor for tomorrow's spending). Cigarettes won in the POW camp because they were standardized, divisible, durable, and universally wanted — for almost exactly the same reasons gold won out in human history. Today's paper currency and electronic deposits are fiat money: they have no intrinsic value and cannot be redeemed for gold; they are accepted because "the state decrees they must be accepted for taxes + everyone expects everyone else to accept them."

Key concepts

Fiat Money 法定货币
Money established by state decree and untethered from any commodity. Its value rests on collective expectation and legal status — which is also why it is so vulnerable to erosion by inflation.
Monetary Aggregates (M0/M1/M2) 货币口径
M0 = currency in circulation; M1 = currency + demand deposits; M2 = M1 + time deposits and other "near-monies." The broader the aggregate, the weaker its liquidity. When the news cites "TSF/M2 growth," it is talking about the broadest measures.
Liquidity 流动性
How easily an asset can be converted into money without loss of value. Cash is the most liquid, real estate the least. A financial run is, at bottom, the moment when everyone demands "maximum liquidity" at once.
The real world · Cigarette money and the mackerel economy

Radford (1945) recorded that once cigarettes became the camp's unit of account, a "credit market" even emerged — smoke now, settle later — along with "camp inflation and deflation" as prices swung with the cigarette supply. A more modern version: after US prisons banned tobacco, vacuum-sealed pouches of mackerel became the underground currency. The lesson: money is a social technology, and any object with money's properties can be "selected" as money — including today's e-payment balances and central bank digital currencies.

Common pitfalls

"Money sits in the central bank's great vault, and only becomes currency when withdrawn." — The overwhelming majority of modern money (more than nine-tenths of M2) was never printed paper at all; it is digits on banks' ledgers, created out of thin air by the act of lending (next chapter). "The central bank turning on the printing press" is a cartoon image of the monetary system — short of a cash shortage, the presses have little to do with money creation.

Quiz 1.1Cigarettes became money in the POW camp most importantly because:
Explanation: Many goods have intrinsic value (coffee, shirts), but money must be the all-around best at being standardizable, divisible, storable, and generally accepted. Nobody "decreed" cigarettes to be money — they were spontaneously "selected" through use, just like gold. Fiat money adds state fiat and self-sustaining expectations on top.
Quiz 1.2Time deposits count in M2 but not in M1 because they are…
Explanation: Monetary aggregates are ranked by exactly one criterion: liquidity. The tiers are not an ordering of "importance" but of "how readily the funds can be spent." Once you see this, you understand why central banks watch several aggregates at once: diverging growth rates across them trace money migrating between "live" money and "dead" money.

Chapter summary

  • Money = medium of exchange + unit of account + store of value; acceptance rests on collective expectations.
  • Fiat money has no intrinsic value; it stands on law and expectations.
  • Aggregates are tiered by liquidity: M0 ⊂ M1 ⊂ M2.

Chapter 2Banks and money creation

Opening question: The central bank has "printed" only 10 billion yuan in cash, yet deposits across the whole economy can reach 50 billion. Who created the extra 40 billion?

The core intuition: loans create deposits

A bank does not need to collect deposits before it can lend — the act of lending itself creates the deposit. When you borrow 1 million yuan to buy a home, the bank simply writes "deposit +1,000,000" into the seller's account; that is where the money is born, on a ledger. Of course, banks cannot lend without limit: under the reserve ratio a slice of every deposit must be posted as reserves, and the loans that go out flow back as new deposits — relent, re-posted… shrinking round by round, until total deposits = initial reserves ÷ reserve ratio. That is the money multiplier.

Key concepts

Fractional-Reserve Banking 部分准备金制
Banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest. It is the wellspring of money creation — and the seed of "deposits can be run on."
Money Multiplier 货币乘数
The ratio of total deposits to the monetary base, with a theoretical ceiling of 1/reserve ratio. Not a fixed parameter: it moves with bank behavior and the public's cash preferences.
Monetary Base 基础货币
Currency plus banks' reserves at the central bank — the part the central bank controls directly; broad money such as M2 expands on top of it through the multiplier.
Common pitfalls

"The bank lends out my deposit — the money just changes pockets." — That is the oldest, "intermediary" view. The modern view: in lending, a bank simultaneously creates a deposit (a liability) and a loan (an asset), and both sides of its balance sheet expand at once. System-wide lending determines not "who gets to use this money" but "how much deposit money exists in the economy." An individual bank and the banking system as a whole behave by entirely different logics — the most counterintuitive lesson in money and banking.

Quiz 2.1The reserve ratio falls from 20% to 10% (all else unchanged). What happens to the theoretical cap on the money multiplier?
Explanation: The multiplier cap = 1/r. At r = 20% → 5×; at r = 10% → 10×. A reserve-ratio cut is a strong easing signal: each yuan of base money supports more deposits. After 2008 the major central banks moved to an "interest-rate corridor + ample reserves" framework, demoting multiplier theory — but it remains the clearest scaffolding for understanding the layers of money.
Quiz 2.2For the banking system, "loans create deposits" means the money supply is…
Explanation: The central bank controls the base and the policy rate, but every round of "multiplier expansion" needs a bank willing to lend (risk controls, capital adequacy) and someone willing to borrow (demand). This explains a recession oddity: the central bank "floods the system" while credit shrinks — you can lead a horse to water, but you can't make it drink (the microfoundation of the liquidity trap).

Chapter summary

  • Loans create deposits; most of the money supply is digits on bank ledgers.
  • Multiplier cap = 1/reserve ratio; the real-world multiplier is constrained by behavior.
  • The central bank controls the base and rates; broad money is a dance of three partners.

Chapter 3Central banks and policy tools

Opening question: The Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the People's Bank of China (PBOC) — they produce no goods at all, yet they are called "the economy's first engine." What tools do they actually use to move an economy worth tens of trillions?

The core intuition: the central bank manages the price of money

The modern central bank's conventional arsenal: ① open-market operations (buying and selling government bonds or conducting repos to inject and drain base money, pinning the short-term rate on target); ② the policy rate / interest-rate corridor (the central bank's lending facility as the ceiling and its deposit facility as the floor, with the market rate caught in between); ③ the reserve requirement ratio (moves the multiplier ceiling — a heavy weapon, rarely fired). In crises, add ④ unconventional tools: quantitative easing (QE — buying long-term bonds outright to push down long rates) and targeted facilities. The frameworks all converge on the same destination: move market rates, and through them the spending of millions of households and the investment of firms.

Key concepts

Open-Market Operations 公开市场操作
The central bank buys and sells securities to adjust banking-system reserves — the everyday fine-tuning instrument for interest rates.
Interest-Rate Corridor 利率走廊
The central bank sets the rate at which banks borrow from it (the corridor's upper edge) and the rate at which they deposit with it (the lower edge); the market rate is pinned inside, with no need for frequent intervention.
Quantitative Easing (QE) 量化宽松
When short-term rates are already at zero but easing is still needed, the central bank buys long-term bonds outright, compressing the term premium. A crisis-era invention whose side effects and exit problems are still fiercely debated.
The real world · The PBOC's toolkit

The PBOC's everyday tools: the policy chain running from the medium-term lending facility (MLF) to the Loan Prime Rate (LPR); reverse repos to steer short-term liquidity; relending and rediscounting for targeted support of small businesses and green sectors; reserve-requirement adjustments; and structural instruments (such as relending for tech innovation). Unlike the Euro-American setup of "single target (inflation) + policy rate," the PBOC runs a multi-objective mandate (growth, prices, employment, the balance of payments, financial stability) — hence the broader toolkit.

Common pitfalls

"QE is just showering money on the public / printing it straight for the government." — QE's counterparties are financial institutions, and what it buys are the bonds they hold: Treasury securities enter the central bank's asset side, and reserve deposits enter its liability side. The money parks inside the banking system; whether it turns into credit and spending depends on banks and firms. That is why the enormous QE of 2008–2019 in the US, Japan, and Europe coexisted with chronically low inflation, while in 2020–21 — after fiscal checks went straight to households and credit surged — inflation came roaring in. Who gets the money, and what they do with it, matters more than where the money comes out.

Quiz 3.1The central bank wants to raise rates. Its most standard move is to…
Explanation: In the modern framework, the central bank uses open-market operations ± corridor settings to tighten or loosen reserves, letting the market rate "climb" to target on its own. Directly commanding a particular rate (B) is an administrative device that market economies rarely use; and C has the direction backwards — injecting liquidity usually accompanies a rate cut.

Chapter summary

  • The conventional three-piece kit: open-market operations, the interest-rate corridor, the reserve ratio; unconventional: QE.
  • The central bank's lever is the price of money (interest rates), not the quantity (printing).
  • Whether QE ends in inflation depends on who the money flows to and what it funds.

Chapter 4Interest rates and the transmission mechanism

Opening question: From March 2022, the Federal Reserve raised rates by a cumulative 5.25 percentage points in about a year and a half. How many stations must that hammer blow travel through — and for how long — before it lands on inflation?

The core intuition: interest rates are gravity

A rate hike is like turning up the whole economy's "gravity on money": money-market rates → deposit and loan rates → bond and equity valuations (discount rates rise, and asset prices sag) → business investment and household home-buying (credit gets dearer) → aggregate demand cools → wage and price increases slow. The chain is long, and the lag typically runs 6–18 months — which is the hardest part of monetary policy: the knife moves today, the wound hurts next year, so the central bank must "drive looking at the future." Judging the stance also requires the real interest rate = nominal rate − expected inflation: a nominal 5% against 6% inflation is still easy money; against 1% inflation it is genuinely tight.

Key concepts

Real Interest Rate 实际利率
The nominal rate minus expected inflation (the Fisher equation). Decisions hinge on the real rate: it is the true relative price of consuming today versus tomorrow.
Policy Lag 政策时滞
Recognition lag (spotting the problem) + decision lag + transmission lag. For monetary policy they are long and variable (6–18 months); for fiscal policy the pattern runs the other way.
Expectations Channel 预期渠道
The central bank guides the expected future path of rates, and the public prices assets and contracts accordingly. Manage expectations well and half the transmission is already done — the root of the central-bank communication revolution.
Quiz 4.1The nominal interest rate is 4% and expected inflation is 6%. The actual stance of monetary policy is…
Explanation: The real rate = 4% − 6% = −2%. The true cost of borrowing is negative — borrow today, repay tomorrow, and inflation chips in for you. That was exactly the situation in the opening phase of the 2021–22 global inflation: nominal rates rose, yet real rates stayed deeply negative and demand stayed hot, until aggressive hikes pushed real rates positive.
Quiz 4.2Why must a central bank "drive looking at the future"?
Explanation: The lag means "wait until inflation arrives, then hike" is always too late — brake today and the car only slows next year; and if the economy has cooled by then on its own, the brake becomes a disaster (over-tightening). Friedman's "long and variable lags" are the starting point of every cautious, rule-leaning tendency in monetary policy, inflation targeting included.

Chapter summary

  • The transmission chain: policy rate → market rates → asset valuations → credit and spending → aggregate demand → prices.
  • Judge the stance by the real interest rate; don't be fooled by the nominal one.
  • Lags of 6–18 months: monetary policy is the art of playing against the future.

Chapter 5Inflation

Opening question: At the end of 2021 the Fed still called inflation "transitory"; in 2022 US CPI surged to 9%. Where does inflation actually come from? And why, once it gets going, is it so hard to subdue?

The core intuition: too much money chasing too few goods

The oldest framework is the quantity theory of money: MV = PQ (money × velocity = prices × real output). Over the long run V and Q are fairly stable, so inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon — sustained price increases cannot stand without sustained excess money creation. In the short run, inflation has two kinds of triggers: demand-pull (aggregate demand overheating: stimulus programs, credit booms) and cost-push (supply shocks: oil prices, pandemic-era supply chains). The prescriptions differ: rate hikes work on the former; on the latter, hikes cannot cure the shortage — they can only suppress the wage–price spiral that follows. Inflation's most insidious cost is not "things get dearer" but blurred price signals and arbitrary redistribution of wealth (creditors → debtors). And deflation is often more dangerous still: consumption and investment are postponed while everyone "waits for cheaper," the real burden of debt grows heavier, and the economy spirals down.

Key concepts

Inflation Tax 通货膨胀税
When the government finances itself by issuing money, the purchasing power of everyone's cash holdings is diluted — taxation without legislation.
Wage–Price Spiral 工资—物价螺旋
Prices rise → workers demand raises → costs push prices higher still. Once expectations "unanchor," inflation becomes self-fulfilling and vastly costlier to cure.
Inflation Targeting 通胀目标制
The central bank publicly commits to keeping inflation at about 2%, anchoring expectations with its reputation. With expectations anchored, rate hikes need not overshoot.
The real world · A thermometer for hyperinflation

Hungary in 1946: prices doubled every 15 hours, and the state printed the largest banknote denomination in human history (10²⁰ pengő); then came China's 1948 gold yuan, Zimbabwe in 2008, and Argentina in 2023 (annual inflation above 200%). The shared script: monetized fiscal deficits → unanchored expectations → a run on the currency → accelerating depreciation. The cure, in every case without exception, has needed the same three-piece kit — cut off the fiscal tap, grant the central bank independence, install a new anchor — from Germany's 1923 Rentenmark to the currency reforms of the 1990s.

Quiz 5.1"Inflation is ultimately a monetary phenomenon" means…
Explanation: A one-off supply shock (oil, hog epidemics) can push up particular prices, but economy-wide inflation can rarely persist unless money accommodates it. Yet "ultimately" is a long-run claim: in the short run inflation can perfectly well be ignited by supply shocks and kept burning by expectations and the wage spiral — which is exactly where the "transitory" call went wrong: money had already overshot; the powder was laid, and only the match was missing.
Quiz 5.2Unexpected inflation hits 10%. The biggest winners and losers are…
Explanation: Inflation dilutes the real value of debt: a 30-year mortgage at 4% carries a real rate of −5% under 9% inflation — borrowers gain while lying down, while savers and bondholders watch their purchasing power evaporate. Inflation is a redistribution of wealth without legislation, and that is the hardest core of the claim that "inflation is unjust": it punishes the prudent saver.

Chapter summary

  • Long run: MV = PQ; sustained inflation always rides on excessive money creation.
  • Short run: demand-pull and cost-push call for different medicine; unanchored expectations are the hardest to cure.
  • Inflation = blurred signals + arbitrary redistribution; the deflationary spiral is often deadlier.

Chapter 6Financial stability and crises

Opening question: In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) went from "star performer" to receivership in about 48 hours. It had no bad loans and none of the classic run triggers — so how does a "good bank" die in two days?

The core intuition: a bank's cause of death is written on its balance sheet

A bank's core business is maturity mismatch: borrow short (deposits withdrawable at any time) and lend long (loans and bonds cannot be unwound early). As long as depositors do not all come at once, all is well; the moment they do — a bank run — even the healthiest bank can hardly escape (first come, first served; the latecomers are wiped out → rationally racing to be first through the door). The SVB version: deposits were highly concentrated among tech startups (a small crowd racing one another) + a large share of those deposits was parked in long-duration bonds; after rates rose, unrealized bond losses + deposit outflows → sell bonds and crystallize the losses → the losses become public → run. The three-piece defense: deposit insurance (so small depositors need not run), the lender of last resort (the central bank lends into the panic and takes "good liquidity and bad liquidity" alike), and prudential regulation (capital and liquidity requirements). The price is moral hazard: insured banks dare to run more risk — the tug-of-war between regulation and risk never has a final whistle.

Key concepts

Bank Run 银行挤兑
A self-fulfilling panic in which depositors all withdraw at once: because you fear others will run first, you run first. The bad equilibrium of a coordination game (see 04, Game Theory).
Lender of Last Resort 最后贷款人
Bagehot's principle: in a panic the central bank should lend freely — against good collateral, at a penalty rate — extinguishing the panic without rewarding recklessness.
Minsky Moment 明斯基时刻
Stability breeds complacency, complacency breeds leverage, leverage breeds collapse. The longer the markets stay calm, the closer the crash.
The real world · Anatomy of the Great Depression (1930–33)

The stock-market crash by itself did not make the Great Depression. The fatal second blow was the wave of banking panics: in 1930–33 roughly 9,000 US banks failed and the money supply contracted by one-third, while the Fed stood by (on nominally "liquidationist" grounds). Friedman and Schwartz's verdict: the Great Contraction was a failure of monetary policy. That lesson shaped the century that followed — deposit insurance (1933), the lender-of-last-resort function, and the "stop the bleeding first, whatever it takes" rescues of 2008 and 2020. Bernanke's academic career, built on studying the Depression, earned him the 2022 Nobel Prize.

Common pitfalls

"With deposit insurance, banks never fail, depositors face zero risk, and more coverage is always better." — The higher the coverage, the less depositors shop around for sound banks and the more boldly banks gamble (moral hazard amplified). That is why countries cap their insurance (US$250,000 in the United States, 500,000 yuan in China): leaving large depositors exposed above the cap is precisely what keeps them watching their bank. Every financial-stability design is the same trade-off question: the cost of panic versus the cost of moral hazard.

Quiz 6.1A bank run is a "self-fulfilling panic." Its game structure is…
Explanation: If others don't run, my deposit stays safe (good equilibrium); if others run, I must run even earlier (bad equilibrium). There is no "dominant strategy" defection problem here — it is pure failure of expectation coordination. So the remedy is not moral suasion but a change in the game's payoffs: deposit insurance makes "running" pointless, and the bad equilibrium is simply removed.
Quiz 6.2In its lender-of-last-resort role, the Bagehot principle a central bank should follow is…
Explanation: Distinguishing "short of liquidity but solvent" (worth saving) from "insolvent" (belongs in restructuring or bankruptcy) is the heart of a century of experience. A would create giant moral hazard (too big to fail); B penalizes recklessness through the penalty rate and collateral while still extinguishing contagion. The controversy of 2008 was exactly this: in the furnace of a crisis, that line is very hard to hold.

Chapter summary

  • A bank = a maturity-mismatch business; a run = the bad equilibrium of a coordination failure.
  • The three-piece defense: deposit insurance, lender of last resort, prudential regulation; the price is moral hazard.
  • The Great Depression's biggest lesson: in a panic, the central bank must be the system's anchor.

Wrap-upNext steps & further reading

Money and banking leads upward into 06 Financial Economics (asset pricing and the term structure of interest rates), outward into 07 International Economics (exchange rates and the trilemma), and backward into 17 History of Economic Thought (the running argument among Keynes, Friedman, and modern monetary theory).

  • Frederic Mishkin, The Economics of Money, Banking, and Financial Markets — the global standard textbook, complete in coverage.
  • Friedman & Schwartz, A Monetary History of the United States — the authoritative anatomy of the Great Contraction (a doorstop; the relevant chapters will do).
  • Carl Walsh, Monetary Theory and Policy — for graduate-level ascent.
  • Adam Tooze, Crashed — a first-rate narrative history of the decade after the 2008 crisis.
