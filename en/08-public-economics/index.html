Track 08 · Sectors & international

Public Economics (Public Finance)

公共经济学

Who takes care of the things markets cannot? Every cent the government collects in taxes — whose pocket does it come out of, and how many transactions does it erase? This track studies how far the government's "visible hand" should reach: taxation, spending, redistribution, and public decision-making itself.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • catalog the five types of market failure and explain why "market failure" does not automatically mean "government should take over" (government failure)
  • draw the deadweight loss of a per-unit tax on a supply–demand diagram and describe how DWL scales with the square of the tax rate and with elasticities
  • use elasticities to predict tax incidence (who really bears the burden) and explain what the Laffer curve does and does not imply for policy
  • distinguish progressive / proportional / regressive taxes and compare the efficiency–equity trade-offs of the major tax instruments (including the Ramsey rule)
  • explain the leaky bucket of redistribution and the design of the negative income tax, and analyze voting paradoxes, the median voter, and rent-seeking
  • deliver a cost–benefit verdict on a public project using the social discount rate and shadow prices
Prerequisites: 01 Microeconomics (supply & demand, surplus, and market failure)

Chapter 1Why we need government: taking stock of market failures

Opening question: Supermarket shelves hold bread and smartphones, so why can't they hold "national defense," "streetlights," or "flood control"? The sharper question runs the other way: since governments make mistakes too, what evidence would ever let us say "this one belongs with the government"?

The core intuition: the price system is an automatic bookkeeper — with blind spots

Market prices work like a marvelous bookkeeping machine: who wants what, who can pay, who should produce — the ledger balances itself. But the machine runs on only two assumptions: property rights that are clear and tradable, and symmetric information between the two sides of a trade. Smoke from a chimney has no "shareholders"; a streetlight cannot bill each passerby one by one (free riding); a used-car seller knows more than the buyer — the price mechanism fails in all these places. So government is called in to "balance the books" with taxes and public spending. But remember: government is itself an institution staffed by people with limited information and interests of their own. Repairing one invisible hand with another hand that trembles is a trade-off in its own right.

Key concepts

Market failure
市场失灵
The price mechanism fails to allocate resources to the social optimum. The five big categories: monopoly power, externalities, public goods, asymmetric information, and macro instability together with the distribution of income.
Public goods
公共品
Goods that are both non-excludable (no one can be stopped from using them) and non-rival (one person's use does not diminish anyone else's), such as national defense or a lighthouse. Markets under-supply them because of free riding.
Externality
外部性
Unpriced effects of an action on bystanders: pollution is a negative externality; the positive externality of vaccination leaves private supply too low. Policy tools: Pigouvian taxes, subsidies, and defining property rights.
Information asymmetry
信息不对称
One side of a transaction knows more than the other, producing adverse selection and moral hazard (insurance, credit, and health-care markets) — see Track 04.
Government failure
政府失灵
Government intervention makes outcomes worse than the market's: missing information, distorted incentives, capture by interest groups, or flaws in the decision mechanism itself (see Chapter 5, public choice).

Here is "where does government belong" as an inventory — the type of market failure, the matching policy tool, and the new risks each tool brings:

  • Monopoly and market power → antitrust, price regulation → risks: regulator capture and mis-set regulated prices;
  • Externalities → Pigouvian taxes / subsidies, tradable quotas → risks: marginal damages are measured poorly and one rate is applied to all;
  • Public goods → tax finance, public provision → risks: stated demand does not reveal true preferences, so over- or under-provision;
  • Information asymmetry → mandatory disclosure, social insurance, licensing → risks: moral hazard gets amplified instead;
  • Distribution and macro stability → progressive taxes, transfers, fiscal policy → risks: distorted incentives and policy lags.

The test is not "is the market perfect?" but which imperfect institution is cheaper — the imperfect market or the imperfect government. That comparison is the recurring benchmark of public economics.

The real world · Lighthouses and the 22 percent ledger

For decades the lighthouse was the textbook exhibit that pure public goods must be provided by government — until Ronald Coase dug through British shipping history: most English lighthouses of the 17th–19th centuries were privately built, bundling their fees into port charges collected from shipowners, and the system ran for two hundred years. Public goods can be privately produced; what is hard is designing the collection mechanism. Facts point the other way too: in 2023 China's general public budget spending was about 27.5 trillion yuan, roughly 22% of GDP; counting government funds and the rest of the "four budgets," the full-scope figure approaches one-third of GDP, and the OECD average is higher still. In a modern economy the "visible hand" stopped being a garnish long ago — it is a resource allocator standing shoulder to shoulder with the market, which is why what it gets right and wrong is worth an entire track.

Common pitfalls

"Public goods are whatever the government produces; whatever the government provides is a public good." Both directions are wrong. Compulsory education is financed by the public budget, but textbooks and school buildings are private goods (excludable and rival); the lighthouse is a public good, yet it was privately built and operated. In economics, "public provision" (who pays) and "public production" (who makes it) are two independent decisions — a city can outsource the making of its streetlights to a private firm, and a state-owned enterprise can produce a perfectly competitive private good. The other trap is "a market failure exists, so government should take over" — that holds only when the net gain from intervention (after subtracting the cost of government failure) is positive.

Quiz 1.1Which of the following comes closest to a "pure public good" (both non-excludable and non-rival)?
Explanation: National defense cannot exclude any resident from protection (non-excludable), and protecting one more person takes nothing away from the others (non-rival) — the canonical pure public good. A rush-hour subway car loses space the moment one more person boards (rival); a toll road can put up a gate (excludable); and the textbook itself is a private good — "financed by the budget" changes how it is provided, not what it is.
Quiz 1.2"This market fails, so a government takeover is sure to improve welfare." Where is the error?
Explanation: The right decision rule compares second-best against second-best: an imperfect market versus an imperfect government. Regulators may lack information, be captured, or face distorted electoral incentives (Chapter 5); bureaucracies face no profit constraint (Chapter 16). A market failure shows only that improvement is possible, not that intervention is better — every policy still has to pass the cost–benefit test of Chapter 6.

Chapter summary

  • Five market failures: monopoly, externalities, public goods, information asymmetry, and distribution/stability problems.
  • A public good is defined by two properties (non-excludable, non-rival) — not by who funds it or who produces it.
  • Government failure mirrors market failure: the benchmark is "which imperfection is smaller?"
  • Size of the public sector: China's full-scope spending is around 30% of GDP; the OECD average is higher.

Chapter 2The economics of taxation: deadweight loss, incidence, and the Laffer curve

Opening question: Charge the same one extra yuan per item, and yet: on cigarettes, the tax barely dents smoking and smokers bear most of the cost; on jewelry, transactions collapse and dealers howl. When the tax blade comes down, whose cake is it cutting — and how much lands on the table?

The core intuition: a tax is a wedge, and the trades it erases are pure loss

A per-unit tax drives a tax wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers keep: buyers pay more, sellers receive less, and trades that both sides were willing to make get squeezed out. The surplus those vanished trades would have created (buyer surplus + seller surplus) disappears into thin air — that is deadweight loss (DWL): welfare that no one collects, pure leakage. And who really bears a tax has nothing to do with who the law says pays it — only elasticities matter: the side that is less able to walk away (the less elastic side) bears more. Think of a packed subway car: whoever is in more of a hurry gets squeezed flatter.

Key concepts

Deadweight loss
无谓损失
The net social surplus lost when a tax (or another intervention) pushes quantity away from the equilibrium; geometrically the triangle between the supply and demand curves, roughly proportional to the square of the tax rate.
Tax incidence
税收归宿
Where the burden of a tax actually lands in the economy. Determined by the relative elasticities of supply and demand, not by who is legally liable; the less elastic side bears the larger share.
Tax wedge
税楔子
The gap between the buyer's price and the seller's price. The thicker the wedge, the more trades shrink — and the larger the deadweight loss.
Laffer curve
拉弗曲线
The inverted-U relationship between the tax rate and total revenue: revenue is zero at both 0% and 100%. It implies that a revenue-maximizing rate exists, but not that cutting taxes always raises revenue.
DWL ≈ ½ × t × ΔQt is the tax per unit and ΔQ the shrinkage in quantity traded; since ΔQ itself is proportional to t, DWL grows roughly with the square of t — double the rate, roughly quadruple the loss

Tax incidence: statutory incidence (who remits the tax on the invoice) and economic incidence (who actually ends up poorer) are two different things. Tax the seller, and sellers pass the burden forward to buyers or backward to factor owners, as elasticities allow. The extreme cases are worth memorizing: with perfectly inelastic demand (a life-saving drug), buyers bear everything and DWL is zero; with perfectly inelastic supply (land in the city center), sellers bear everything and DWL is again zero — the more "dead" the tax base, the smaller the efficiency loss, and the more brutal the distribution. That is the efficiency case economists make for property taxes and land-based finance.

The Laffer curve: a higher rate pulls revenue in two opposite directions — the rate itself lifts revenue, but the base (the income people report, the transactions that take place) shrinks as the rate climbs. Avoidance, the underground economy, reduced labor supply, and capital flight are all "base responses." When responses are strong enough, there is a revenue-maximizing rate t*: beyond it, raising the rate lowers revenue.

The real world · The window tax and the tobacco tax

In 1696 England introduced a window tax: levied per window, meant to tax "whoever has the big house." The nation responded with a wave of bricked-up windows, and the rental rooms of Dickens's London went pitch dark — the tax squeezed a good ("daylight") straight out of consumption: a textbook deadweight loss, forcibly abolished only in 1851. Today's contrast is the tobacco excise: addiction makes demand extremely inelastic, so a tax passes almost fully through to smokers and barely shrinks the base — fiscally "efficient," with the positive externality of less smoking thrown in for free. But low-income households smoke at higher rates, so the burden is regressive. One instrument, with efficiency, equity, and health goals all entangled — exactly the opening problem of tax design in Chapter 3.

Common pitfalls

"The law makes the store remit the tax, so the store bears it." Wrong. Taxing sellers and taxing buyers produce identical incidence — prices adjust on their own, and elasticities rule (in Figure 2-1 the gap between the two price lines has nothing to do with who writes the check). The second frequent error: "the Laffer curve proves that cutting taxes always raises revenue." That holds only when the current rate is already past the peak (inside the forbidden zone); on the left side of the curve, a cut is just a revenue loss. The curve itself carries a deeper point: the revenue-maximizing rate is not the welfare-maximizing rate — welfare must net out DWL and weigh equity too.

Quiz 2.1The law says the convenience store (the seller) remits a 1-yuan tax per drink. If consumers are quite price-insensitive (inelastic demand), who mainly bears the tax?
Explanation: Incidence is set by relative elasticities, not by legal liability. With inelastic demand, the store can raise prices without losing customers, so it shifts the burden forward: the buyer's price jumps while the seller's net barely moves (set Ed to 0.5 in Figure 2-1 and watch the buyer's share pass half). In reverse, when supply is inelastic (say, land in the city center), the burden lands mostly on the seller.
Quiz 2.2Other things equal, a per-unit tax rises from 1 yuan to 2 yuan per item. Roughly how many times larger does the deadweight loss become?
Explanation: DWL ≈ ½·t·ΔQ, and ΔQ is itself proportional to t, so DWL ∝ t²: double the rate, roughly four times the loss; triple it, roughly nine times. This grounds two policy instincts: broad base with low rates beats narrow base with high rates, and a one-off need to raise a large sum is far cheaper to spread across many tax bases than to pile onto a single one.
Quiz 2.3Which statement about the Laffer curve's policy implications is correct?
Explanation: Revenue is zero at both ends (0% and 100%), so a peak t* exists — but its location depends on the strength of avoidance and labor-supply responses (it moves with e in Figure 2-2), and there is no reason it should be exactly 50%. A and C err on opposite sides: a cut raises revenue only inside the zone, a hike raises it only on the left side. For overall tax burdens in most advanced economies, the empirical literature mostly places them left of the peak — though for high marginal rates on particular groups, the forbidden-zone evidence is real.

Chapter summary

  • A tax is a wedge driven between the buyer's price and the seller's: quantity contracts, and the erased surplus is DWL, growing with t² and with elasticity.
  • Incidence is set by relative elasticities, not by who legally pays: the less elastic side bears more.
  • The more inelastic the base (land, necessities), the smaller the efficiency loss — and the more concentrated the distributional hit.
  • The Laffer curve has a peak at t*, located by how the base responds; a cut raises revenue only to the right of it, and maximum revenue ≠ maximum welfare.

Chapter 3Designing a tax system: balancing efficiency and equity

Opening question: Raise the same 100 million yuan from wages, from consumption, from property, or from profits, and the outcomes differ wildly. A nation's tax system is not a single rate schedule but a thousand of these trade-offs stacked on top of one another — so what is the standard for a good tax system?

The core intuition: a good tax system in four words — broad base, low rate

Two axes judge a tax system. Efficiency: distort decisions as little as possible — Chapter 2 showed DWL growing with the square of the rate, so "a little from many bases" is far cheaper than "a lot from one base": that is broad base, low rate. Equity has two layers: horizontal equity (people in the same position pay the same tax) and vertical equity (more from those who can afford more, i.e., progressivity). The two axes fight constantly: progressivity calls for high marginal rates, and high marginal rates do their fiercest distorting at the top of the income distribution. Optimal tax theory (Mirrlees) studies exactly this constrained problem: hold the redistribution goal fixed and minimize the efficiency loss. The winning designs are often surprisingly plain: close the loopholes, widen the base, and be gentler on labor income than on the stock of capital.

Key concepts

Progressive / proportional / regressive
累进 / 比例 / 累退
Classified by whether the tax as a share of income rises, stays flat, or falls as income rises. Judge it by the average tax rate, not by the schedule of marginal rates.
Horizontal & vertical equity
横向公平与纵向公平
Horizontal: equal ability to pay pays equal tax; vertical: greater ability pays more. The first demands a clean base (few loopholes); the second defines progressivity.
Tax base & tax rate
税基与税率
What is taxed (income, consumption, property, profits) and how much. Revenue = base × rate: loopholes erode the base, force rates higher, and amplify distortions — a vicious circle.
Ramsey rule
拉姆齐规则
The optimal commodity-tax structure: tax each good in inverse proportion to its demand elasticity — more on the inelastic, less on the elastic — to minimize the total distortion. But it collides with vertical equity (necessities are precisely where the poor spend).
Tax rate ∝ 1 / demand elasticityThe Ramsey inverse-elasticity rule (Ramsey, 1927): the efficiency-optimal commodity tax contracts every good's traded quantity by the same proportion; but "picking on the inelastic" systematically pushes the burden onto necessities — efficiency and equity collide head-on

To gauge a whole system's progressivity you must compute the net redistribution of taxes and transfers together (Chapter 4) — any single rate schedule read alone will mislead.

The real world · The global minimum corporate tax and special additional deductions

In 2021, 136 jurisdictions agreed under the OECD framework on a 15% global minimum corporate tax rate (taking effect in the EU and elsewhere from 2024). The motive was exactly the prisoner's dilemma of tax competition: countries kept undercutting each other's rates to attract booked profits, the base was hollowed out by "profit shifting," and no one dared raise rates unilaterally. After coordination, the battlefield shifts from "who is cheaper" to "whose infrastructure is better," and taxpayers collectively save the rent dissipation of the race to the bottom. The Chinese practice runs in the other direction: since 2019, the individual income tax has included special additional deductions (children's education, elder care, mortgage interest, infant childcare, and more) — in essence adjusting "nominal income" toward a base closer to "true ability to pay": horizontal equity, taken to fine grain.

Common pitfalls

"The rate schedule is progressive, so the tax system is progressive." Wrong twice. First, indirect taxes such as VAT and excises are regressive, so overall progressivity depends on the weighting across taxes and on the loopholes in the base (capital gains and hidden income at the top are the hardest to reach). Second, progressivity must be judged by the net effect — the Nordic countries' direct taxes are not extremely progressive, but they fund the state with heavy consumption taxes and then channel the proceeds back to low and middle incomes through universal public services and transfers, ending up among the world's strongest net redistributors. Judge a tax system by the complete loop: from whom it is collected, and into whose hands it returns.

Quiz 3.1A VAT taxes consumption at a uniform proportional rate. Why is it usually considered regressive?
Explanation: Regressive means tax / income falls as income rises. A VAT's nominal rate is uniform, but low-income families have a high Engel coefficient (most of their income must be spent, much of it on heavily taxed necessities) and little saving, while high-income families consume a far smaller share of theirs. The same 13% therefore claims a bigger slice of a poor family's income — "a proportional tax on consumption is regressive on income." A is wrong because the nominal rate really is uniform; and D's credit mechanism merely avoids double taxation — it does nothing to change who finally pays.
Quiz 3.2By the Ramsey rule, taxing low-elasticity goods more heavily is more efficient. Why should we still be wary of doing this in practice?
Explanation: The Ramsey rule is a pure efficiency proposition: contract every good's traded quantity by the same proportion and total DWL is minimized. But "picking on necessities" means food, water, and basic medicines carry the high rates — and Chapter 2's incidence analysis tells us the less elastic side bears more, exactly where low-income households are concentrated as demanders. Optimal tax theory (Diamond–Mirrlees and successors) corrects the rule with distributional weights. C is wrong because elasticities can be estimated; D has it backwards — an inelastic base is the easiest to tax. The classic compromise: zero or reduced rates on food (a common VAT practice across countries).

Chapter summary

  • Two axes judge a tax system: efficiency (fewer distortions) and equity (horizontal: equal treatment; vertical: more from greater capacity).
  • DWL grows with the square of the rate → broad base, low rate beats narrow base, high rate.
  • Ramsey rule: rates inversely proportional to elasticity are most efficient, but they conflict with the vertical equity of protecting the poor.
  • Overall progressivity is the net of taxes + transfers, not any single rate schedule.
  • Tax competition is a global prisoner's dilemma: the global minimum corporate tax is one attempt at coordination.

Chapter 4Public spending and redistribution: the leaky bucket

Opening question: Carry a bucket of water from the rich to the poor, and some slops out along the way. How much gets spilled decides how much water we should carry at all. With this "leaky bucket," Arthur Okun posed the question every welfare state must answer: where is the limit of redistribution?

The core intuition: a transfer is not a move between buckets — it is a change of buckets

Intuitively, redistribution looks like moving house: scoop from bucket A, pour into bucket B, total unchanged. But real transfers leak: administrative costs (eligibility checks, collection, fraud prevention), distorted incentives (high marginal tax rates make people work less; benefit phase-outs make them afraid to earn more), and avoidance and migration (capital and the highly skilled vote with their feet). What leaks is not somebody's income — it is the whole pie shrinking. So optimal redistribution is not "more is fairer"; it stops at the margin where the social gain of one more yuan transferred equals the extra efficiency cost leaked. The design art is building a sturdier bucket — for example, subsidizing only people who work (preserving the incentive to work) is one plank patched into place.

Key concepts

The leaky bucket
漏桶效应
Okun's metaphor: efficiency losses are unavoidable in the course of redistribution, and the optimal scale of redistribution sits where marginal social benefit equals marginal leakage. The leaks come from administrative costs and distorted incentives.
Negative income tax
负所得税
A design championed by Milton Friedman: below a threshold, people pay no tax and instead receive a subsidy, phased out gradually as income rises (a phase-out rate below 100% preserves work incentives) — one unified program replacing categorical welfare schemes.
Automatic stabilizers
自动稳定器
Taxes and transfers move countercyclically without new legislation (taxes fall and unemployment benefits rise in a recession), propping up aggregate demand; they complement discretionary policy.
Pay-as-you-go (PAYG)
现收现付制
Current workers' contributions directly pay current retirees' pensions rather than being saved for the future. Strengths: risk-sharing across generations and no investment risk; weakness: population aging pushes contribution rates up (see Track 14).

Two extensions. First, social security is more than redistribution: pensions (PAYG), health insurance, and unemployment insurance all carry an insurance function — pooling longevity, illness, and unemployment risks that no individual can bear alone — which makes the "should we" debate more complicated than "how much leaks" (Track 14 develops this). Second, automatic stabilizers: progressive taxes and unemployment benefits make the budget automatically collect less and spend more in recessions — the first line of macro defense (fiscal policy in Track 02), with the virtues of zero lag and immunity to the political cycle.

The real world · The EITC and targeted poverty alleviation: planking the bucket

The US Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) is the negative income tax idea made real: it subsidizes only low earners who work, with the subsidy first rising and then phasing out slowly as income grows. Around 2023 it reached some 30 million households a year, making it one of the largest cash-transfer programs in the United States. It welds "top up income" to "preserve incentives" — recipients must be employed, which dodges the welfare trap — and both parties have supported it in turn. China's counterpart is the dibao safety net and the targeted poverty-alleviation campaign: by 2020, all rural residents below the current poverty line had been lifted out, by way of the combined program of income, education, medical care, and housing support (the "two assurances and three guarantees"), plus the administrative machinery of cadres stationed in villages to identify households — spending heavy administrative capacity to suppress "mistargeting leakage," at a considerable cost of verification and enforcement. Neither design dominates; they are planks fitted at different points, for different leakage structures and different administrative capacities.

Common pitfalls

Two symmetric extremes, both wrong. "Redistribution is just moving water — costless." That ignores administrative costs and distorted incentives: a 100% marginal phase-out rate makes recipients refuse overtime or give up work altogether (the welfare trap) — the most visible leak in the bucket. "If it leaks at all, not a cent should be redistributed." Equally wrong: leakage measures a cost, not a veto. As long as society values equity highly enough (or the marginal social benefit of transfers is positive), leaky redistribution still beats none. The real argument is not "whether" but "which plank to patch, and how far down the road to carry the bucket."

Quiz 4.1What is the core meaning of Okun's "leaky bucket" metaphor?
Explanation: The leaky bucket states a trade-off, not a verdict: transfers carry unavoidable efficiency costs that grow with scale, so an interior optimum exists (τ* in Figure 4-1). C and D each occupy an extreme — the first denies that society's valuation of fairness can outweigh the leak, the second denies that administrative and incentive costs have a positive minimum. A swaps an economics proposition for a legal one: the bucket says nothing about legitimacy.
Quiz 4.2Compared with a welfare benefit that is cut by 1 yuan for every extra yuan earned, what is the negative income tax's (NIT) key advantage?
Explanation: A "lose one yuan of benefit per yuan earned" scheme imposes a 100% effective marginal tax rate — extra work brings no extra income, so the rational choice is to stay under the threshold (the welfare trap). An NIT's phase-out rate (say 30%–50%) means each extra yuan earned reduces the benefit by only 0.3–0.5 yuan, so working always pays — the classic plank in the bucket, and the mechanical heart of the EITC. D describes exactly the old problem the NIT corrects; B is wrong because an NIT still needs income reporting and verification.

Chapter summary

  • Redistribution leaks: administrative costs, labor-supply distortions, avoidance and migration — and the pie shrinks with them.
  • Optimal redistribution sits where marginal benefit = marginal leakage — not "the more the better," and not "none at all."
  • Negative income tax / EITC: topping up income while preserving work incentives — the model design for "planking the bucket."
  • Social security doubles as insurance; progressive taxes + unemployment benefits are macro automatic stabilizers.

Chapter 5Public choice: voting, agendas, and rent-seeking

Opening question: With 3 people and 3 options, will majority rule always deliver the outcome "the majority wants"? Condorcet answered no in 1785 — and the problem is stranger than intuition suggests: sometimes the person who decides the order of the votes wields more power than the people who vote.

The core intuition: preferences cannot always be "added up"

Everyone carries an ordered preference list in their head, but when many lists are aggregated into one "social list," strange things happen: with three people holding A>B>C, B>C>A, and C>A>B, pairwise voting yields A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A — a cycle, with no stable winner. Whoever then controls the agenda (which pair votes first) controls the result. When preferences are "single-peaked" (each person has one ideal point, declining on both sides), the cycle disappears and the equilibrium lands on the median voter's ideal point — which is why two-party contests drift toward the center (the Downs model). And Kenneth Arrow proved a bleaker, general theorem: no aggregation mechanism can simultaneously satisfy a modest set of reasonable conditions. Politics is not a perfect adding machine; it is a market with its own supply and demand — votes, power, and rents are traded in it, and rent-seekers spend real money shaping the rules for dividing the pie rather than growing it.

Key concepts

Condorcet cycle / cyclical majorities
孔多塞循环 / 循环多数
Majority voting cycles among options — A beats B, B beats C, C beats A — a failure of transitivity. Multi-peaked preferences leave no stable ranking of "society's majority preference."
Median voter theorem
中位选民定理
With single-peaked preferences, a one-dimensional issue, and simple majority rule, the outcome converges to the option the median voter most prefers; two-candidate platforms move toward the center (Downs, 1957).
Arrow's impossibility theorem
阿罗不可能定理
No mechanism for aggregating social preferences (with three or more alternatives) can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship — a "perfect voting rule" is mathematically impossible.
Rent-seeking
寻租
Spending resources to capture or defend artificial rents created by government (licenses, tariffs, monopoly rights) (Tullock, 1967; Krueger, 1974). Lobbying costs are a net social loss — resources spent dividing the pie, not baking it.

From voting failures to a broader insight: the political market has supply and demand too. Voters are rationally ignorant (one vote barely moves anything, so digging into policy does not pay), politicians maximize votes, bureaucrats maximize budgets and headcount, and interest groups — concentrated beneficiaries, firmly organized — routinely defeat a large and dispersed public. Small, concentrated interests (industries shielded by tariffs, licensed practitioners) often beat the diffuse general public. Rent-seeking theory draws a chilling corollary: the welfare loss from monopoly or regulation is not just the Harberger triangle — it also includes the entire lobbying cost dissipated in the fight for the license, resources that could have been producing something.

The real world · The New York taxicab medallion: the rise and fall of a rent

New York has capped taxi licenses (medallions) since 1937; for decades the count sat frozen at just over 13,000. The artificial scarcity turned the medallion into an asset: around 2013 a single medallion traded for more than a million dollars — money that bought no cab and served no passenger, but purchased the "exclusive right to operate" itself: the holder's discounted stream of future rents. Countless drivers borrowed to buy medallions, staking their working lives on the debt; when ride-hailing struck, prices collapsed and left a landscape of debt behind. The rent-seeking ledger: the lobbying to keep the cap, the loan interest, the medallion premium — all of it real social cost paid for the "right to divide the pie," and not a cent of it baked anything. For contrast: Anne Krueger (1974), studying Indian licenses and shipping quotas, formally named rent-seeking and estimated the associated dissipation at a sizable fraction of GDP.

Common pitfalls

"The outcome of the vote is the will of the people." Under cyclical majorities, that aggregate simply does not exist — change the agenda and you change the "will" (Figure 5-1). Even with single-peaked preferences and an outcome stable at the median voter, what you have is the will of the median: majority voting is deaf to intensity (a lukewarm 51% flattens an intense 49%). The second trap: "lobbying is just expressing an opinion." When policy creates artificial rents, the spending around them (lawyers, lobbyists, connections, queuing) is a net social loss — exactly what Tullock pointed out and traditional analysis missed.

Quiz 5.1Single-peaked preferences, a one-dimensional issue, simple majority rule, a two-candidate race — where does the equilibrium platform land?
Explanation: Median voter theorem (Black, 1948; Downs, 1957): with single-peaked preferences on a one-dimensional issue, any platform off the median is beaten by an opponent who moves to the other side of the median and hugs it — hence the two-party convergence toward the center. A's mean fails: when the preference distribution is skewed right, the mean is pulled up by the rich while the median stands still — the classic point that "the median voter governs, while the mean only reflects income" (see also the wage-distribution discussion in Track 09).
Quiz 5.2With three people, three options, and a majority cycle (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A), who in fact decides the outcome?
Explanation: Inside a cycle, no option is stably preferred by a majority (A's premise does not exist). Controlling the agenda — which pair votes first, which option "sits out" into round two — delivers any result you like: the three buttons in Figure 5-1 each produce a different winner. That is the institutional meaning of agenda-setting power: fights over parliamentary rules, committee assignments, and amendment order are no procedural footnote. C confuses majority rule with Borda counting and other scoring rules; D misses that the outcome is anything but random — to the agenda-setter it is highly controllable.

Chapter summary

  • Majority voting over multi-peaked preferences produces Condorcet cycles: no stable winner, and the agenda decides.
  • Single-peaked preferences + a one-dimensional issue → median voter theorem: campaigns converge toward the center.
  • Arrow's impossibility theorem: a perfect mechanism for aggregating social preferences is mathematically impossible.
  • Rent-seeking: resources dissipated over the right to divide the pie are a net social loss (licenses, tariff lobbying).
  • Policies whose benefits are diffuse but whose losses are concentrated are the easiest for organized minorities to defeat.

Chapter 6Cost–benefit analysis: pricing the priceless

Opening question: A high-speed rail line, a dam, a climate policy — worth it or not? The costs and benefits mix steel and concrete with time, human lives, clean air, and the well-being of generations not yet born. Government investment has no income statement; cost–benefit analysis (CBA) builds one for it.

The core intuition: convert everything to one yardstick, then mind two dials

The idea of CBA is plain: list every year's costs and benefits, discount them to present value, add them up, and proceed only if the NPV is positive. The difficulty lies in two dials. Dial one: the discount rate r — future benefits are divided by (1+r) to the t-th power. It looks like a technical parameter but is really an ethics question: discounting means our descendants' welfare weighs less on today's scales, and choosing r at 1% or at 5% is enough to flip climate policy from "act now" to "take it slow." Dial two: shadow prices — where market prices are distorted (unemployment, regulation, tax wedges), value resources at their social opportunity cost: the true cost of hiring an unemployed worker is not the wage but the leisure and the output elsewhere that he gives up. One notch on either dial can swing the verdict — so the honest way to do CBA is to put parameter sensitivity on the table, not to report a single number.

Key concepts

Social discount rate
社会贴现率
The rate at which a public project's future benefits are converted to present value. Choosing it has a positive dimension (the return to capital) and a normative one (intergenerational equity), and it sways the verdict on long-horizon projects enormously.
Shadow price
影子价格
The social opportunity cost of a resource, entered in place of distorted market prices: the shadow wage of unemployed labor is below the market wage; foreign exchange, land, and environmental capacity each carry their own shadow prices.
Value of a statistical life (VSL)
统计生命价值
A population-level value for life estimated from wage–risk trade-offs (what people will pay to reduce the probability of death), used to value benefits in traffic-safety, environmental, and health regulation. It estimates not "the price of a life" but the aggregation of willingness to pay for tiny changes in risk.
Contingent valuation
条件价值评估
Surveys that ask people directly what they would pay for non-market goods (scenery, the existence of a species). The disputed part is hypothetical bias, so the design must be careful (realistic payment scenarios, probes for uncertainty).
NPV = Σ (B_t − C_t) / (1 + r)^tB and C are the benefits and costs in year t; projects with NPV greater than 0 pass the test. Each extra percentage point of r in the denominator cuts a year-50 benefit by nearly forty percent — long-horizon projects are brutally sensitive to r
The real world · The Clean Air Act's 30 : 1 and the 8% parameter manual

The United States has institutionalized CBA: executive regulations must clear review by the White House's budget office (OIRA). The EPA's retrospective assessment of the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments estimated that in 2020 alone benefits came to about $2 trillion against costs of about $65 billion — a benefit–cost ratio of roughly 30 : 1 — with most of the benefits from premature deaths averted by lower particulate pollution, converting "health" into currency precisely via the value of a statistical life (VSL). China's counterpart is the national economic evaluation inside feasibility studies: the official manual "Methods and Parameters for the Economic Evaluation of Construction Projects" (third edition, 2006) long prescribed a social discount rate of 8% and required parameter adjustments for shadow prices and shadow wages — the same discipline, in a different language.

Common pitfalls

"The discount rate is a technical detail — any value will do." A year-50 benefit keeps about sixty percent of its value at r = 1% but under ten percent at 5%. For intergenerational projects — climate, infrastructure — the choice of r is a stand on intergenerational ethics: the Stern Review used about 1.4% (a near-zero rate of pure time preference) and called for immediate, forceful abatement; Nordhaus argues for roughly 3% or more, reflecting market returns, and reaches far gentler conclusions. The second trap: "a negative NPV means the project is worthless." NPV measures aggregate efficiency and carries no distributional weights — a project with a slightly negative NPV that still brings electricity to the poorest region may pass once distributionally weighted. An honest CBA reports sensitivity analyses instead of hiding value judgments inside parameters.

Quiz 6.1In appraising a public project that employs many unemployed workers, how should the labor costs be entered?
Explanation: Shadow price = the resource's social opportunity cost. If the workers were previously unemployed and the hiring does not crowd out labor for other projects, the social cost of employing them is not the wage (that is just a transfer) but the forgone leisure and alternative employment — usually below the wage. Pricing at market rates overstates cost and wrongly kills labor-intensive public projects. But C goes too far: leisure has value, training has costs, and in the long run labor supply is not perfectly inelastic; estimating the shadow wage must also allow for "crowding-out" (in a tight labor market, the shadow wage approaches the market wage).
Quiz 6.2Which statement about the social discount rate r is most accurate?
Explanation: The higher r is, the harder distant benefits get discounted — that is less weight on future generations (B has the direction reversed). It entangles the return to capital (the positive side) with pure time preference and intergenerational equity (the normative side) — exactly the root of the Stern (about 1.4%) versus Nordhaus (3% or more) divide in climate economics: near-identical model structures, one different r, and opposite policy conclusions. D's problem is that market rates embed taxes, risk premia, and market incompleteness, and need not represent how society ought to weigh the welfare of its descendants.

Chapter summary

  • CBA discounts and sums all costs and benefits: NPV greater than 0 passes the test.
  • The social discount rate is the intergenerational-ethics dial: the level of r can flip the ranking — and the survival — of long-horizon projects.
  • Shadow prices replace distorted market prices with social opportunity costs (unemployed labor, foreign exchange, environmental capacity).
  • Non-market values are estimated with VSL, contingent valuation, and related methods — watch for hypothetical bias.
  • An honest study reports sensitivity analyses and distributional weights instead of hiding its value judgments.
