Environmental & Resource Economics

环境与资源经济学

Why do environments get polluted and resources run down? The answer usually is not that "people have turned bad," but that prices fail to count the damage and the scarcity. This track studies how market failure takes shape in the environment and in natural resources — externalities, the tragedy of the commons, intergenerational problems — and the three families of corrective tools: putting a price on pollution, assigning property rights, and setting caps.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • Draw private and social marginal cost on a supply-and-demand diagram, explain why polluting goods are "over-produced," and mark the deadweight loss
  • Explain why the optimal Pigouvian tax rate equals marginal external cost, and discuss uniform emission standards and the tax-neutral "double dividend" debate
  • State the conditions and the conclusion of the Coase theorem, and explain why transaction costs make "bargaining it out" fail for climate change
  • Use the logistic growth model to explain how fisheries collapse, and distinguish maximum sustainable yield from the open-access equilibrium
  • Reproduce the Hotelling rule and explain how price signals automatically pace the extraction of nonrenewable resources
  • Explain how the discount rate determines the social cost of carbon, distinguish mitigation from adaptation, and describe the free-rider problem in global cooperation
Prerequisites: 01 Microeconomics (supply & demand, surplus, market failure) 08 Public economics (taxation, public goods)

Chapter 1The Economic Nature of Environmental Problems: Externalities and Social Costs

Opening question: In December 1952, London was locked in dense smog for five days. Sulfur compounds from coal burning had nowhere to disperse, and later studies attributed more than ten thousand deaths to respiratory disease. Yet the factories and households burning the coal never paid a cent for those deaths — should they have? What did market prices leave out here?

The core intuition: Nobody grieves over a cost that never shows up on the bill

Imagine a restaurant allowed to dump its garbage free into the neighbor's yard: it will certainly "produce" too much garbage — not because the owner is wicked, but because dumping is free to it. Prices are society's information system: when part of the true cost (the neighbor's health, the fishery's stock, the climate of future generations) never appears on the decision maker's bill, rational people overuse the "free" part. That is an externality — not a moral failure, but a structural problem of missing prices.

Key concepts

Externality 外部性
The effect of one economic agent's actions on a bystander's well-being that is not settled through market prices. Negative externalities impose harm (pollution); positive ones confer benefits (vaccination).
Marginal External Cost 边际外部成本
The marginal damage imposed on outsiders by each extra unit of output. Social marginal cost = private marginal cost + marginal external cost.
Social Cost vs Private Cost 社会成本与私人成本
Private cost is what the decision maker bears; social cost is what society bears in total. The gap between the two is exactly the part the market leaves unpriced — which is why the market equilibrium for polluting goods systematically deviates from the social optimum.
The real world · From the London smog to blue skies over Beijing

The 1952 London smog led directly to Britain's Clean Air Act of 1956: households were barred from burning high-sulfur soft coal and power plants were moved out of the cities — administrative measures filling in for an absent price. Compare China: after the dense sequencing of the 2013 Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan (the "Ten-Point Air Plan") and the 2018 Blue Sky Protection Campaign, Beijing's annual average PM2.5 fell from 89.5 μg/m³ in 2013 to 30 μg/m³ in 2022, a drop of roughly two-thirds. The cleanup cost enormous sums, but the health gains (fewer respiratory diseases and premature deaths) were just as large — the essence of environmental policy is to put the MEC once advanced by lungs and hospitals back on the bills of the energy and industrial sectors.

Common pitfalls

"Zero pollution is the optimum." — Abatement consumes real scarce resources (engineers, catalysts, wind farms). The optimal pollution level sits where marginal damage = marginal abatement cost: squeezing pollution from 25 tons down to 0 means giving up output that consumers value above its social cost. Environmental capacity is itself an "asset" that has to be budgeted carefully. Another misconception: "Externalities show that firms are wicked." — Inside a structure of missing prices, law-abiding profit-maximizing firms will inevitably pollute too much; anyone else running them would do the same. The fix is to change the incentives, not merely to change the people.

Check yourself

Quiz 1.1In Figure 1-1, the unregulated market equilibrium quantity is 40 tons and the social optimum is 25 tons. Which statement is correct?
Explanation: Voluntary exchange guarantees maximal surplus only for the two parties to the trade; the victims (third parties) are never at the table. Between 25 and 40 tons, the demand price (100−Q) is below social marginal cost (50+Q), so those tons carry negative net social benefit. A confuses "voluntary" with "efficient"; C has the direction reversed — subsidies to expand output fit positive externalities (see Quiz 1.2).
Quiz 1.2In which of the following cases will the market quantity be "below" the social optimum?
Explanation: A positive externality = benefits spilling over to others. Decision makers weigh only private benefit, so "do-good" behavior is too rare — vaccination, education, R&D, and beekeeping are the classic examples. The corrective tool is a subsidy (a "negative Pigouvian tax"). A is a negative externality, with quantity too high; C is the efficiency benchmark, where the market quantity exactly equals the social optimum.

Chapter summary

  • Externality = a spillover not settled through the market; a negative externality makes the market quantity too high, a positive one too low.
  • SMC = PMC + MEC; the efficient quantity sits where demand meets SMC, not PMC.
  • The optimal level of pollution is not zero: the condition is marginal damage = marginal abatement cost.
  • Externalities are a structural problem of missing prices; the cure is to make decision makers face the true cost.

Chapter 2Pigouvian Taxes and Subsidies: Writing External Costs into Prices

Opening question: If a piece of the price is missing, the most direct fix is to restore it — issue a "bill" for every ton of pollution. In 1920 the British economist Pigou proposed taxing each unit of pollution by exactly the marginal damage it causes. Government need not know how each plant should abate; it only has to set the price right. Does this really work?

The core intuition: The tax is not a punishment; it is the bill mailed back

The essence of the Pigouvian tax is to internalize the costs that others have advanced on your behalf: for every ton you emit, you repay society at the marginal damage per ton. When the tax rate exactly equals the MEC at the optimal output, the firm's private cost curve is lifted bodily onto the SMC, and the market finds its own way to 25 tons — without anyone commanding a single firm. Firms with cheap abatement abate more (the tax saved is profit); firms with costly abatement abate less and pay more tax; at the margin, the cost of the last ton abated is equalized across all firms — that is the magic of "minimum total cost for a given total abatement." The government sets one number correctly, and knowledge dispersed across millions of firms is mobilized automatically by the price.

Key concepts

Pigouvian Tax 庇古税
A tax levied on each unit of external damage at a rate equal to the marginal external cost at the optimal output. It "mails" the external cost back to the decision maker, who now faces the true social cost.
Internalizing the Externality 内部化
Using taxes, subsidies, or property-rights arrangements to turn costs and benefits formerly borne externally into the decision maker's private costs and benefits. The common goal of every environmental policy instrument.
Double Dividend 双重红利
An environmental tax improves the environment (the first dividend); if the revenue is used to cut distortionary taxes such as the income tax, it also trims the tax system's efficiency losses (the second). The real-world debate: environmental taxes tend to be regressive and need compensating rebates.
The real world · Sweden's carbon tax and China's carbon market

Sweden introduced a carbon tax in 1991, starting around SEK 250/ton and ratcheting up to roughly SEK 1,200/ton (about USD 115, among the highest in the world). Over the following three decades, Sweden's real GDP grew a cumulative 80% or so while net greenhouse-gas emissions fell by about one-third, with visible decarbonization of district heating and transport — growth and abatement can run together — and the tax reform simultaneously lowered taxes on labor income: a real-world footnote to the "double dividend." China launched its national carbon emissions trading market in 2021 (covering, first, the power sector's 2,000-odd key emitters — about 4.5 billion tons of emissions a year, the largest coverage in the world), starting from free allowances and intensity-based control; the opening price was about 48 yuan/ton, and in 2024 it briefly broke 100 yuan/ton — a price signal strengthening by degrees, squarely Pigouvian in direction.

Common pitfalls

"The Pigouvian tax exists to raise revenue for the government, so the higher the rate the better." — Look at Figure 2-1: past 30 yuan the deadweight loss widens again. The Pigouvian objective is efficiency, not fiscal revenue; in theory the revenue of the optimal Pigouvian tax should be neutralized through tax cuts or lump-sum rebates, or it becomes an ordinary distortionary tax. Another common misunderstanding: "Just set the same emission cap for every plant — same effect, and simpler." — Uniform standards ignore the huge differences in abatement costs: letting low-cost firms abate more and high-cost firms less hits the same total for far less social cost (see Quiz 2.2), and only the tax keeps a standing profit incentive to "abate one more ton beyond the standard."

Check yourself

Quiz 2.1The optimal Pigouvian tax rate should equal?
Explanation: At a rate equal to MEC, the private cost facing firms coincides with social cost, and the equilibrium quantity automatically equals the social optimum. B forgets that abatement itself is costly — pushing quantity to zero sacrifices all output with positive net social benefit (drag to 40 in Figure 2-1 to see the new loss). C is Laffer-curve logic (see Track 08), unrelated to correcting the externality: the revenue-maximizing rate is usually far above the MEC.
Quiz 2.2Compared with "the same emission standard for every plant," the core efficiency advantage of the Pigouvian tax (or tradable quotas) is?
Explanation: With one tax rate, every firm abates until "marginal abatement cost = tax rate" — the last ton abated costs the same at every firm: the equimarginal principle of cost-effective abatement. A uniform standard forces high-cost and low-cost firms to abate equally much, wasting resources. The total is set only indirectly by the rate (A has it backwards: a standard fixes the total directly, a tax fixes the marginal incentive); both instruments require emissions monitoring (C is wrong). The tax's extra bonus: abating more than required keeps saving tax money, so the innovation incentive has no ceiling.

Chapter summary

  • The Pigouvian rate = marginal external cost at the optimal output; the aim is to correct prices, not to raise revenue.
  • With the rate set right, firms "voluntarily" reach the social optimum; overshooting creates a new deadweight loss.
  • A uniform price allocates abatement across firms cost-effectively and keeps innovation incentives alive.
  • Double dividend: environmental tax revenue used to cut other distortionary taxes does double duty (mind the regressive offset).

Chapter 3Property Rights and the Coase Theorem: Can Bargaining Replace the Tax?

Opening question: Pigou said "let the government set the price." In 1960, in "The Problem of Social Cost," Coase asked back: harm is reciprocal — shutting the factory down also "harms" the factory, doesn't it? If the law assigns the rights clearly, can the parties bargain the price out themselves, with no government-set tax rate at all?

The core intuition: Before punishing anyone, let the parties talk

The rancher's cattle wander into the farmer's wheat and graze. If the law says "cattle may graze at will," the farmer can pay the rancher to build a fence; if it says "the wheat field is inviolable," the rancher can pay for the "right to graze." As long as bargaining is costless, no matter who is assigned the right, the two sides converge on the same spot: the number of acres grazed is exactly where "the rancher's gain from one more head = the farmer's extra loss of wheat." That is the Coase theorem: property rights clearly assigned + zero transaction costs → efficient resource allocation; the initial assignment determines only who pays whom (distribution), not how the resource is used (efficiency). The value of this mirror is that it reflects reality: real-world bargaining involves thousands of parties, asymmetric information, and costly enforcement — transaction costs are the main battlefield of environmental problems.

Key concepts

Coase Theorem 科斯定理
When property rights are clearly assigned and transaction costs are zero, bargaining among the parties achieves an efficient allocation of resources regardless of how the rights are initially distributed; the initial distribution affects only the distribution of income. Coase won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1991.
Transaction Costs 交易成本
All the costs of bargaining, contracting, monitoring, and enforcement: finding parties, negotiating, gathering information, going to court. The larger the transaction costs, the less feasible "settling externalities by private bargain," and the more valuable institutions (taxes, regulation, courts).
Cap-and-Trade 总量管制与交易
Government sets a total cap on emissions, allocates or auctions the allowances to firms, and lets them trade — a "Coaseanized" Pigouvian tax: the cap is set by government, the price is bargained out by the market.
The real world · The U.S. Acid Rain Program: the most successful "Coasean" experiment

The 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments created tradable emission allowances for sulfur dioxide, the culprit behind acid rain: a hard cap declining year by year, allowances freely tradable among power plants, prices publicly quoted. The result: U.S. SO₂ emissions were halved over the 1990s and targets were met early, while actual abatement costs came to only about a third of ex-ante expert forecasts — because low-cost plants abated hard and sold their surplus allowances to high-cost plants, running exactly on the "equimarginal" logic of Figure 2-1. It became the global template (the EU carbon market, the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative RGGI, and the California–Quebec market all descend from it). The reverse lesson is just as valuable: in its first phase the EU ETS over-allocated allowances, and the price at one point fell to nearly zero — the quality of a property-rights instrument depends on cap setting and on the political economy of allocation.

Common pitfalls

"The Coase theorem proves government should step aside — privatize everything and be done." — That reads the theorem exactly backwards. Its assumptions (zero transaction costs, clearly identified parties, enforceability) are expensive and scarce in reality: courts, registries, monitoring, and enforcement are themselves public institutions. Climate change is a bargain of "all humanity (unborn generations included) against all humanity," with astronomical transaction costs; only taxes or caps can serve as the "industrial-scale stand-in for the Coasean bargain." Another misconception: "You can't privatize air, so the property-rights approach is useless." — You cannot privatize the "atmosphere," but you can define "emission rights" and let them circulate among firms; the carbon market is precisely such a property-rights arrangement.

Check yourself

Quiz 3.1With zero transaction costs and clearly defined property rights, the Coase theorem predicts: whether the law grants the farmer or the rancher the "right to be spared from cattle grazing the wheat," the number of acres of wheat grazed will?
Explanation: With zero transaction costs the parties bargain until no mutual gains remain — where "the rancher's marginal gain from the last head = the farmer's marginal wheat loss" — independent of the initial assignment. Assign the right to the farmer, and the rancher pays for the grazing; assign it to the rancher, and the farmer pays for a fence: same efficiency, different distribution. A confuses distribution with allocation; C denies the bargaining mechanism itself, yet bargaining is the theorem's core (it fails in reality because transaction costs are nonzero, not because the logic is wrong).
Quiz 3.2Why is Coasean private bargaining a poor fit for climate change?
Explanation: The Coasean framework fits settings with few parties, clear causation, and enforceable deals. Climate pulls 8 billion people, nearly two hundred countries, and generations not yet born into one "negotiation" — organization costs and free-riding incentives both tend toward infinity; that is the micro-foundation of the global-coordination problem in Chapter 6. A contradicts the scientific consensus; C is wrong: emission rights are perfectly definable (a carbon allowance is exactly such a defined right); the difficulty lies not in "definability" but in "the politics and enforcement after definition."

Chapter summary

  • Coase theorem: clear property rights + zero transaction costs → bargaining reaches efficiency; the initial rights govern only distribution.
  • The theorem's value is as a "benchmark mirror": it shows how large real transaction costs are, and how much public institutions matter.
  • Tradable quotas = government sets the quantity, the market finds the price — the confluence of Coasean ideas and Pigouvian tools.
  • The quota instrument's Achilles' heel: cap setting and allocation (over-allocation → price collapse, the EU ETS lesson).

Chapter 4The Tragedy of the Commons and Public Goods: Individual Rationality, Collective Ruin

Opening question: Every fisher knows overfishing ruins the fishery — so why does every boat keep adding nets? Hardin's 1968 "The Tragedy of the Commons" gave the cold answer: the tragedy needs no villains — a group of rational individuals is enough to destroy the resource they all live on.

The core intuition: The gain is mine, the cost is everyone's

If I send out one more boat, the extra catch is all mine; the drain on the stock is shared by every boat — I bear only a fraction of the loss. As long as "private gain > privately borne cost," every boat expands, until profit is fully diluted away. This is an N-player prisoner's dilemma: each choice on its own is rational; summed, it is collective ruin. It runs on the same logic as Chapter 1: under open access, the commons are simply everyone imposing an externality on everyone — except that this time nobody is a clean bystander.

Key concepts

Tragedy of the Commons 公地悲剧
The structural problem of open-access resources being overused to the point of degradation: benefits are private, costs are shared, and individually rational choices add up to collective ruin. Hardin, 1968.
Open Access 开放获取
A resource regime in which anyone may freely enter and use — the true root of the tragedy. Common-pool resources (fisheries, pastures, groundwater) can themselves be governed; the problem is the "absence of rules of exclusion."
Public Goods 公共品
Goods that are both non-excludable (you cannot keep others from using them) and non-rival (one person's use does not leave less for others): national defense, lighthouses, greenhouse-gas mitigation. Voluntary market provision is necessarily insufficient.
Free-Riding 搭便车
Enjoying the benefits of a public good without sharing its cost. The more people involved and the harder the monitoring, the more free-riding — the shared micro-mechanism behind undersupplied public goods and deadlocked climate talks.
The real world · Newfoundland cod: how a five-hundred-year fishery collapsed in five years

Newfoundland's Grand Banks cod had fed European fishers for five hundred years. Trawlers and sonar turned the hunt into industry in the 1950s and 60s; in the 1970s Canada's 200-mile zone pushed out the foreign fleets — only for domestic capacity to explode into the gap, with the government subsidizing shipbuilding to boot. In 1992 the stock collapsed, Canada declared a total moratorium, and some 30,000 fishers and processing workers were jobless overnight; the government has since paid out billions of Canadian dollars in compensation. Thirty years on, the cod have still not recovered commercially — a renewable resource, once past an ecological threshold, is for a time a "nonrenewable" one. Open access plus subsidy-boosted effort: a textbook tragedy-of-the-commons script.

Common pitfalls

"The tragedy is rooted in human greed, and there is no cure." — The attribution is wrong. The very same fishers fish sustainably for generations under quotas or community self-governance. Ostrom, the 2009 Nobel laureate in economics, studied common-pool resources — Swiss alpine pastures, Spanish irrigation communities, Japanese village forests — and found locals keeping commons clear of the tragedy for centuries with design principles such as "clear boundaries + locally made rules + graduated sanctions + low-cost conflict resolution." "Public ownership dooms a resource to tragedy; privatization is the only way out" — equally wrong: the tragedy comes from "open access," not from "public ownership"; state, private, and community regimes can all succeed and all fail. What matters is institutional detail.

Check yourself

Quiz 4.1The root of "individually rational, collectively irrational" in the tragedy of the commons is?
Explanation: This is a structural problem: under open-access incentives, anyone placed in that position expands — not expanding means ceding the resource to others to drain. C is the symptom ("many boats, few fish"), not the cause; even with many fish and few boats, the incentive structure unchanged, the tragedy replays. Ostrom's fieldwork showed: what changes is institutions, not human nature.
Quiz 4.2If public goods such as lighthouses and national defense relied entirely on voluntary market provision, supply would fall short, because?
Explanation: Non-excludable → you cannot keep non-payers outside the benefits → everyone's best strategy is to hide their true willingness to pay and wait for others to foot the bill (an N-player prisoner's dilemma) → voluntary provision falls below the efficient level. This has nothing to do with how high costs are (A) or whether demand exists (C) — the more public and the more vital the need, the less anyone wants to be the first to pay. The fixes: government provision funded by compulsory taxation, or technology and institutions that change excludability (e.g., encrypted satellite navigation signals).

Chapter summary

  • Tragedy of the commons = mutual externalities under open access: private benefits, shared costs; entry continues until profits dissipate.
  • The socially optimal number of boats is smaller than the open-access equilibrium number; the gap is the wasted resource rent.
  • Public goods are undersupplied by markets because of free-riding; taxation or institutional innovation is needed.
  • Ostrom: between state and private ownership lies successful community self-governance — institutional design decides outcomes.

Chapter 5Renewable and Nonrenewable Resources: The Fisheries Model and the Hotelling Rule

Opening question: Fish bear young; coal does not — that one sentence holds the two halves of resource economics. A fishery is principal that bears interest: harvest too much and you wound the principal, harvest too little and you waste the interest. A mine is a deposit that bears no interest: the only question is "sell this year, or hold until next." What are the two logics called?

The core intuition: Manage the resource stock as a portfolio asset

Renewable resources (fisheries, forests, groundwater): the larger the stock, the more nature adds, but growth rises and then falls (too small a stock lacks a breeding base; near carrying capacity, the fish crowd one another out), tracing a bell-shaped curve. Harvest is a straight line pulled up from the origin; where it crosses the bell curve sits the "sustainable equilibrium." More effort steepens the line and shrinks the equilibrium stock; past the bell's peak, more boats, fewer fish, and lower yield too; past the critical slope, the line pins the whole growth curve beneath it — the only equilibrium left is zero: collapse. Nonrenewable resources (oil, coal, minerals): the stock by nature neither grows nor shrinks; "keeping a barrel underground" is an investment that must match the return on bonds — in equilibrium the resource's net price (rent) rises at the interest rate: the Hotelling rule. It explains why doomsday forecasts keep missing: a rising price lights three lamps at once — exploration, substitution, and conservation.

g(x) = r·x·(1 − x/K)  →  MSY = rK/4 = 12.5; x*(E) = K·(1 − qE/r); Ecollapse = r/q Math box: the Schaefer model — with carrying capacity K = 100 thousand tons, intrinsic growth rate r = 0.5, and catchability q = 0.05, the critical effort is 10. The open-access equilibrium subtracts costs as well: entry continues until profit reaches zero, at a stock and a yield both below MSY.
(Pt+1 − c) = (1 + i)·(Pt − c) Math box: the Hotelling rule (1931) — the "rent" above extraction cost c must grow at the interest rate i, or no one would be willing to hold the resource for tomorrow. The long-run price path is capped jointly by the interest rate, the scarcity rent, and the cost of the backstop technology.

Key concepts

Maximum Sustainable Yield 最大可持续产量
The sustainable harvest at the top of the growth curve. Note: MSY is the biological optimum, not the economic one — once harvesting costs are counted, the economic optimum (MEY) sits at an effort below MSY.
Individual Transferable Quota 个体可转让配额
Divide the total allowable catch into quotas held by fishers and tradable: science sets the total, trading delivers the efficiency — fitting the fishery with the dual governance of "property rights + markets."
Hotelling Rule 霍特林规则
In competitive equilibrium the net price (rent) of a nonrenewable resource rises at the interest rate: holding the resource underground is an investment whose equilibrium return must equal the interest rate. Hotelling laid the foundation of resource economics in 1931.
The real world · The Yangtze's ten-year fishing ban and New Zealand's ITQs

The Yangtze was once "the cradle of freshwater fisheries"; relentless fishing and ecological decline slashed the four major Chinese carps and drove the baiji dolphin functionally extinct. From 2021, the river's key waters came under a ten-year fishing ban: more than 230,000 fishers left the harvest for new livelihoods. The 2022 survey counted the Yangtze finless porpoise back up to 1,249 (from 1,012 in 2017), a historic turnaround — only by banking the "stock principal" back in full does the growth curve regain the capital to climb. New Zealand was first to roll out ITQs nationwide, in 1986: the total allowable catch is set annually by scientific assessment and quotas trade freely; fishers turned from "chasing the fish" to "managing an appreciating fishing right," and the fishery went from chronic losses to sustainable profit — the global template for ITQs (Iceland, Australia, and others followed).

Common pitfalls

"MSY is the economic optimum for the fishery." — MSY looks only at biological growth and ignores harvest costs and fish prices. Draw the costs in, and the economic optimum (MEY) arrives at an effort below MSY: the fuel and depreciation spent chasing the last ton of "free growth" may not be worth it. The other extreme: "resource doomsaying versus tech-optimism — one side must be right." — The Limits to Growth (1972) forecast rapid mineral depletion; it missed because "reserves" are an economic concept: prices rise → more exploration, leaner extraction, cheaper substitutes (the Hotelling mechanism plus induced innovation). But renewable resources have thresholds and irreversibilities — Newfoundland cod proves the pessimists can be right for fisheries. Nonrenewables run on prices and substitutes; renewables fear open access — each side is half right.

Check yourself

Quiz 5.1In the Schaefer model, as effort rises beyond the MSY level, the equilibrium stock and the sustainable yield will?
Explanation: Sustainable yield = natural growth = g(x*), and x* = 100 − 10E declines linearly with effort. Past E = 5, the equilibrium slides down the left slope of the bell: smaller stock → less growth → less sustainable harvest. B confuses "this season's catch" with "sustainable yield" (mining the stock can pay wildly for a while, until there are no fish left); C would hold only if the growth curve turned flat, which it never does.
Quiz 5.2By the Hotelling rule, in competitive equilibrium the "net price (rent)" of a nonrenewable resource will in the long run?
Explanation: If the rent does not rise at the interest rate, the mine owner's best move is to sell everything today and buy bonds — the collective sell-off depresses today's price and tightens future scarcity, until "holding one more year" appreciates by exactly the interest rate. The same logic explains the long-run ceiling on resource prices: when the rent path pushes the price up to the cost of the backstop technology (solar-made hydrogen displacing oil, say), demand migrates and the path is capped. A ignores the opportunity cost of holding; C points opposite to scarcity.

Chapter summary

  • Renewable resources: a bell-shaped growth curve × a straight-line harvest; excessive effort → stock and yield fall together, to collapse (critical E = r/q).
  • MSY is the biological ceiling; the economic optimum MEY lies below it (harvest costs count); the open-access equilibrium is worse still.
  • ITQs solve the quantity problem and the efficiency problem at once with "a cap + tradable property rights."
  • Hotelling rule: the rent on a nonrenewable resource rises at the interest rate; the price signal steers exploration, conservation, and substitution.

Chapter 6The Economics of Climate Change: The Social Cost of Carbon and the Discount-Rate Dispute

Opening question: Every extra ton of coal burned today spreads its damage across the globe and the next few centuries: sea levels, crops, heat waves, migration. Setting the "right price" for that ton — the social cost of carbon — may be the most consequential calculation in the history of economics. And the answer hangs almost entirely on a parameter a few decimal places wide: the discount rate.

The core intuition: The discount rate is the exchange rate that converts the future into today

The social cost of carbon (SCC) is the sum of the present values of all the marginal damage this ton of CO₂ will cause over the coming centuries. The discount rate ρ is that exchange rate: at ρ = 3%, a 100-yuan disaster 100 years from now is worth only 5 yuan today; and the damages happen to concentrate in the far future — so the SCC is hyper-sensitive to ρ (roughly proportional to 1/ρ²). Stern (The Stern Review, 2006) argued from intergenerational ethics: near-zero pure time preference, ρ ≈ 1.4%, a high SCC → strong immediate mitigation; Nordhaus (the 2018 Nobel laureate) argued for discounting at the market return on capital, ρ ≈ 4–5%, a far lower SCC → gradual mitigation. The two are fighting not over a technical parameter but over the ethical question "how much do we owe the future" — economics compressed it into one Greek letter.

PV = D·e−ρt; at ρ = 2%, $1 in 100 years ≈ $0.135; at ρ = 5%, ≈ $0.007 Math box: damages accumulate roughly with time while discounting decays exponentially, so the present-value integral ∫t·e−ρtdt ∝ 1/ρ² — the source of "double the discount rate, quarter the SCC."

Key concepts

Social Cost of Carbon 碳社会成本
The present value of all the future marginal damages caused by emitting one more ton of CO₂ — the theoretical benchmark for the optimal carbon tax (or the reserve price in carbon-allowance auctions).
Social Discount Rate 社会贴现率
The rate at which future benefits and damages are converted into present value, built from pure time preference, consumption growth, and risk aversion (the Ramsey formula). Partly a market parameter, but even more an intergenerational ethical choice.
Mitigation vs Adaptation 减缓与适应
Mitigation cuts emissions or adds carbon sinks, reducing the generation of damage; adaptation builds levees and breeds drought-tolerant varieties, reducing the bearing of damage. The two are complements; for irreversible thresholds, adaptation cannot substitute for mitigation.
The real world · From the Paris Agreement to China's "dual carbon" goals

The 2015 Paris Agreement wrote "holding warming below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels, pursuing efforts toward 1.5 °C" into global goals, pushed along by the ratchet of "nationally determined contributions + stocktakes every five years that tighten them" — acknowledging free-riding and binding it instead with reciprocity and transparency. In 2020 China announced carbon peaking before 2030 and carbon neutrality before 2060; in 2023 China added about 290 GW of wind and solar capacity combined, more than 40% of the world's new builds, and its renewable capacity historically overtook coal-fired capacity. In the same period, the EU launched its Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), pinning the carbon price onto imports — the embryo of a climate club: those who mitigate together trade tariff-free, and free-riders pay.

Common pitfalls

"The discount rate is a technical detail — leave it to the models." — Look at Figure 6-1: it is not a detail; it is the switch that flips the conclusion. How much weight the future gets is an ethical judgment: the unborn hold no franchise in the market, and the "market interest rate" cannot vote for them. "Adaptation can replace mitigation." — A sea wall holds back 30 centimeters of sea-level rise, but not the destabilization of the West Antarctic ice sheet, an irreversible threshold: damage past a tipping point does not obey the logic of "pay and bear it," and the marginal cost of adaptation tends to infinity. "Let the others cut while I ride along — best for me." — In a one-country game that is indeed true, which is exactly what it means for climate to be a "global public good"; but if more than 190 countries all reason that way, it is Chapter 4's tragedy of the commons replayed at planetary scale — hence carbon clubs, CBAM, and the ratchet.

Check yourself

Quiz 6.1Other assumptions unchanged, the discount rate falls from 3% to 2%. The social cost of carbon (SCC) will?
Explanation: Climate damages land mostly decades to centuries out. A smaller ρ → the weight of far-future damages in today's terms is amplified exponentially. When the EPA switched from 3% to 2% in 2023, the official SCC jumped from about $51 to about $190/ton — same damage model, opposite policy conclusions. C inverts the causality: patience (a low ρ) is precisely what brings far-future damages into view, raising the SCC, not lowering it.
Quiz 6.2"Build levees and plant drought-tolerant crops to adapt, and we can skip paying for mitigation" — where does this go wrong?
Explanation: Adaptation lowers the bearing of damage; mitigation lowers its generation. For continuous, quantifiable damage (30 cm of sea-level rise) adaptation works; for irreversible thresholds (West Antarctic ice-sheet collapse, permafrost carbon release, species extinction) its marginal cost tends to infinity — money cannot buy back a tipping point. A is too absolute: many adaptive measures (early-warning systems) are highly cost-effective; C misses the logic: mature or not, "adaptation ≠ a substitute."

Chapter summary

  • SCC = the present value of all future damages from a marginal emission; the theoretical benchmark for the optimal carbon price.
  • The SCC is hyper-sensitive to the discount rate (≈ 1/ρ²): the Stern–Nordhaus dispute is an ethical fight in mathematical dress.
  • Mitigation reduces the generation of damage; adaptation reduces the bearing of it; for irreversible thresholds, adaptation cannot replace mitigation.
  • Climate is a global public good: free-riding → the tragedy of the commons at planetary scale; clubs, CBAM, and the ratchet are today's antidotes.

  • Tietenberg & Lewis, Environmental and Natural Resource Economics — the standard intermediate textbook, developing every topic in this track systematically; available in Chinese translation, and the first choice for exam preparation and going deeper.

  • Nicholas Stern, A Blueprint for a Safer Planet — the flagship statement of the low-discount-rate camp; read it for the other pole of the Stern–Nordhaus dispute.
  • Partha Dasgupta, The Economics of Biodiversity: The Dasgupta Review (2021) — the newest framework for bringing natural capital into national wealth accounting; the contemporary frontier of environmental economics.
