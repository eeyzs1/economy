Industrial Organization

产业组织

Microeconomics teaches us the polar worlds of perfect competition and monopoly, but real markets almost all live in between: a handful of airlines, two dominant food-delivery platforms, one WeChat. Industrial organization (IO) studies how real markets work, how firms compete and collude, and when government should step in — it is the bridge that carries microeconomic theory into antitrust policy.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • measure market power with the Lerner index and the HHI, and explain what information each rests on and where each falls short
  • predict oligopoly prices under Cournot and Bertrand competition, and explain how price converges to the competitive level as the number of firms grows
  • use the discount-factor logic of repeated games to explain why cartels are unstable and when tacit collusion can be sustained
  • apply the inverse-elasticity rule of third-degree price discrimination, and distinguish legal differential pricing from algorithmic price discrimination against loyal customers
  • analyze barriers to entry and strategic conduct such as predatory pricing, and understand the consumer welfare standard of antitrust
  • use network effects, critical mass, and two-sided markets to explain platform competition and "free" business models
Prerequisites: 01 Microeconomics (monopoly, producer surplus) 04 Game theory (Nash equilibrium, repeated games)

Chapter 1Market power: who can set prices, and who must take them

Opening question: The same bottle of water sells for 2 yuan at a convenience store and 10 yuan at a mountaintop scenic spot; Moutai can raise its price year after year, while a flour mill can only take the going price. The difference is not cost — it is who holds pricing power. The first task of industrial organization is to bolt a ruler onto "market power": how big is it, and where does it come from?

The core intuition: market power = the ability to charge a higher price without losing all your customers

A perfectly competitive firm that raises its price by one cent loses every customer — it is a "price taker." A firm with market power that raises its price loses only its price-sensitive customers, because what it sells lacks close substitutes. The sources of power are a mixed bag: a unique product, patents, licenses, economies of scale, network effects, switching costs... But measurement takes only two routes: look at behavior — how far price sits above marginal cost (the Lerner index) — or look at structure — how concentrated the market is (the HHI). The former is intuitive, but marginal cost is hard to observe; the latter needs only share data, but is merely a proxy for power.

Key concepts

Market power
市场势力
The ability to price above marginal cost without losing all of your customers. Rooted in the scarcity of substitutes; the common starting point of profit analysis and policy analysis alike.
Lerner index
勒纳指数
L = (P − MC)/P, ranging from 0 (perfect competition) to 1 (the extreme case). Under profit maximization it equals exactly 1/|ε|: the less elastic demand, the greater the power.
Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI)
赫芬达尔-赫希曼指数
The sum of the squared market shares (in percent) of all firms, ranging from 0 to 10,000. More sensitive to big firms: a pure monopolist scores 10,000, while an infinity of tiny firms approaches 0.
Markup
加成率
How far price sits above marginal cost, often written P/MC or (P − MC)/P. In empirical work, "rising markups" is shorthand for expanding market power.
HHI = s₁² + s₂² + … + sₙ²sᵢ is firm i's market share in percent. Four firms at 25% each → HHI = 2500; a single firm holding 100% → 10,000.
L = (P − MC) / P = 1 / |ε|The profit-maximizing markup rule: the smaller the elasticity |ε| (the harder customers are to lose), the higher the optimal markup. Under perfect competition |ε|→∞, so L→0.

The real world · Why Coca-Cola's bid for Huiyuan was blocked

In 2008 Coca-Cola offered about US$2.4 billion for Huiyuan Juice. In March 2009, China's Ministry of Commerce — invoking the Anti-Monopoly Law, then barely six months in force — prohibited the acquisition. The reasoning was pure concentration logic: the deal would significantly raise Coca-Cola's share of the juice market, and "the increase in concentration would have the effect of eliminating or restricting competition," with competition on the buyer side harmed as well, since it would also strengthen Coca-Cola's power over upstream fruit farmers. It was the first merger ever blocked under China's Anti-Monopoly Law, and a landmark moment for HHI-style analysis (shares plus the marginal change) entering Chinese regulatory practice.

Common pitfalls

"A high market share equals an illegal monopoly." Antitrust punishes the abuse of power, not power itself; a high share may simply be the reward for a better product. The reverse error is just as common: "a high HHI must mean high profits." If entry barriers are low, the profits of a concentrated market are instantly eroded by potential entrants (see the "contestable market" in Chapter 5). And the HHI depends entirely on how the relevant market is defined: the wider the market is drawn ("beverages" vs "100% juice"), the lower the HHI — market definition is often the real first battlefield of antitrust litigation.

Quiz 1.1A market has four firms with shares of 30%, 30%, 20%, and 20%. What are the HHI and its concentration band?
Explanation: HHI = 30²+30²+20²+20² = 2600. A confuses the concentration ratio (CR4, the sum of the top four = 100%) with the HHI (a sum of squares); squaring gives bigger firms more weight, which is why the HHI is far more sensitive to "one dominant firm" than to "evenly matched rivals." C gets the arithmetic right but the band wrong: 2600 > 2500, which is highly concentrated under the U.S. merger guidelines. For reference: four equal shares of 25% give an HHI of exactly 2500, right on the line.
Quiz 1.2A firm's profit-maximizing price corresponds to a demand elasticity of |ε| = 5. What is its Lerner index?
Explanation: Expanding the profit-maximizing first-order condition MR = MC gives (P − MC)/P = 1/|ε| = 1/5 = 0.2. A reverses the relationship between elasticity and power: the less customers can walk away (the smaller |ε|), the higher the markup; as |ε|→∞, L→0 — perfect competition. B is simply 1/5 miscomputed as 0.5. In empirical work, economists often run the ruler the other way: measure the markup, then back out the elasticity and the power.

Chapter summary

  • Market power = the ability to price above marginal cost without losing all your customers; its root is the scarcity of substitutes
  • The Lerner index L = (P − MC)/P = 1/|ε| measures power from the behavior side; the weakness is that marginal cost is hard to observe
  • The HHI = sum of squared shares (0–10,000) measures concentration from the structure side, weighting big firms more heavily
  • Both are only proxies: the HHI ignores entry barriers and L is hard to compute — antitrust needs the two to corroborate each other
  • Relevant market definition precedes all arithmetic: draw the market wide or narrow and the HHI changes beyond recognition

Chapter 2Oligopoly competition: Cournot and Bertrand

Opening question: Must a market with only two or three firms charge high prices? Airlines and cement have long sustained prices far above cost, yet the subsidy wars in ride-hailing and food delivery slashed prices below cost. Same oligopoly — why such different endings? The answer lies in the variable of competition: are firms fighting over "quantities" or over "price"?

The core intuition: choosing quantities is like dividing a pie; choosing prices is like fighting over it

Cournot competition: each firm simultaneously decides its quantity, and the market-clearing price follows. If you produce one more unit, you push down the price everyone faces — each firm accounts only for its own effect on price, so output lands below the competitive level but above monopoly. The more firms there are, the more each one's influence on price is diluted, and the equilibrium price slides all the way toward marginal cost. Bertrand competition: firms simultaneously post prices, and all customers flock to the cheapest one. Price a single cent above your rival and you sell nothing — so they undercut each other until price = marginal cost, even with only two firms. That is the "Bertrand paradox": two is enough to reproduce perfect competition. Reality sits in between: capacity constraints keep the "price war" from playing out fully (closer to Cournot), and product differentiation stops the low-price firm from taking every customer (weakening the paradox).

Key concepts

Cournot (quantity) competition
古诺竞争
An oligopoly model in which firms simultaneously choose quantities and the market clears at a price. With n identical firms, the equilibrium price lies between the monopoly price and marginal cost, and falls monotonically as n grows.
Bertrand (price) competition
伯特兰竞争
A model in which firms simultaneously post prices and consumers buy only from the cheapest. With a homogeneous good and identical marginal costs, two firms are enough to push price down to marginal cost.
Bertrand paradox
伯特兰悖论
The counterintuitive result that "two rivals suffice for the competitive outcome." Resolved in reality by three factors: capacity constraints, product differentiation, and repeated interaction.
Reaction functions (best responses)
反应函数
Your optimal choice given your rival's quantity (or price). The Nash equilibrium is the intersection of the two reaction curves — "my best response to you is exactly your best response to me."

The real world · The ride-hailing subsidy war: a Bertrand experiment

Around 2014, Didi and Kuaidi — later joined by Uber China — waged a textbook Bertrand price war over an identical mobility service: per-ride subsidies, double-sided red envelopes, free weekends. Prices stayed below cost for years, with cumulative "burn" measured in the tens of billions of yuan. Identical product plus zero switching costs meant customers simply flowed to whoever subsidized more, and no side dared stop first. The ending was equally classic: the price war could not turn a profit, Didi and Uber China merged in 2016, subsidies receded, and prices returned to a (Cournot-like) normal. Price wars start with Bertrand logic and often end with rising concentration — the "predatory subsidies" controversy that Chapter 5's antitrust discussion takes up.

Common pitfalls

"With only two or three firms, they must be colluding at high prices." Bertrand shows that with a homogeneous product, no capacity constraints, and one-shot interaction, two firms are enough to drive price down to cost. "Price wars are always great for consumers." True in the short run — but if the aim of the war is to drive rivals out of the market and pave the way for later price hikes (predatory pricing), the long run may tell a different story. Another technical pitfall: treating Cournot and Bertrand as the "right vs wrong" model — they are two polar institutions for the same market. Real-world capacity investment (capacity set first ≈ Cournot) and menu pricing (prices posted first ≈ Bertrand) each sit closer to one of them.

Quiz 2.1Two firms produce a perfectly homogeneous product, both with a marginal cost of 10 yuan. They set prices simultaneously in a one-shot game; the lower price takes the whole market (a tie splits it). What is the Nash equilibrium price?
Explanation: At P = 10, no one wants to deviate: raising the price loses every customer, and cutting it means selling below cost — the more you sell, the more you lose. A fails because pricing below cost is unprofitable; C describes collusion, which needs the scaffolding of a repeated game (Chapter 3) — in a one-shot game, "quietly cutting the price" is the dominant strategy. That is exactly the Bertrand paradox: two firms suffice to reproduce perfect competition.
Quiz 2.2In the symmetric Cournot model, what happens to the equilibrium price when the number of firms n rises from 2 to 10?
Explanation: P* = (a + n·c)/(n+1) decreases monotonically in n: n = 1 is the monopoly price, and as n→∞, P*→c. With a = 100 and c = 20, P ≈ 46.7 at n = 2 and P ≈ 27.3 at n = 10. The Cournot model thus delivers a continuous spectrum from monopoly to competition — "atomic competition" is not a qualitative break but a limit. A grafts the collusion intuition (Chapter 3) onto a noncooperative model; C ignores the structure of strategic interaction.

Chapter summary

  • Oligopoly outcomes hinge on the variable of competition: Cournot picks quantities (price between monopoly and competition), Bertrand picks prices (straight down to cost)
  • Bertrand paradox: homogeneous good + identical costs + one-shot game, and two firms already deliver P = MC
  • Three real-world factors dissolve the paradox: capacity constraints, product differentiation, repeated interaction
  • The Cournot price falls monotonically in the number of firms toward marginal cost: n is the dial of competitive intensity
  • Price wars (the ride-hailing case) start with Bertrand logic and often end with rising concentration

Chapter 3Collusion and cartels: why cheating is the norm

Opening question: When OPEC holds a meeting, global oil prices move on the news — the power of collusion is beyond doubt. Yet the historical record shows that cartel members almost always quietly overproduce, and the organization repeatedly hollows out into a shell. If collusion is so profitable, why do the people sitting around one table never quite see eye to eye?

The core intuition: collusion is a prisoner's dilemma that only "the future" can hold together

Swap the Cournot duopoly for a "joint monopoly": each of the two firms produces half the monopoly quantity, and both take home more profit than at the Cournot equilibrium — collusion pays. But given that your rival honors the deal, the extra units you quietly produce sell at the high price, so cheating is the dominant strategy. In a one-shot game the cartel necessarily collapses — the cartel's intrinsic instability. The only antidote is a repeated game: if cheating today brings retaliation tomorrow (everyone reverts to the low-price Cournot "punishment phase") and tomorrow matters enough (the discount factor δ is large enough), the short-run gain from cheating cannot outweigh the future loss, and cooperation sustains itself in the "shadow of the future" — with no written agreement at all. That is called tacit collusion.

Key concepts

Cartel
卡特尔
An explicit alliance of firms formed to restrict output and raise price. Per se illegal in most jurisdictions; cartels between states (OPEC) sit beyond the reach of any antitrust law.
Tacit collusion
默契合谋
Coordinated price increases without an agreement, resting on mutual understanding. Extremely hard to establish in court and to police — the frontier problem of antitrust.
Trigger strategies (grim trigger)
触发策略
A punishment scheme in repeated games: cooperate as long as no one deviates; once someone deviates, revert forever to the low-profit punishment equilibrium (such as Cournot).
Discount factor δ
贴现因子
What one yuan tomorrow is worth today (between 0 and 1). A larger δ means the future matters more and players are more patient, making collusion easier to sustain.
δ ≥ (πdev − πcoop) / (πdev − πN)The condition for collusion to be sustainable: πcoop = the profit from honoring the agreement, πdev = the one-shot profit from deviating, πN = the punishment-phase profit (e.g., Cournot), and δ = the discount factor. The greater the temptation to cheat, the more patience it takes.
The real world · OPEC and two price wars

In November 2014, OPEC refused to cut production to defend the price and let oil fall from about US$100 to below US$30 in early 2016 — widely read as punitive overproduction aimed at North American shale: squeezing higher-cost supply out of the market with low prices, a state-club version of the trigger strategy. In March 2020, after production-cut talks collapsed, Saudi Arabia slashed its official selling prices and announced higher output; oil crashed in a single day in what became known as the Saudi–Russia price war. Meanwhile, once a transnational cartel enters the courtroom, the price is steep: the international vitamins cartel of the 1990s (Roche, BASF, and others) drew fines of several billion dollars in Europe and the United States, and several executives went to jail — an explicit cartel earns monopoly profits and wagers prison time and fines against them.

Common pitfalls

"Collusion requires a signed agreement and a smoke-filled room." Repeated games can make tacit understanding an equilibrium: price matching, most-favored-customer clauses, and public announcements of future output can all serve as coordinating devices — which is exactly why tacit collusion is such an enforcement headache. "If prices rise, there must be a cartel." Cost shocks (crude oil, chip shortages) push prices up too; what identifies collusion is behavioral evidence — prices marching upward in lockstep while costs stand still, output caps in peak season — not prices alone. And the converse error, "once formed, a cartel earns steadily," is also wrong: internal incentives to cheat, new entrants outside, and demand fluctuations — three forces tearing at it at any moment.

Quiz 3.1In which of these market environments is tacit collusion hardest to sustain?
Explanation: Trigger strategies presuppose observation: punishment is possible only if "lost business" can be attributed to cheating. In B, noise buries the cheating signal, the punishment mechanism fails, and collusion is most likely to unravel — the theoretical explanation for why price wars are more frequent in booms. A and C are stabilizers of collusion: few rivals, transparency, and a high δ make the condition δ ≥ (πdev−πcoop)/(πdev−πN) easier to satisfy.
Quiz 3.2What is the economic root of a cartel's "intrinsic instability"?
Explanation: Inside a cartel, P > MC means each extra unit sold is nearly pure profit: the prisoner's-dilemma structure πdev > πcoop > πN is the fatal flaw — even with no enforcement at all, OPEC members have repeatedly overproduced. A is small-change friction; C is an external constraint that simply does not apply to a cartel between states (OPEC), so it can hardly explain "intrinsic" instability. Add outside entrants nibbling away the excess profits, and the cartel is beset from within and without.

Chapter summary

  • Collusion earns more than the Cournot equilibrium, but in a one-shot game cheating is the dominant strategy — a cartel is a prisoner's dilemma writ large
  • Repeated games with trigger strategies can sustain cooperation when δ ≥ (πdev−πcoop)/(πdev−πN)
  • Stabilizers of collusion: few firms, transparent behavior, stable demand, high δ; destabilizers: noise, secret discounts, entry
  • Tacit collusion needs no agreement and is extremely hard to prove in court — the frontier problem of antitrust
  • The OPEC record shows both faces: pumping more can punish outsiders (squeezing out shale), while member overproduction dissolves the cartel from within

Chapter 4Price discrimination: different money for the same bottle of water

Opening question: Same flight, adjacent seats, same meal — and the fares can differ by a factor of two. Software comes in a "Home edition" and a "Pro edition," with the latter five times pricier for features you will never use. A student movie ticket runs at nearly half the adult price. How do firms size up each customer and charge accordingly — and is this good or bad for consumers?

The core intuition: a uniform price leaves consumers' money "on the table"

Under uniform pricing, a customer willing to pay 500 yuan pays only 300 — 200 yuan of consumer surplus stays on the table. The firm's dream is to charge each person individually at their willingness to pay: first-degree price discrimination (perfect discrimination) captures all the surplus yet still delivers the efficient level of output. With limited information, the firm settles for less — second-degree relies on a self-selecting menu (quantity discounts, versioning) that lets customers reveal their own cards, while third-degree prices by observable group traits (students, seniors, regions). The pricing rule for third-degree discrimination is remarkably clean: charge the less elastic group a high price and the more elastic group a low price — business travelers racing the clock have no alternative (inelastic) and pay the full fare, while students with time on their hands shop around (elastic) and buy the discount ticket.

Key concepts

Price discrimination
价格歧视
Charging different customers different prices for the same good (with the differences not driven by cost). Preconditions: market power + the ability to distinguish willingness to pay + the ability to block resale.
Willingness to pay (WTP)
支付意愿
The maximum amount a customer would pay for the good. The whole art of discrimination is to move price as close as possible to each person's WTP.
Versioning
版本划分
The self-selection menu of second-degree discrimination: deliberately building "premium/stripped-down" versions (software, cabin classes) so that high-WTP customers pay for the expensive version by their own choice.
Personalized pricing
个性化定价
Quoting a price per person based on their user profile — known in China as "big-data price discrimination against loyal customers." The information-age approximation of first-degree discrimination, and a focus of regulation.

The real world · From revenue management to regulating big-data price discrimination

Airlines are the textbook case of price discrimination: a single flight is sliced into dozens of fare buckets by booking time, change conditions, and channel, and "revenue management" systems solve this figure's markup formula in real time — leisure travelers departing at midnight and booking two months ahead (elastic) get the low fares, while business travelers who must fly now (inelastic) pay full price. The digital age slices harder: same platform, same product, and longtime users see higher prices — algorithmic price discrimination against loyal customers, the practice known in China as "big-data shashou." Article 24 of China's Personal Information Protection Law, in force since 2021, explicitly prohibits "unreasonable differential treatment on transaction prices and other trading conditions," after Shenzhen and other cities had already legislated. The regulatory keyword is precisely "unreasonable": cost-based price differences (dearer express delivery to remote areas) are legal, while algorithmic pricing that bets on "you can't be bothered to comparison-shop" is banned.

Common pitfalls

"Price discrimination always makes consumers worse off." Not necessarily: third-degree discrimination may bring small markets that a uniformly high price would leave unserved (students, small language communities) into supply, and under first-degree discrimination total output equals the competitive level with zero deadweight loss (only the surplus changes pockets). Judge welfare by the output effect, not by moral reflex. The reverse error: "any price difference counts as discrimination." Pricing that reflects cost differences (peak-load electricity prices, long-haul freight) is not discrimination; genuine discrimination requires that the price gap does not come from a cost gap. And the enforcement precondition is often forgotten: if resale were easy, student tickets would be scalped until the arbitrage vanished — which is why discrimination concentrates in services and experiences (you must board the plane yourself) rather than in resellable goods.

Quiz 4.1A cinema charges students half price and adults full price. Under the inverse-elasticity rule, what is the logic?
Explanation: (P − MC)/P = 1/|ε| holds for each market segment separately: a large |ε_students| → a low optimal markup → a low price; a small |ε_adults| → a high price. This is neither charity (A) nor a cost difference (C — a seat in the same screening costs the same for everyone): it screens out the price-sensitive and serves them cheaply while preserving the high price for the insensitive. The preconditions: verifiable student IDs and non-transferable tickets.
Quiz 4.2Under first-degree (perfect) price discrimination, what happens to output and the division of surplus?
Explanation: Each transaction is priced at the buyer's WTP, and anyone with WTP ≥ MC buys — every mutually beneficial trade happens, output lands exactly where the demand curve meets MC, and deadweight loss vanishes. But each price grazes the buyer's WTP, so consumer surplus is squeezed dry and falls entirely to the firm. A perfect illustration of why efficiency and distribution must be judged separately: first-degree discrimination has efficiency going for it and distribution against it. A describes ordinary monopoly; C conjures something from nothing — trades above WTP never happen.

Chapter summary

  • Three preconditions for price discrimination: market power + distinguishable WTP + blocked resale
  • First-degree prices person by person (all surplus to the firm, efficient output); second-degree relies on a self-selection menu (versioning); third-degree splits the market by group
  • The core formula of third-degree discrimination: (Pᵢ − MC)/Pᵢ = 1/|εᵢ| — the less elastic pay more
  • Judge welfare by the output effect: discrimination may expand what is served, or may merely transfer surplus
  • The regulatory line is "reasonableness": cost-based price differences are legal, algorithmic price discrimination against loyal customers is banned by the Personal Information Protection Law

Chapter 5Entry barriers and antitrust

Opening question: Ride-hailing platforms once burned enormous subsidies to "lose money for two years, then profit for ten" — cook the rivals to death first, raise prices afterward. Does that story hold up economically? And why is it so hard to secure a conviction in court? The deeper question: when one firm occupies most of a market, should the regulator punish its bigness, or police only the harm it does to competition?

The core intuition: monopoly profit is a magnet, and barriers are the fence

Excess profit attracts sharks the way blood does — new firms smell it and rush in, output expands, and prices fall back. So what determines whether market power can persist is not today's market share but the height of the barriers to entry. Some barriers are born (structural): scale economies spreading fixed costs, patent protection, self-reinforcing network effects, users' switching costs. Others are deliberate (strategic): limit pricing (holding price down so that entry is unprofitable), threats of excess capacity, exclusive tying, and predatory pricing (sell at a loss to drive rivals out, then earn it back with interest under monopoly). The whole art of antitrust is walking the tightrope between "protecting competition" and "punishing success" — the modern mainstream applies the consumer welfare standard: ask whether conduct raised prices, reduced output, or stifled innovation, not simply whether the firm is big.

Key concepts

Barriers to entry
进入壁垒
Factors that let an incumbent keep price above cost indefinitely without inducing entry. Structural: scale economies, patents, network effects, switching costs; strategic: limit pricing, exclusive dealing.
Sunk costs
沉没成本
Expenditures that cannot be recovered once inside (specialized equipment, brand advertising). The larger the sunk component, the more cautious the entry — which incumbents can exploit to deter.
Predatory pricing
掠夺性定价
A strategy of pricing below cost to drive rivals out, then recouping the losses through monopoly pricing. Theoretically coherent; legally it requires proof of "below cost + a credible prospect of ex post recoupment."
Contestable market
可竞争市场
When entry and exit involve no sunk costs, even a sole monopolist dare not price high — potential competition substitutes for actual competition. A rebuke to static thinking that stares only at concentration.
The real world · "Pick one of two" and the platform antitrust storm

In April 2021, China's State Administration for Market Regulation found that Alibaba had abused its dominant market position by forcing merchants on its platform to "pick one of two" (they could not open stores on rival platforms at the same time), and fined it 18.228 billion yuan; that October, Meituan was fined 3.442 billion yuan for the same conduct. The target of the penalties was not "the platforms are big" but exclusionary behavior: forced exclusivity both raised merchants' operating costs and blunted rival platforms' ability to attract supply — a textbook strategic barrier, wounding the competitive process itself. The same campaign banned big-data price discrimination against loyal customers, the blocking of outside links, and more. These cases marked China's shift in platform-economy regulation from "tolerant and prudent" to "normalized regulation," with an analytical framework aligned with international practice: define the relevant market → establish dominance → examine whether the conduct eliminates or restricts competition.

Common pitfalls

"Big companies should be broken up." Antitrust protects competition, not competitors — still less does it punish excellence: if a high share comes from lower costs and better products, breaking the firm up only hurts consumers. "Price cuts are always a good thing." If the low price is predatory (below cost plus recoupment later), today's bargain is the bait for tomorrow's monopoly. But don't swing to the other extreme and cry predation at every low price: the law requires proof that the price falls below an appropriate cost benchmark and that the predator has the capacity to recoup its "investment" — the vast majority of price cuts are simply efficiency. "Subsidized losses equal predation." The recoupment link is still missing: if the market is freely enterable, the day prices rise is the day a new rival walks through the door, and the predation never pays back.

Quiz 5.1Which combination of evidence is most critical for legally distinguishing predatory pricing from legitimate price cuts?
Explanation: Pricing below cost shows this is an investment, not competition (nobody sells at a loss for fun); a credible recoupment prospect shows it is a strategy, not suicide (the Areeda–Turner cost test plus ex post recoupment analysis). Neither leg can be dropped: looking only at low prices misreads efficiency competition as predation, and looking only at injured rivals degenerates into protecting competitors rather than competition. A and C are emotional evidence, not economic logic.
Quiz 5.2What is the most important determinant of whether a firm's market power can persist over the long run?
Explanation: Monopoly profit is the loudest advertisement there is: as long as entry is free, excess profits are bound to be eroded by new capacity — contestable-market theory even shows that the mere threat of entry can hold prices down. Antitrust review therefore asks first "can rivals get in?" (barriers, sunk costs, exclusive contracts) and only then looks at share. A forgets that share may itself be the fruit of efficiency, and can be eroded at any moment; C is the naivest form of inductive fallacy.

Chapter summary

  • Monopoly profit attracts entry: barriers to entry (structural + strategic) determine whether power persists
  • The higher the sunk costs, the more cautious the entry — incumbents can exploit this to deter (limit pricing, excess capacity, exclusive dealing)
  • Establishing predation = below cost + a credible recoupment prospect; drop either leg and you get a false verdict
  • Modern antitrust mainstream applies the consumer welfare standard: protect competition, not competitors, and certainly do not punish scale
  • The "pick one of two" cases: what was punished was exclusionary conduct as a strategic barrier, not platform size itself

Chapter 6Network effects and platform economics

Opening question: Even if a messaging app twice as good launched tomorrow, you most likely would not switch — because all your friends are on WeChat. Why do some markets tip into winner-take-all while others sustain a crowd of giants? And why are platforms forever free to users, heavy on commissions to merchants, and sometimes even paying users to climb aboard?

The core intuition: the users themselves become part of the product's value

Ordinary goods get their value from the production side; network goods get their value from other users — the more people on WeChat, the more useful WeChat is to you. This is economies of scale on the demand side (network effects), and it brings three consequences. First, critical mass: below a threshold user base nobody wants to join (a negative-feedback death spiral), while past the tipping point growth reinforces itself (positive feedback). Second, a winner-take-all tilt: the leader siphons away users while laggards bleed faster and faster. Third, lock-in: switching costs pile up with your social graph, your data, and your habits. A platform is in the two-sided market business: the more riders, the keener drivers are to join; the more drivers, the faster your ride arrives — each side attracts the other. Pricing can therefore be "non-neutral": free or even subsidized on the side that is more elastic and contributes more to the network (users), and charged on the less elastic, more dependent side (merchants, drivers). Free is not charity — on the user side, the platform is simply "buying its inventory."

Key concepts

Network effects
网络效应
The utility a user derives from the product rises with the total number of users. Direct effects (social networks) and cross-side effects (each side of a platform attracting the other, like buyers and sellers).
Two-sided (platform) market
双边市场
A platform joining two groups of users, where the size of one side affects the value of the other. Optimal pricing looks at total two-sided profit, hence the familiar "subsidize one side, charge the other."
Critical mass
临界质量
The user base at which the net payoff to joining turns from negative to positive. Below it the network withers; above it growth drives itself — the solution to the cold-start problem is to burn cash buying critical mass first.
Winner-take-all
赢家通吃
The extreme outcome when network effects are strong, multi-homing is limited, and there is no room for differentiation: one firm takes the whole market. Multi-homing (installing two apps at once) and technology generational shifts weaken it.

Interactive demo: critical mass

The real world · The red-envelope wars: buying "critical mass" with cash

Over the 2014 Spring Festival, WeChat's "grab the red envelope" game got millions of users to bind their bank cards overnight — Jack Ma called it "the Pearl Harbor attack." Alipay's counterpunch was just as blunt: at the 2016 CCTV Spring Festival Gala it launched "Collect the Five Blessings," with everyone completing the set splitting a red-envelope pool of over 200 million yuan, and the campaign has recruited new users every year since. It looks like a marketing war; underneath it is the critical-mass formula at work. Payments are a classic two-sided network (payer ↔ payee): subsidies (cash red envelopes) temporarily raise v, pushing the net-payoff line past zero and the user base past n*; the positive feedback of offline QR-code payment scenarios then takes the baton. Today both platforms charge individuals nothing for transfers but take commissions on merchant receipts — textbook two-sided pricing: subsidize the elastic side, charge the dependent side.

Common pitfalls

"Free = no revenue = no market power." A zero price does not mean zero power over price: when users "pay" with attention, data, and their social graphs, the free side is precisely the platform's supply channel; exclusionary conduct aimed at the free side (blocking outside links, restricting sharing) can also harm competition. "Network effects necessarily mean winner-take-all." That holds only when network effects are strong, users find multi-homing hard (using several platforms at once), and products are homogeneous. Otherwise Instagram and TikTok coexist, and Didi and Amap's ride-hailing share the same frame — multi-homing, niche scenarios, and generational technology shifts (from text to short video) can all sustain several strong players. When analyzing a platform market, first map out clearly "how many sides, and who subsidizes whom."

Quiz 6.1A ride-hailing platform showers new riders with large subsidies while taking a commission from drivers. What is the two-sided-market explanation?
Explanation: Two-sided pricing looks at total profit, not one side at a time: more riders → faster pickups → drivers keener to join → more cars → more riders (cross-side network effects). Charging little or even subsidizing the price-sensitive, high-contribution side can lever the whole loop into motion (the "non-neutrality" result of Rochet and Tirole). A mistakes strategy for charity; C reverses the logic — the commission presupposes drivers' dependence (low elasticity) rather than aiming to shrink their numbers.
Quiz 6.2A social product's user base is currently below critical mass. Where will market feedback push it?
Explanation: Below critical mass, U(n) < 0: joining loses money and so does staying, and negative feedback dominates — this is why the overwhelming majority of social products die in the "cold start," and it is also the only rationale for cash-burning subsidies (temporarily raising v): push the base past n* and let positive feedback take the baton. A applies the after-the-tipping-point logic to before it; C's steady state does not exist — both ends are leaking funnels, and what does not advance retreats.

Chapter summary

  • Network effects = economies of scale on the demand side: the more users, the better the product — value is produced jointly by the users
  • Critical mass n* = (p − v)/k: offline it is the line between life and death; online it is where the subsidy war is headed
  • Two-sided-market pricing is non-neutral: subsidize the elastic, high-contribution side, charge the dependent side
  • Winner-take-all requires strong network effects + limited multi-homing + homogeneous products; multi-homing and technology generational shifts can break it
  • Free does not mean powerless: attention and data are implicit prices, and exclusionary conduct on the free side faces antitrust scrutiny too

Industrial organization is the hub of applied microeconomics: it builds on 01 Microeconomics (monopoly and surplus analysis) and 04 Game Theory and Information Economics (the Nash equilibrium and repeated-game tools used again and again in this track); it then leads back to 15 Behavioral Economics (how bounded rationality rewrites the analysis of markups and collusion), with a natural detour to 11 Development and Innovation Economics (the long-run interplay of innovation, patents, and competition).   • Jean Tirole, The Theory of Industrial Organization — the field's bible and the Nobel laureate's grand assembly of the framework; mathematically demanding, best taken as an advanced step.
