Track 03 · Core theory

Econometrics

计量经济学

Economic theory tells us “what should happen”; econometrics tells us “what actually happened in the data” — and how far we can go in saying “this is because…”. It is economics’ sharpest tool, and also its easiest to misuse.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • Distinguish correlation from causation, and spot confounding variables, reverse causality, and selection bias
  • Explain what OLS regression is actually doing, and read the coefficients, standard errors, and R² in a regression table
  • Explain what control variables accomplish — and the danger of “bad controls”
  • Interpret p-values and confidence intervals correctly, and dodge the common statistical traps
  • Describe the logic of four causal-identification strategies: randomized experiments, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, and instrumental variables
Prerequisites: 01 Microeconomics (comfort with scatter plots and functions is enough) No prior statistics needed — every concept required is introduced along the way

Chapter 1Correlation Is Not Causation

Opening question: A study published in a leading medical journal found that a country’s per-capita chocolate consumption is strongly positively correlated (r ≈ 0.79) with its number of Nobel laureates per ten million people. The Swiss eat the most chocolate and collect the most Nobel Prizes. Question: does eating more chocolate raise your odds of winning one?

The core intuition

When two variables move together (correlation), three stories are possible: A causes B; B causes A; or a third factor C causes both A and B. Chocolate and Nobel Prizes are the third kind: national wealth C — rich countries can afford chocolate, and they can afford scientific research. This “C hiding in the background” is what econometrics calls a confounding variable. The entire problem of causal inference boils down to one sentence: the data we observe are missing the comparison “what would Y have been if X had not happened?”

Key concepts

Correlation coefficient 相关系数
Measures how much two variables move together, on a scale from −1 to +1. It describes co-movement only — it promises nothing about which variable causes which.
Confounder 混杂变量
A third variable that influences both the suspected cause and the outcome (national wealth in the example above) — obstacle number one standing between correlation and causation.
Counterfactual 反事实
“If you had not gone to college, what would you be earning today?” That state, the one that never happened, is the counterfactual. Causal effect = actual outcome − counterfactual outcome. The trouble: the counterfactual can never be observed directly.
Simpson’s paradox 辛普森悖论
The phenomenon in which the direction of an association reverses between the pooled view and the within-group view — a confounder flipping the conclusion.
The real world

A genuine study: Messerli (2012, New England Journal of Medicine) reported the chocolate–Nobel correlation, and the author himself stressed that it was correlation, not causation. Classic cousins: “ice-cream sales rise in step with drownings” (confounder: summer) and “more firefighters, more fire damage” (reverse causality: more fires, so more firefighters sent).

Common pitfalls

“The correlation is so high (0.79!) that causality is nearly certain.” — Wrong. The strength of a correlation is irrelevant to whether it is causal: a confounder can push the correlation very high, while a true causal relationship can look faint because of measurement error. Causality is settled by research design (Chapter 6), not by the size of a coefficient.

Quiz 1.1Which of the following most plausibly explains the strong positive correlation between per-capita chocolate consumption and the number of Nobel laureates?
Explanation: Wealth C causes both A (chocolate is affordable) and B (research spending is ample), which explains why the two move together. A is the causal reading the author explicitly rejected; C is reverse causality and strains logic. Choosing B is confounder thinking.
Quiz 1.2“People who drink coffee every day have lower mortality.” To push this from correlation toward causation, the most urgent question to ask is?
Explanation: Sample size and significance settle only whether the correlation is real, not whether it is causal. Coffee drinkers may be younger, richer, and more likely to see a doctor — these systematic differences are the true threat to a causal conclusion. Spotting and interrogating confounders is habit number one of econometrics.

Chapter summary

  • A correlation has three explanations: A→B, B→A, or C→A together with C→B (confounding).
  • Causal effect = actual − counterfactual; the counterfactual cannot be observed, and that is the root of every difficulty.
  • Simpson’s paradox is the reminder that pooled data can fully mask — or even reverse — the true relationship.

Chapter 2Regression: Drawing the Line

Opening question: We have starting salaries and GPAs for 8 graduates, with the points scattered messily across a plot. “For each extra GPA point, how much higher is the starting salary?” We need a straight line through the scatter — and infinitely many lines pass through it. Which one is best?

The core intuition

Score every candidate line: for each point, measure its vertical distance to the line (the residual), square it, and add the squares up (squaring counts overshoots and undershoots as errors alike, and punishes large misses hard). The line with the lowest score is the one ordinary least squares (OLS) selects. Try it with your own hands below: before pressing the “optimal solution” button, can you get the sum of squared residuals close to its minimum?

Key concepts

Dependent and independent variables 因变量与自变量
The outcome being explained (starting salary Y) and the variable doing the explaining (GPA X). Notation: Y = β₀ + β₁X.
Regression coefficient 回归系数
β₁ is the slope: when X rises by 1 unit, Y changes by β₁ units on average. β₀ is the intercept: the baseline level when X = 0.
Residual 残差
The gap between an actual value and the line’s prediction. OLS has exactly one goal: make the sum of squared residuals as small as possible.
R² (goodness of fit) R²（拟合优度）
The fraction of the variation in Y that the line accounts for, running from 0 to 1. A value of 0.49 means “about half is explained.” Caution: a high R² implies neither causality nor a correct model.
Salarŷi = β̂0 + β̂1 · GPAi Read as: the fitted value of starting salary = intercept + slope × GPA. A hat over β marks a value “estimated from the sample.”
Common pitfalls

“The higher the R², the better the model.” — Not necessarily. Adding a pure noise variable barely lowers R²; among time series, two variables that each trend upward (GDP and local rainfall, say) will comfortably produce R² > 0.9. R² measures how well the line fits — not whether it is right, and certainly not causality.

Quiz 2.1By which criterion does OLS choose the line?
Explanation: OLS = Ordinary Least Squares: the estimate that minimizes the sum of squared residuals (SSE). Squaring stops positive and negative deviations from canceling out, and it punishes large outliers heavily.
Quiz 2.2A regression returns β̂1 = 0.8 (thousand yuan per GPA point). Which reading is correct?
Explanation: A regression coefficient describes only the average association in the sample. Whether “causes” is defensible depends on research design (Chapters 5 and 6); and even then it holds only “on average.” Cultivating the habit of saying “average association” is the first line of defense against being fooled by regression tables.

Chapter summary

  • OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals; the slope reads as “when X rises 1 unit, Y changes by β₁ on average.”
  • R² measures fit — not correctness, and not causality.
  • A coefficient licenses only “average association”; causal language requires extra conditions.

Chapter 3Control Variables: The Idea of Multiple Regression

Opening question: Ice-cream sales and drownings are strongly correlated, and the confounder is “season.” What happens to the coefficient on ice-cream sales if we add a season variable to the regression (summer = 1, winter = 0)? It collapses — it may vanish altogether. That is the power of control variables: comparing under “other things equal.”

The core intuition

In the multiple regression y = β₀ + β₁x + β₂z, β₁ answers this question: between two people whose z is exactly the same, how much does y differ on average for each extra unit of x? Put vividly, regression “first slices the data by z, compares the effect of x within each slice, then pools the slice-by-slice conclusions with weights.” Chapter 1’s Simpson’s paradox is repaired precisely by “controlling for school type.”

Key concepts

Multiple regression 多元回归
A regression that includes several explanatory variables at once. Each coefficient reports that variable’s partial effect “holding the other variables constant.”
Omitted-variable bias (OVB) 遗漏变量偏差
Leave out a variable that both affects y and is correlated with x, and the coefficient on x gets “contaminated.” The direction of the contamination is the product of the signs of those two correlations.
Bad control 坏控制
Controlling for a variable that sits in the middle of the causal chain absorbs the very effect you want to estimate. Example: controlling for “income” when estimating the return to education — income is the outcome itself.
The real world

To estimate whether attending college raises income, you must control for family background (parents’ income, hukou — household-registration status) — otherwise the confounding of ability and background will overstate the return to education. But control for “the industry of the first job after graduation” and you may have controlled too much: industry is a channel through which education affects income, and pinning it down cuts one link out of the causal chain. Choosing controls is design work — “the more the better” is not a principle.

Common pitfalls

“More controls mean more rigor.” — Wrong. Two risks: (1) a bad control absorbs the true effect; (2) the control is itself a “post-treatment” variable (affected by x), importing fresh bias. The principle: control for background variables that were determined before x, and regard with suspicion anything that might be “a consequence of x.”

Quiz 3.1Estimating a test-prep course’s effect on scores: strong students are more likely to enroll. Simply comparing enrollees with non-enrollees will?
Explanation: The omitted variable “student baseline” is positively correlated with enrolling and positively correlated with scores, so the OVB direction = (+)×(+) = overstatement. No sample size rescues systematic bias — a huge sample merely estimates the wrong answer more precisely.
Quiz 3.2Which variable is the most suitable control when estimating the effect of education on income?
Explanation: Parental education predates the respondent’s own schooling, making it a clean background control. Job rank and choice of major both sit on the causal path from education to income (education shapes major and job rank, which then shape income); controlling for them “controls away” part of education’s true effect — the classic bad control.

Chapter summary

  • A multiple-regression coefficient is a partial effect “holding other variables constant.”
  • Omitting a correlated confounder → bias; its direction is the product of the two correlations’ signs.
  • Good controls precede x in causal time; variables on the causal chain are bad controls.

Chapter 4Uncertainty and Significance

Opening question: In a class of 30 students you estimate that “each hour of tutoring raises scores by 1.2 points.” Re-estimate in another class and you get 0.3. Which estimate deserves belief? Or neither? We are missing a language for answering: how unstable is this estimate?

The core intuition

A sample is a single “draw” from the population. Draw again and the coefficient moves. Imagine repeating the sampling process many times: the amount the coefficient bounces around is the standard error. Small standard error → the coefficient is stable from sample to sample → we place more confidence in it. A 95% confidence interval means: of intervals built the same way, 95% will cover the true value in the long run — not “there is a 95% probability that the true value falls inside this particular interval.”

Key concepts

Standard error (SE) 标准误
The sampling variability of an estimator. Quadruple the sample and the standard error roughly halves (SE ∝ 1/√n).
Confidence interval (CI) 置信区间
Estimate ± 1.96×SE (large-sample approximation). When the interval excludes 0, the result is usually called “statistically significant at the 5% level.”
p-value p 值
The probability, assuming the effect is actually zero, of observing data as extreme as what we got, or more so. A small p says “the data would be unusual under a zero effect” — and nothing more.
Statistical power 统计功效
The probability of detecting an effect that truly exists. In low-power studies, even a “significant” estimate tends to exaggerate the effect size.
Common pitfalls (the three classic p-value mistakes)

(1) “p = 0.03 means a 3% probability that the null hypothesis is true” — wrong: the p-value takes the null as true as its starting point. (2) “p > 0.05 = no effect” — wrong: it means only that a zero effect cannot be ruled out, especially when small samples leave power low. (3) “Significant = important” — wrong: with a big enough sample, every trivial difference turns significant. Statistical significance ≠ economic significance (whether the effect is large enough to be worth acting on is a separate question).

Quiz 4.1A study reports p = 0.20. The correct statement is?
Explanation: “Not significant” ≠ “does not exist.” With small samples and low power, genuinely real effects often miss significance. Nor is the p-value “the probability the null is true.” The moment you see the argument “not significant → no effect,” check sample size and power.
Quiz 4.2In a huge-sample regression (n = 2 million) of “hours online on test scores,” the coefficient is −0.002 points per hour, p < 0.001. The reasonable reading?
Explanation: Two million observations make even a sesame-seed-sized effect “significant.” 3 hours a day × 365 days × 0.002 ≈ 2 points a year — practically nothing. The lesson: effect size first, significance second; and a large sample does nothing whatsoever about confounding and causality.

Chapter summary

  • The standard error measures sampling variability; CI = estimate ± 1.96·SE.
  • A p-value measures “how unusual the data are under the null,” not “the probability the null is true.”
  • Statistical significance ≠ economic significance; not significant ≠ no effect.

Chapter 5Endogeneity: The Enemy of Identification

Opening question: The numbers say: cities that pour more resources into policing have more homicides. Do police manufacture crime? Or do high-crime cities simply hire more police? When x and y influence each other — or are pulled along by common factors — x is endogenous, and its regression coefficient cannot be read as causal.

The core intuition

“Endogeneity” is an umbrella term for every ailment that leaves “x correlated with the error term”: (1) omitted variables (aptitude shapes both education and income); (2) reverse causality (crime → police); (3) measurement error (income under-reported — and the poorer the household, the wilder the misreporting); (4) self-selection (the people who attend college are the ones who would have earned more anyway). For a causal answer there is exactly one core move: find a source of variation in x that behaves as if it were randomly assigned.

Key concepts

Endogeneity 内生性
The explanatory variable is correlated with other, unobserved determinants of y. With endogeneity present, the OLS coefficient systematically departs from the causal effect.
Self-selection bias 自选择偏差
People choose their own way into the treatment group (going to college, migrating, starting a business) — and hidden ability and motivation lurk behind those choices.
Identification 识别
The research-design problem of “recognizing” a causal effect inside observational data. Identification strategy is the soul of modern empirical economics.
The real world · Three classic scenes

The hospital paradox: surveyed in an emergency room, seat-belt wearers show a higher injury rate — because only the badly injured are taken to hospital (selection bias). Survivorship bias: in World War II, returning bombers’ bullet-hole patterns were tallied to decide where to add armor — the statistician Wald pointed out that the planes worth examining are the ones that never came back (a selected sample). Healthy-user bias: people who take vitamins are healthier to begin with, so a naive comparison blows the estimated benefit of supplements out of proportion.

Quiz 5.1“Smaller classes score better.” But suppose class size is assigned by the principal according to student ability (weaker students placed into small classes). Then?
Explanation: If weaker students fill the small classes, their “disadvantaged intake” drags the estimate down (understatement); if children of high-expectation parents get packed into small classes, the estimate is inflated. The direction depends on “who is placed into the small classes” and on how that placement moves scores. The OVB formula delivers exactly this product direction. The lesson: first ask “who decides class size?”
Quiz 5.2“People who buy insurance are hospitalized more often — so insurance makes people sicker.” The sharpest critique is?
Explanation: Who buys insurance is not random — people in poor health need it more (this is the health-care edition of the “adverse selection” you will meet in Track 05, Money & Banking, and Track 06, Financial Economics). It is a selection problem, not a statistical-power problem.

Chapter summary

  • Four sources of endogeneity: omitted variables, reverse causality, measurement error, self-selection.
  • Endogeneity is a disease no sample size can cure; the way out lies in research design.
  • Faced with any association, first ask: “Is the way people enter the treatment group related to the outcome?”

Chapter 6The Causal-Inference Toolbox

Opening question: If observational data are a minefield at every step, how do economists dare say things as specific as “a 10% rise in the minimum wage reduces teen employment by only 0–1%”? With four “identification weapons.”

The core intuition: randomization is the gold standard

Split the subjects randomly into a treatment group and a control group, and before treatment the two groups are identical in the statistical sense (identical even in unobserved ability, character, and luck). Any post-treatment difference between the groups can then only be the effect of the treatment — random assignment “reproduces” the counterfactual for us. When true randomization is impossible in the real world, economists hunt for institutional variation that is approximately random: bright-line policy cutoffs, the sequencing of timing, the luck of a lottery draw.

The four weapons

MethodCore ideaClassic applicationKey assumption
Randomized experiment (RCT)Assign the treatment at random; the group difference = the causal effectThe 2008 Oregon Medicaid lottery: Medicaid granted at randomRandomization carried through as designed; no spillovers
Difference-in-differences (DID)Before/after × treatment/control, netting out the groups’ fixed difference and their common trendCard & Krueger (1994): New Jersey vs. Pennsylvania minimum wageParallel trends (absent the policy, the two groups would track each other)
Regression discontinuity (RD)Exploit a threshold where the policy kicks in only above the line, comparing the two sidesLong-run incomes of students on either side of a scholarship cutoffIndividuals cannot precisely manipulate the score (crossing the line is as good as random)
Instrumental variable (IV)Find an exogenous variable that affects y only through xUse “distance to college” to predict years of schooling, then estimate the income returnRelevance + exclusion (the instrument operates only through x)
Common pitfalls

“DID/RD/IV was used, so the result is causal.” — Every one of these methods has a make-or-break assumption: DID fails when parallel trends do not hold (the treatment group was already on an upswing); RD fails under precise manipulation of the threshold (the people a point below the line were different all along); IV fails when exclusion is violated (living near a college affects income directly, not only through schooling). When reading an empirical paper, first ask “is the identification assumption credible?” — then look at the coefficients.

Quiz 6.1DID’s most crucial assumption is?
Explanation: Parallel trends are about trajectories (changes), not levels — the before/after differencing in DID exists precisely to cancel out fixed level differences. So “the treatment group starts lower” is not fatal; “the treatment group was already growing fast” is. In practice, several pre-policy periods are used to test whether the trends run in parallel.
Quiz 6.2In a regression-discontinuity (RD) study, the plot most worth checking is?
Explanation: If people can manipulate the assignment variable (nudging a score across the line), the two sides of the cutoff are no longer “quasi-random,” and RD’s foundation collapses. An abnormal heap in the density plot at the cutoff (far too many people just clearing it) is the fingerprint of manipulation. This check is standard equipment in RD papers.
Quiz 6.3When a randomized experiment is out of reach, the closest substitute for “random assignment” is?
Explanation: The gold standard of identification is “treatment assignment unrelated to potential outcomes.” Neither A nor B cuts that link. Natural experiments (bright-line policies, lotteries, birth dates that fix when a child starts school) are precious precisely because they are “randomized experiments run by nature.”

Chapter summary

  • Randomization reproduces the counterfactual; the RCT is the gold standard.
  • DID rests on parallel trends, RD on quasi-random crossing of the cutoff, IV on relevance plus exclusion.
  • When reading empirical work: audit the identification assumption first, trust the coefficients second.

Wrap-upNext steps & further reading

Econometrics is the engine behind every applied branch: 09 Labor (returns to education, minimum wages), 11 Development (home turf of the RCT poverty experiments), and 14 Health & Education (insurance experiments) all lean on this chapter’s tools again and again. To push on into time series (forecasting, volatility) and panel-data methods, climb the reading list below.

  • Wooldridge, Introductory Econometrics: A Modern Approach — the standard undergraduate textbook, giving equal weight to intuition and hands-on practice.
  • Joshua Angrist & Jörn-Steffen Pischke, Mostly Harmless Econometrics — the secret manual of the causal-inference route; companion to Chapters 5–6 here.
  • Angrist & Pischke, Mastering ’Metrics: The Path from Cause to Effect — the popular edition, five weapons explained to the end.
  • The Causal Inference: The Mixtape open lecture notes (Scott Cunningham) — free online and code-friendly.
