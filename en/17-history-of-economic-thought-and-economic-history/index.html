Track 17 · Cross-disciplinary & thought

History of Economic Thought & Economic History

经济思想史与经济史

This track does two things. First, it asks why economists thought the way they did — every theory is an answer to the problems of its age. Second, it asks how the economy itself evolved — growth, divergence, industrialization, and turning points. Read it, and the sixteen tracks before it fall back into historical coordinates: you will know where those models came from, why they look the way they do, and which debates are still unfinished.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • recount, in chronological order, the core claims and signature works of mercantilism, the Physiocrats, classical political economy, Marxism, the marginalist school, Keynesianism, and monetarism
  • work a two-country, two-good specialization example using opportunity-cost comparison, and explain why a country that is absolutely behind in everything can still gain from trade
  • use the Malthusian regime to explain how long-run income stagnation and population swings were linked before the Industrial Revolution
  • use diminishing marginal utility to resolve the diamond–water paradox (the paradox of value), showing that price is set by marginal quantities, not totals
  • compare the neoclassical, New Keynesian, behavioral, evolutionary, and institutionalist schools on price stickiness, rationality assumptions, and policy leanings
  • use the Great Divergence–Great Convergence framework to read the long-run shape of world GDP per capita since 1500, and recount at least three competing explanations
Prerequisites: None (works as a starting point or as a capstone)
Related tracks: 02 Macroeconomics 11 Development & Innovation Economics 15 Behavioral Economics 16 Political Economy & Institutional Economics
First, tell the two "histories" apart

The history of economic thought studies how economists' theories evolved — who influenced whom, which problems were solved, which were shelved. Economic history studies the process of the economy itself — growth, inflation, crises, structural change. This track puts the two in one chapter because each is the other's mirror: theories are answers to the problems of an age, and the age's economic facts test the theories in return. Keynes's General Theory grew out of the Great Depression; Friedman's monetarism grew out of the stagflation of the 1970s — without the history, you will imagine the textbook fell from the sky.

A quick tour of economic thought: eleven milestones

The timeline below is the map for the whole track. Each chapter unpacks one stretch of it; whenever a later chapter leaves you lost, come back here to reorient.

1662
Petty's A Treatise of Taxes and Contributions — political arithmetic begins

"Labour is the father of wealth and land its mother." Petty insisted on discussing the affairs of state in "number, weight, and measure" — the source of national-income estimation and statistical economics.

1758
Quesnay's Tableau Économique — the economy first drawn as a circular flow

A physician at Versailles abstracted agrarian society into flows of money among landlords, farmers, and artisans. The circular-flow perspective of macroeconomics (see track 02) traces back to here.

1776
Smith's Wealth of Nations — economics becomes a discipline

Division of labor, market price, and the "invisible hand". The Declaration of Independence appeared the same year: on one side the birth of a nation, on the other a market society's account of itself.

1798
Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population — a brake installed on growth

The warning that population grows geometrically while subsistence grows arithmetically shaped the next fifty years' thinking about poverty and growth.

1817
Ricardo's On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation — the triumph of the abstract model

Differential rent, the iron law of wages, and comparative advantage: for the first time, economics derived sharp policy conclusions from highly abstract assumptions.

1848
Mill's Principles of Political Economy — the classical system summed, and loosened

The definitive synthesis, with a crack left open: "the laws of production are natural; how wealth is distributed is society's to choose."

1867
Marx's Capital, volume I — surplus value and accumulation

Distribution and power returned to the center of analysis: where profit comes from, how capitalism accumulates, why periodic crises are unavoidable.

1871
Jevons and Menger — the marginal revolution strikes together

Two scholars unknown to each other published books in the same year, moving value from "objective labor" to "subjective utility", and from "totals" to "margins".

1890
Marshall's Principles of Economics — the scissors of supply and demand close

Supply and demand determine price together like the two blades of a pair of scissors; "economics" replaced "political economy" as the standard name of the field.

1936
Keynes's General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money — macroeconomic policy is born

Deficient effective demand can sustain an unemployment equilibrium; fiscal and monetary policy become routine tools for managing aggregate demand.

1963–1976
Monetarism and rational expectations — the counter-revolution

Friedman and Schwartz's A Monetary History of the United States rewrote the explanation of the Great Depression; the Lucas critique demanded macro models built on optimization and rational expectations.

The expansion of the past thirty-odd years deserves notice too: the Nobel has gone in turn to North and Fogel in 1993 (economic history and institutions), Kahneman in 2002 and Thaler in 2017 (behavioral economics), and Acemoglu and colleagues in 2024 (institutions) — the map of mainstream economics is still growing. Chapter 5 picks up this thread.

Chapter 1 Ahead of Smith: Mercantilism and the Physiocrats

Opening question

In 1651 England passed the Navigation Acts: goods from Asia or Europe could be carried into England only on English ships or ships of the producing country. Around the same time, France's finance minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert advised his king: export manufactured goods in exchange for gold and silver, and tax imports heavily. Seen through today's eyes, these are "anti-free-trade" policies. So the question is: were they a collective error made by people who did not yet understand economics, or a shrewd strategy for national survival? And why did economics as a discipline grow precisely out of this "bullion-above-all" doctrine?

The core intuition

The core intuition

Put mercantilism back into its own time: the Reformation and successive wars had sliced Europe into dozens of competing regimes; whoever could afford a standing army and a navy survived, and the hard currency of armies was gold and silver. Hence "the nation's wealth = the bullion in the treasury", and the policy goal a favorable balance of trade. Its error was to identify wealth with money itself; but on its correct side it saw national competition, infant industries, and economies of scale — today's mix of "export orientation + industrial policy + foreign-exchange reserves" is still its modern version.

The Physiocrats staged the first programmatic "anti-mercantilist revolution". Wealth, they said, is not gold and silver but the land's net product (produit net); order is not the product of regulation but of the natural order — let prices guide freely, and resources will find their way to where they should go. Quesnay's Tableau Économique was the first depiction of the whole economy as a circular-flow diagram; the "circular flow" perspective of macroeconomics begins there.

Key concepts

Mercantilism
重商主义
Europe's doctrine of national wealth and power in the 16th–18th centuries: wealth is gold and silver, the means an export surplus and protection. It treated economic policy as a tool in the struggle for power among states — the "prehistoric mainstream" of economics.
Favorable balance of trade
贸易顺差
The value of exports exceeds the value of imports. Mercantilists saw it as the engine for accumulating bullion; Hume later showed that a persistent surplus would vanish automatically through rising prices.
Physiocracy
重农学派
The 18th-century French school led by Quesnay: only agriculture, where nature's powers join in, creates a net product; it stood for laissez-faire and a single tax on land. The name means "the rule of nature".
Produit net
净产品
The surplus of output over the cost of producing it. The Physiocrats held that commerce and manufacturing merely change the form of matter and create no surplus; only agriculture, with the help of natural forces, yields a net product.
Tableau Économique
经济表
Quesnay's 1758 diagram, drawing the flows of spending among landlords, farmers, and artisans as a closed loop — the earliest model of the economy as an aggregate circulation, regarded as the starting point of macro analysis.
Laissez-faire
自由放任
"Leave us alone": the Physiocrats' slogan, a call to abolish controls and privileges and let the natural order work. It later became the banner of classical liberalism.

The real world

The real world · The Navigation Acts and "Britain in mercantilist clothing"

Britain used the Navigation Acts to shield its shipping, three Anglo-Dutch wars to smash Holland's carrying trade, and royal charters to arm the East India Company into "a commercial empire with a fleet"; meanwhile the Corn Laws protected landlords and colonial monopoly fed the textile industry. The telling part is the timing of the undressing: the Corn Laws were repealed in 1846 and the Navigation Acts in 1849 — by then Britain was already "the workshop of the world", its manufactures feared no competition, and so classical free-trade theory (Chapter 2) arrived exactly on cue. This is no coincidence but the norm in the history of economic thought: doctrines defend policies, and policies switch with the stage of development. Worth remembering the next time "should industrial policy retire" comes up.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

Pitfall 1: "Mercantilism = a byword for ignorance." Textbooks use it as the cautionary tale, but back in a war-ridden, state-competitive seventeenth century, hoarding hard currency and nurturing manufactures was a rational strategy; East Asia's export-oriented industrialization after the war carried mercantilist genes too. The right way to criticize it is to point out that "wealth ≠ money" and to name its zero-sum worldview — not to sneer at the ancestors.

Pitfall 2: "Physiocracy = imperial China's pro-agriculture, anti-commerce policy." Similar wording, opposite direction. The Physiocrats stood for laissez-faire, against regulation and privilege, and wanted grain controls abolished and trade opened at home and abroad; China's traditional suppression of commerce was the command logic of a state subduing its merchant class. Quesnay was called "the Confucius of Europe" precisely because he read a natural order into the Confucian idea of wu wei (non-action) — the same word, two institutional meanings.

Chapter summary

  • Mercantilism: wealth = gold and silver, goal = a trade surplus, means = protection and privilege; it was the economics of the age of competing nation-states, not mere stupidity.
  • Hume's price–specie flow mechanism dissolved the possibility of a "permanent surplus" from within: surplus → bullion inflow → prices rise → surplus gone.
  • The Physiocrats: wealth = the land's net product, order = the natural order, prescription = laissez-faire; the Tableau Économique founded circular-flow analysis.
  • The protagonists of the pre-classical age were merchants, physicians, and officials: economics was born of problems of statecraft — policy first, theory after.
  • Beware two pitfalls: mercantilism ≠ ignorance; Physiocracy ≠ imperial China's suppression of commerce.

Chapter 2 Classical Political Economy: The Dismal Science of Growth

Opening question

In The Wealth of Nations, Smith posed a question that has never dulled: it is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest. Yet why does a society in which everyone pursues their own interest not collapse into a jungle of mutual plunder, and instead remain orderly while its wealth grows? Stranger still: why did the classical school, the founder of the "economics of growth", end almost to a man in pessimism — Ricardo arriving at the stationary state, Malthus prophesying famine?

The core intuition

The core intuition

The modern restatement of the invisible hand: the price is at once an information system and an incentive system. When something becomes scarce its price rises, which both tells users "use less" and tells suppliers "produce more" — no one gives orders, yet the dispersed knowledge of millions is coordinated. Its preconditions are competition and property rights, which is exactly why Smith kept insisting on "preventing merchants from conspiring".

Three pillars held up the classical system: the labor theory of value (value originates in labor, providing the yardstick for distribution analysis); a theory of distribution (wages, rent, and profit divide the output, and what one gains the others lose); and diminishing returns combined with population pressure (the ceiling on growth). Ricardo pushed distribution to its limit: land differs in fertility, the marginal land sets the price of grain, and differential rent accrues to landlords; Malthus installed the population brake: whenever wages rise above subsistence, population grows and pushes wages back down. The fruits of technical progress were thus eaten by population and rent, and the economy slid toward the stagnant stationary state. Mill delivered the closing argument but left a door ajar — "the laws of production are natural; how wealth is distributed is a matter of choice" — a door that led to Marx, and later to the welfare state.

Key concepts

The invisible hand
看不见的手
Smith's famous metaphor: given competition and property rights, self-interested behavior is steered by the price mechanism toward promoting society's welfare. It is a conditional proposition, not an unconditional promise.
Division of labor
分工
The opening theme of The Wealth of Nations: dividing labor in a pin factory raised output hundreds of times over. Smith added that the division of labor is limited by the extent of the market — the larger the market, the deeper the division.
Comparative advantage
比较优势
Ricardo's discovery: even if a country is absolutely behind in every product, as long as the structure of opportunity costs differs, specializing in the product with the lowest opportunity cost and trading can still benefit both sides at once.
Differential rent
级差地租
More fertile or better-located land yields more, and the excess accrues to the landlord. Population growth forces society to till ever-poorer marginal land; rent rises and profit is squeezed — which is why Ricardo despised the Corn Laws.
The iron law of wages
工资铁律
The proposition that wages tend toward subsistence in the long run: above subsistence → population grows → labor supply expands → wages fall back. A corollary of Malthus's population theory.
Say's Law
萨伊定律
"Supply creates its own demand": what is produced always exchanges for demand for other things, so a general glut is impossible. It was the cornerstone of the classical view of macro — and Keynes's target number one (Chapter 4).

The real world

The real world · Qing China: a Malthusian specimen that needed no European theory

From 1700 to 1850, China's population grew from roughly 150 million to 430 million. Peace, the folding of the head tax into the land tax, and American crops (maize, sweet potatoes) pushed "technology level A" higher, and population swelled with it; farmland per capita fell from about 4 mu (1 mu ≈ 667 m²) to under 2 mu, and the surplus labor was absorbed into ever-finer intensive cultivation — what historians call "involution". Wages lingered near subsistence, and natural disasters combined with population pressure to brew the great rebellions of the mid-nineteenth century. Qing officials had never heard of Malthus, but the economy behaved exactly as in Figure 2-1: this mechanism operates without needing a theory — which is precisely the value of economic history as a test of economic theory.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

Pitfall 1: "The invisible hand = Smith wanted zero government." In Book V of The Wealth of Nations Smith explicitly lists the sovereign's duties: defense, justice, public works and institutions (including roads, bridges, education); he supported taxing luxuries and opposed merchants conspiring to hold down prices. He even viewed the joint-stock company with suspicion. The "invisible hand" is a conditional proposition, not a laissez-faire blank check.

Pitfall 2: Judging by absolute advantage instead of comparative advantage. "The other side is better (or worse) than us at everything, so trade is pointless" is the most common computational error. Comparative advantage compares opportunity costs, not absolute labor hours — a country that is absolutely behind in every field necessarily still has a relative edge in some opportunity cost (see the worked example in Quiz 2.1).

Quiz 2.1Country A needs 100 labor-hours to produce a car and 10 hours for a garment; country B needs 120 and 20 respectively. Specializing by comparative advantage, the right assignment is —
Explanation: Compute the opportunity costs: in country A one car costs 10 garments, in country B one car costs 6. B's opportunity cost of producing cars is lower (6 < 10), so B has the comparative advantage in cars; symmetrically, A has it in garments (0.1 cars per garment < roughly 0.167), so the answer is A. C confuses absolute with comparative advantage: the side that is absolutely behind across the board still necessarily has a product in which it is relatively better off. B gets the direction backwards.
Quiz 2.2Why did Malthus's prophecy fail in the industrialized world? The answer closest to the mainstream explanation is —
Explanation: C is correct. The Industrial Revolution broke the trap with two things: sustained technical progress that overwhelmed diminishing returns, and the "demographic transition" — birth rates fall as income and education rise (see track 11, chapter 6), so population no longer swallows the increment. A contradicts the facts (technology accelerated and population grew too); B was not the general experience of the industrialized world. But remember: in the world before 1800 the mechanism of Figure 2-1 did hold for the long haul — Malthus was not "all wrong".

Chapter summary

  • Smith: division of labor + prices (the invisible hand) coordinate dispersed knowledge; The Wealth of Nations is at once a founding document of economics and an anti-mercantilist manifesto.
  • Ricardo: differential rent and distributional conflict; comparative advantage showed that both sides gain by specializing according to opportunity cost — the cornerstone of trade theory (track 07).
  • Malthus: the iron law of wages and the population brake — technical progress diluted by population growth, income per person trapped near subsistence.
  • Mill: the synthesizer of the classical system, whose "the laws of production are natural, distribution is a choice" left the door open for later interventionist thought.
  • The classical base tone was pessimism (the stationary state), because it had not yet seen sustained technological revolution or the demographic transition.

Chapter 3 Marx and the Marginal Revolution: Two Roads to Value

Opening question

Smith left behind a famous paradox: water is the most useful thing of all, yet costs almost nothing; diamonds are nearly useless, yet are worth fortunes. The classical scholars waved it away with "natural price"; Ricardo locked value onto labor, detaching price from "usefulness" altogether. In 1871, England's Jevons and Austria's Menger, each unaware of the other, delivered the same answer in the same year. What question did they, independently, change?

The core intuition

The core intuition

The marginalists' answer was a double turn: value moved from the objective to the subjective (goods do not "contain" value; people evaluate them), and from totals to the margin. What sets price is not the total utility water delivers but the marginal utility of the "last glass of water". The stock of water is billions of tons, so the last glass is worth almost nothing, and water is cheap; the stock of diamonds is tiny, their marginal utility immense, and they are dear. Utility diminishes as consumption rises — turning "demand" from a slogan into a curve you can draw.

Marx took the other road. He did not ask "what determines relative prices" but "where does profit come from, and where is capitalism headed". The core of the answer is surplus value: what is special about labor power as a commodity is that its use (labor) creates more value than the commodity itself costs (the subsistence wage), and the difference is appropriated by capital; around it unfolds a dynamic picture of accumulation, a rising organic composition of capital, a falling tendency of the rate of profit, and periodic crises. The two roads look mutually exclusive, but they answer different questions — in Marshall's scissors, supply (cost, labor) and demand (utility, the margin) determine price together, like the two blades of a pair of scissors, neither dispensable; Walras pushed the view toward the general equilibrium of the entire market system.

Key concepts

Labor theory of value
劳动价值论
The doctrine that a commodity's value is determined by the labor required to produce it — begun by Petty and Smith, made rigorous by Ricardo, critically inherited by Marx. The marginalists replaced it with utility theory.
Surplus value
剩余价值
Marx's core concept: the portion of the value workers create that exceeds the value of their labor power (the subsistence wage), appropriated without equivalent by the owners of capital — the source of profit.
Organic composition of capital
资本有机构成
The ratio of constant capital (machines and raw materials) to variable capital (labor power). Marx held that it rises with accumulation, the share of variable capital falls, and the result is a falling rate of profit and a reserve army of the unemployed.
Marginal utility
边际效用
The additional utility from consuming one more unit of a good. It usually diminishes as consumption rises; the marginal school held that exchange value (price) is determined by marginal, not total, utility.
Marginal revolution
边际革命
The event in the early 1870s when Jevons (England), Menger (Austria), and Walras (France/Lausanne, Switzerland) almost simultaneously and independently proposed marginal-utility value theory, reshaping the analytical toolkit of economics.
Marshallian scissors
马歇尔剪刀
Marshall's partial-equilibrium framework: demand (utility) and supply (cost) determine price and quantity together like the two blades of a pair of scissors. The supply–demand diagrams in today's textbooks are its direct descendants.

The real world

The real world · Das Kapital's travels in China

Volume I of Capital was published in Hamburg in 1867; more than half a century later, in the late 1920s, the first complete Chinese translation appeared. From the 1950s, political economy on the model of the Soviet textbook became the standard course in Chinese universities and cadre education, and surplus value, the organic composition of capital, and reproduction in two departments became the shared language of generations. From the 1980s on, "socialist political economy" gradually incorporated the commodity economy, the law of value, and market mechanisms, up to the practical history of the "socialist market economy" being written into the preamble of the constitution. The reception history of a theory is itself a history of reform: the theoretical framework determines which problems people can "see", and real problems keep forcing the framework to expand.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

The pitfall: "The marginal revolution 'refuted' Marx; the history of thought is an elimination tournament." This is the most popular linear-progress view of the history of ideas, and it will not stand. First, the two answer different questions — the marginalists answered "how are relative prices determined", Marx answered "where does profit come from and what is capitalism's long-run dynamic". Second, ideas are rarely "refuted"; more often they "divide the labor" — income distribution, financial fragility, and crisis theory in modern macro still work with Marxian questions (after the 2008 crisis, sales of Capital briefly revived). Third, utility theory itself went through successive rebuilds — cardinal to ordinal utility to revealed preference — so today's "standard model" is just as much a product of history. Read the history of thought as "a score sheet written by the winners" and you will underestimate the intelligence of every generation.

Quiz 3.1On the marginal school's explanation, water is cheaper than diamonds because —
Explanation: B is correct: water's total utility is enormous (without water we die), but the stock makes the marginal utility of "one more glass" approach zero, and competition presses the price toward that margin; diamonds are scarce, their marginal utility high, their price high. A states total utility backwards; C is the labor-theory answer — it explains part of supply cost but cannot explain why the same labor meets an abundant stock (like water) and the price still stays low, which is exactly the puzzle piece the marginal revolution supplied.

Explanation: B is correct; this is the defining mechanism of surplus value. A is the "trick of circulation" Marx explicitly rejected: the capitalist class as a whole cannot create surplus value by reselling to one another — circulation only changes distribution. Whether or not one accepts the theory, restating its mechanism accurately is the precondition for understanding the history of thought.

Chapter summary

  • The marginal revolution's double turn: value from objective to subjective, from totals to the margin; price is set by the utility of "the last unit", and the diamond–water paradox dissolves.
  • Three men, one question: Jevons (England), Menger (Austria), and Walras (Lausanne) independently proposed marginal utility theory in the early 1870s; Walras went further, to general equilibrium.
  • Marx's problem domain was different: surplus value, accumulation, organic composition, and crisis, putting distribution and power back at the center of analysis.
  • Marshall's scissors stitched the two roads into "supply and demand determine price together"; the partial-equilibrium framework is still in use today.
  • The history of thought is not an elimination tournament: different schools are often answering different questions.

Chapter 4 The Keynesian Revolution and the Monetarist Counter-Revolution

Opening question

In 1933 the U.S. unemployment rate was about 25 percent, and industrial output had shrunk nearly a third from 1929. Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon's prescription: "Liquidate labor, liquidate stocks, liquidate the farmers, liquidate real estate… purge the rottenness out of the system." Four years later, in 1936, Keynes answered in The General Theory: waiting for the market to heal itself is a species of superstition, because the lesion lies in "effective demand" itself. After 1929 the world took ten years to respond; after 2008, one year — two starkly different answer sheets. Why such a difference?

The core intuition

The core intuition

The classical camp's axiom was Say's Law: supply creates its own demand, a general glut is impossible, and unemployment can only mean wages have not fallen far enough. Keynes's counterattack runs: income is determined by expenditure; people consume only part of any increase in income (marginal propensity to consume < 1), so saving and investment can fail to match — the interest rate does not guarantee that the two clear, because money itself is also an asset (liquidity preference). The economy can therefore settle at an unemployment equilibrium, with no internal force to push it back. The policy implication: fill the demand gap with public spending, which the income–consumption chain then amplifies (the multiplier).

The monetarist counter-revolution started from evidence: Friedman and Schwartz went through the Federal Reserve's archives and found that the money stock contracted by about a third from 1929 to 1933 — the Great Depression was not "proof of market failure" but "a disaster of central-bank dereliction". "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon"; there exists a natural rate of unemployment independent of money, and the long-run Phillips curve is vertical. Lucas went further (the Lucas critique): a change in policy changes people's expectations and behavior, so evaluating a new policy with old parameters is bound to fail — macro models must be built on individual optimization. This counter-revolution directly shaped today's central banks, with their inflation targets and rule-based conduct.

Key concepts

Effective demand
有效需求
Keynes's core concept: actual output and employment in the economy are determined by planned aggregate spending; when demand is deficient the economy can settle at an unemployment equilibrium with no automatic mechanism of return.
Liquidity preference
流动性偏好
The willingness to hold money (the safest, most flexible asset). It breaks the interest rate's ability to coordinate saving and investment automatically — a key link in Keynes's rebuttal of Say's Law.
Paradox of thrift
节俭悖论
If all households raise their saving at once, aggregate demand and income fall with it, and total saving may end up no higher — even lower. A classic case of individually rational behavior composing collective irrationality.
Quantity theory of money
货币数量论
MV = PY: the money stock times its velocity equals prices times output. If V is stable and Y determined by real factors, money growth determines inflation — Friedman's banner.
Natural rate hypothesis
自然失业率假说
There exists an unemployment rate determined by the structure of the labor market; trading inflation for employment works only in the short run, before expectations adjust — the long-run Phillips curve is vertical.
Lucas critique
卢卡斯批判
Proposed by Lucas in 1976: when the policy rule changes, people's expectations and behavior change with it, and the parameters estimated in a model are no longer stable — old models cannot be used to evaluate new policies.

The real world

The real world · 1929 and 2008: the same fire, two fire brigades

After 1929: nominal fealty to balanced budgets, more than 9,000 bank failures from 1930 to 1933, a money stock contraction of roughly a third, real output down a quarter, and a recovery that dragged on until World War II. After 2008: Ben Bernanke — a monetary historian who made his name studying the Great Depression — had the Federal Reserve cut rates to zero within months and launch quantitative easing, with fiscal policy expanding in step; the U.S. recession lasted only a year and a half, and unemployment peaked in October 2009 before slowly receding. The contrast between the two answer sheets is practically this chapter's "lab report": Keynes said stabilize aggregate demand, Friedman said don't let money collapse — in 2008 the decision-makers had learned both lessons. And the European countries that turned to austerity after 2010, and the double-dip recessions that followed, supplied the multiplier with a less glorious footnote.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

Pitfall 1: "Keynes advocated permanent deficits and unlimited government." What Keynes advocated was countercyclical policy: expand in recessions, run surpluses in booms, and smooth the business cycle — not turn fiscal deficits into a way of life. He himself helped design the fixed-but-adjustable exchange-rate order after the war (Bretton Woods). "In the long run we are all dead" was a jab at the excuse of "waiting for the market to heal itself", not an argument for ignoring the long run.

Pitfall 2: "Friedman denied the severity of the Great Depression." The opposite — he held that the Depression was extremely severe; he only moved the responsibility from "the inherent instability of capitalism" to "the Federal Reserve's mistaken contraction": had the central bank acted as lender of last resort, the depression could have remained an ordinary recession. Understand that, and you understand why central banks reacted so fast in 2008: that was a direct execution of the Friedman–Schwartz conclusion.

Explanation: B is correct: one person's spending is another person's income; if everyone cuts back at once, incomes slide together, and since saving = income × saving rate, one factor rises while the other falls — total saving can stand still or even decline. Quiz 4.2The core explanation of the Great Depression in Friedman and Schwartz's A Monetary History of the United States is —
Explanation: B is correct; this is the foundational empirical study of monetarism: amid waves of bank failures the Fed failed in its lender-of-last-resort duty, and monetary contraction amplified the recession through the debt-deflation mechanism. A is the language of (some) Marxist or pessimistic cycle theories; C stands fiscal and monetary roles exactly on their heads — Friedman's whole point was that money "matters a great deal". Interestingly, B does not deny market failure; it simply puts policy failure first — a detail that went on to determine the crisis playbook of the world's central banks.

Chapter summary

  • The Keynesian revolution's three supports: effective demand determines output; liquidity preference severs the automatic clearing of saving and investment; the multiplier amplifies public spending.
  • The paradox of thrift is the best entrance into the fallacy of composition and the difference between macro and micro.
  • Friedman's counter-revolution: the monetary explanation of the Great Depression, "inflation is a monetary phenomenon", the natural rate of unemployment, and the vertical long-run Phillips curve.
  • The Lucas critique: policy changes expectations and behavioral parameters, so macro models must rest on optimization — the shared starting point of new classical macro and New Keynesian economics.
  • The 1929–2008 comparison shows: Keynes and Friedman were less rivals than two lessons of the same fire brigade.

Chapter 5 A Map of Contemporary Schools: A Toolbox, Not a Creed

Opening question

After Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008, watching the same soaring unemployment, some scholars in Chicago said "let markets clear; intervention only delays the adjustment", while scholars in Cambridge and Berkeley said "stimulate fiscally, now, or the 1930s will repeat". Both groups hold the same doctorates and speak the same mathematical language. Is economics a science, or a family that talks past itself? And if the latter, how does it still produce models that work?

The core intuition

The core intuition

Read the contest of schools as a standings contest over "do you believe in markets" and you miss the point. The real disagreements are over three technical parameters: how fast prices adjust (flexible or sticky), how rational expectations are (fully rational, boundedly rational, or rule-following), and which frictions matter most (information, institutions, cognition, or coordination). Order those parameters and you get the map of contemporary schools. The postwar mainstream was the neoclassical synthesis (Samuelson stitched Keynesian macro to Marshallian micro: the long run to neoclassics, the short run to Keynes); after the stagflation of the 1970s shook it, new classical macro (Lucas, Sargent) rebuilt the foundations on rational expectations, while New Keynesian economics (Mankiw, Stiglitz) gave stickiness microfoundations through menu costs and staggered pricing — today's DSGE models at central banks are the descendants of that compromise between the two. Meanwhile, behavioral economics amends the rationality assumption, evolutionary economics swaps equilibrium for a process of selection, and institutionalism puts transaction costs and property rights center stage. They are not teams but different wrenches in one toolbox: the good economist asks "which friction matters most in this problem", not "which school am I".

Key concepts

Neoclassical synthesis
新古典综合
The textbook system Samuelson stitched together after the war: the long run governed by neoclassical growth and price clearing, the short run by Keynesian demand management; IS-LM and the Solow model are its engineering products.
New Keynesian economics
新凯恩斯主义
Accepts rational expectations but supplies microfoundations for price stickiness (menu costs, staggered contracts, efficiency wages); concludes that money is non-neutral in the short run and that policy should follow rules and target inflation.
Behavioral economics
行为经济学
Systematically amends the full-rationality assumption with experimental and psychological evidence: reference points and loss aversion, hyperbolic discounting, default options. It does not overturn the optimization framework; it fits it with realistic gears.
Evolutionary economics
演化经济学
Treats the economy as an evolving population: firms act on routines, markets do the selecting, innovation is mutation; equilibrium is just a way station passed through. The founding work is Nelson and Winter's An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change (1982).
Institutionalism
制度主义
In a world of positive transaction costs, property rights, contracts, and organizational forms determine performance — from the old institutionalism of Veblen and Commons to the new institutional economics of Coase, North, and Williamson.
Freshwater vs. saltwater schools
淡水派与咸水派
A nickname of American academia: the Great Lakes schools (Chicago, Minnesota, and others) trust market clearing and rational expectations more; the coastal ones (MIT, Harvard, Berkeley, and others) accept stickiness and intervention more readily. The quarrel went public after 2008.

The real world

The real world · The Nobel Prize: a history of the mainstream's expanding map

The Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel is the best instrument for watching the boundaries of the mainstream move. The first prize, in 1969, went to the econometricians Tinbergen and Frisch; then Samuelson (1970), Friedman (1976), and Lucas (1995) crowned the three "revolutions" in turn. More telling is the co-optation of the "heterodox": in 1993 North and Fogel won for cliometrics (quantitative economic history), seating economic-history methods at the head table; in 2002 Kahneman and Smith brought experiments and behavior into the inner sanctum; by 2017 Thaler's "nudges" were already written into pension default options in several countries; and in 2024 Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson won for "how institutions determine prosperity" — the Great Divergence debate of Chapter 6 thereby formally joined the mainstream agenda. Medals do not adjudicate who is right, but they faithfully record that the territory of economics keeps expanding amid its disputes.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

The pitfall: "Economics should have (or pretend to have) a single correct answer." To conclude from economists' disagreements that "economics is not a science" is the same error as concluding from doctors' disagreements over a hard case that "medicine is not a science". Mature disciplines converge tightly on the facts (no economist denies scarcity, opportunity cost, or marginal analysis) and disagree legitimately over parameters and frictions. Models are sets of "if–then" conditionals, not lists of truths: asking "do this model's premises hold here?" is always more productive than asking "which school does this model belong to?". The reverse habit — treating schools as teams and picking only the conclusions your team likes — is the worst habit a self-learner can have.

Chapter summary

  • Disagreements between schools are "parameter-level": the speed of price adjustment, how expectations are formed, which frictions matter most — not a war of faith over "believing in markets".
  • The neoclassical synthesis stitched Keynes to Marshall; after stagflation shook it, new classical macro and the New Keynesians rebuilt from the two ends — "rational expectations" and "microfoundations for stickiness".
  • Behavioral, evolutionary, and institutional economics — three outside challengers — amended the rationality assumption, the view of equilibrium, and the frictionless world, and were absorbed into the mainstream one after another.
  • Models are a toolbox: the problem determines the tool, not the standpoint the conclusion.
  • The Nobel map is the instrument for watching the mainstream's boundary move: cliometrics (1993), behavioral (2002, 2017), institutions (2024) entered the list in turn.

Chapter 6 Big Questions of Economic History: The Great Divergence, Industrialization, and the Monetary Order

Opening question

In the year 1000, the residents of Kaifeng and the residents of Venice lived at similar standards — all of them farmers in a Malthusian world, plus a few merchants and artisans. By 1913, GDP per capita in Western Europe was about six times China's (Britain's nearly nine times). Across those nine hundred years, the world economy really tells just one story: a handful of regions in northwestern Europe first slipped the Malthusian rope, and the gap then opened like a rift valley (the Great Divergence); after the mid-twentieth century, countries at the other end of the rope began to catch up (the Great Convergence). How did the rift open? Why England, of all places? And what did the catch-up run on?

The core intuition

The core intuition

Plot nine hundred years of long-run data (Figure 6-1) and you see the boundary between two regimes: the Malthusian regime, where any technical advance is diluted by population growth and income per person stays trapped near subsistence (the mechanism diagram is exactly Figure 2-1 in Chapter 2); and the modern growth regime, where technical progress persistently outruns the population drag and the income curve bends upward for the first time. The Great Divergence is the process by which England, between roughly 1750 and 1850, completed this regime switch first and pulled away from the rest. The mainstream explanation is a "coupling of many factors": cheap coal released the energy constraint (Pomeranz's "coal and colonies" thesis); property rights, patents, and a parliament that constrained the king (the institutions thesis: North, Acemoglu); Atlantic trade and high wages forming a "demand–invention" loop (Allen's high-wage induced-innovation thesis); plus culture, geography, and luck. Its companion, industrialization, was not merely "having machines" but the superposition of three revolutions: an energy revolution (coal and steam), an organizational revolution (the factory system and the modern corporation), and structural transformation (agriculture → manufacturing → services).

The protagonist of the twentieth century was the monetary order: the gold standard collapsed between the world wars; in 1944 the Bretton Woods system established fixed exchange rates with the dollar pegged to gold and other currencies pegged to the dollar; in 1971 Nixon closed the gold window (the unanchoring of gold), and the world entered the age of pure fiat money and floating rates. Alongside it ran the "growth turning point": the economic historian Gordon points out that 1870–1970 was America's "special century" — electricity, the internal combustion engine, running water, and antibiotics transformed life all at once, and productivity growth stepped down after around 1973. Understand these three threads — divergence, industrialization, and the monetary order — and you hold the historical coordinate system for reading economic news about the modern world.

Key concepts

The Great Divergence
大分流
The process, beginning around 1750–1850, by which the income-per-capita gap between northwestern Europe and its overseas offshoots and the rest of the world kept widening; Pomeranz's 2000 book of the same name made it a core issue of global history.
Malthusian regime
马尔萨斯体制
The normal state of the pre-industrial world: income temporarily above subsistence → population grows → the marginal product of labor falls → income falls back. Income per person stagnates in the long run; technical progress translates into population size, not living standards.
Industrialization
工业化
The transformation of economic structure and production methods from agricultural handicrafts to manufacturing and machine-based mass production, layered with an energy revolution (coal, steam) and an organizational revolution (the factory, the corporation) — the main corridor of escape from the Malthusian regime.
Gold standard
金本位
A system in which money is convertible into gold at a fixed parity. Under the classical nineteenth-century gold standard, exchange rates were fixed and capital flowed freely, but domestic policy had to submit to the external balance — at the cost of deflation and unemployment.
Bretton Woods system
布雷顿森林体系
The international monetary order established in 1944: the dollar pegged to gold at $35 an ounce, other currencies pegged to the dollar; the double peg designed by Keynes and White ended in 1971.
The Great Convergence
大收敛
The process, from the mid-twentieth century on, by which latecomer economies (Japan, East Asia, China, India) grew persistently faster than the pioneers and the world income gap fell back from its peak.

Milestones in economic history

Behind every turning point on the long-run curve stand concrete historical events. This timeline posts the "signposts" for Figure 6-1:

c. 1500
The great voyages and the Columbian Exchange

The great exchange of crops, silver, and pathogens between the Old and New Worlds opened the global trade network; the inflow of silver and urban wages planted the seeds of structural change in Western Europe.

1694
The Bank of England is founded

After the Glorious Revolution, "the king too was bound by debt": credible commitment let Britain borrow long term at the lowest interest rates in Europe — the fiscal-military state and the financial revolution converged.

1760s–1840s

Coal, steam, and the factory system reinforced one another; income per person escaped the millennial flat line for the first time — the upward bend of the Western Europe curve in Figure 6-1.

1821
Britain moves to a de facto gold standard

After Newton's recoinage and the wartime suspension, Britain in fact converted at a fixed parity; in the 1870s the gold standard became the international norm.

1870–1914
The first globalization

Steamships, railways, the telegraph, and the gold standard carried massive trade, migration, and capital flows; the trade-to-output ratio was not regained until the end of the twentieth century.

1929–1933
The Great Depression and the collapse of the gold standard

Collapsing output and waves of bank failures gave birth to the Keynesian revolution (Chapter 4); countries abandoned the gold constraint one after another — the cautionary tale for the postwar monetary order.

1944
The Bretton Woods system

The dollar pegged to gold, other currencies to the dollar, with the IMF and the World Bank; the design Keynes witnessed tried to combine the stability of fixed rates with the flexibility of adjustment.

1971
Gold is unanchored

Nixon closed the gold window and the double peg ended: the world entered the age of pure fiat money and floating exchange rates; from then on, inflation became the core battlefield of macro policy.

1978–
China's reform and opening

From the household responsibility system and dual-track prices to special economic zones and WTO entry: structural transformation completed in a little over four decades — the largest sample of the Great Convergence.

2001
China joins the World Trade Organization

Global supply chains reorganized and the "world's factory" took shape; the renewed steepening of the China curve in Figure 6-1 after 2000 is directly tied to this.

The real world

The real world · China's catch-up: the Self-Strengthening Movement and reform & opening as two answer sheets

The Self-Strengthening Movement of the 1860s had already tried to "seek strength and wealth": the Jiangnan Arsenal, the Hanyang Ironworks, the China Merchants Steam Navigation Company — imported machinery arrived even earlier than Japan's Meiji Restoration. But "Chinese essence, Western application" imported technology alone and left property rights and incentives untouched: to its managers, an enterprise under "official supervision and merchant management" was an official post, not an asset; losses were covered by the state, profits disposed of by bureaucrats. Reform and opening, more than a century later, started from the other end: the household responsibility system handed the residual claim to farm households, dual-track prices and special zones let the market grow first at the margin, and WTO entry threw Chinese firms into global competition. The results are written in Figure 6-1: in 1978 China's GDP per capita stood at nearly the same order of magnitude as in 1820; over the following four decades it grew more than twentyfold, by 2010 China had the world's largest manufacturing sector, and in 2021 absolute poverty was declared eliminated. The same technology available, different institutional arrangements, two utterly different answer sheets — precisely the institutionalists' core argument for explaining both the Great Divergence and the Great Convergence.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls

Pitfall 1: "The Great Divergence has a single cause." Every popular version has its fans: "it was all coal", "all colonial plunder", "all culture", "all institutions". Serious work in economic history almost always converges on a coupling of many factors at critical junctures: high wages inducing labor-saving invention requires accessible coal; patent incentives require credible property rights; Atlantic markets require a navy and finance… Single-cause explanations are seductive because they turn an argument into team-picking; read the original literature (Pomeranz vs. Allen) and you find the two sides share most of the facts — the disagreement is about weights.

Pitfall 2: "The 1971 unanchoring of gold = a sufficient cause of all inflation since." Unanchoring did remove the hard constraint on money issuance and opened the institutional space for 1970s inflation; but attributing the whole stagflation to it is over-attribution — the oil shocks, wage–price spirals, and self-fulfilling inflation expectations are equally on the record (see track 05, chapter 5). The opposite extreme is equally to be avoided: "unanchoring changed nothing". Treating an institutional change as "opening a possibility, with the consequences depending on how the central bank used its new freedom" is the steadier historical judgment.

Chapter summary

  • The nine-hundred-year long curve = two regimes: the Malthusian flat line (roughly 1000–1750) and the upward bend of modern growth; the Great Divergence is the stagger in the timing of the regime switch.
  • The mainstream shape of explanations for the Great Divergence is a coupling of many factors: coal and colonies, institutions and property rights, high wages inducing innovation, trade and luck — beware single-cause determinism.
  • Industrialization was the superposition of three revolutions — energy, organization, and structural transformation — not just "having machines".
  • The monetary order in three acts: the classical gold standard → the Bretton Woods double peg → fiat money and floating rates after the 1971 unanchoring of gold; from then on inflation became the core policy battlefield.
  • The Great Convergence is the main line of the second half of the twentieth century, and China's reform and opening is its largest sample: the same world of technology, different institutional arrangements, different answer sheets.
