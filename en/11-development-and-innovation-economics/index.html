Development & Innovation Economics

发展与创新经济学

Why is per-capita income in some countries fifty times that of others? Why does growth sometimes last a century and sometimes stop dead? This track studies how wealth and opportunity are distributed across countries and eras: how to measure “development,” what makes growth sustainable, how poor economies can escape traps, what roles institutions and innovation play, and how population and industrial structure are transformed.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • Compare development levels with the HDI's three-dimensional framework (health, education, income) and explain why the geometric mean leaves lopsided performance nowhere to hide
  • Use growth accounting to decompose output growth into the contributions of capital, labor, and TFP, and say what sits inside the “residual”
  • Draw the S-curve diagram of the poverty trap and show how coordination failure, the critical threshold, and the Big Push interact
  • Recount natural experiments such as the Korean Peninsula and compare how well the geography, institutions, and culture hypotheses explain the gap between rich and poor
  • Explain creative destruction and the patent trade-off, and describe the three phases of the S-shaped technology adoption curve
  • Describe the four stages of the demographic transition and the Lewis turning point, and assess what the demographic dividend and population aging imply for growth and policy
Prerequisites: 02 Macroeconomics · Growth and the Solow model

Chapter 1What is development: measuring beyond GDP

Opening question

An oil exporter has GDP per capita above US$50,000, yet half its women cannot read and life expectancy is shorter than in middle-income countries—is it a “developed country”? Conversely, a small South Asian country has modest per-capita income yet achieves universal literacy and basic health care for all—is it “backward”? Are “rich” and “developed” the same thing? To answer these questions, we must first fit development with a dashboard.

The core intuition

Income is a means, not an end. The economist Amartya Sen argues that the essence of development is expanding human capabilities: people being able to lead lives with real choices—living long, reading fluently, taking part. GDP is like the “weight” line on a medical report; the HDI is like a comprehensive checkup in which blood pressure, cholesterol, and weight each get their own reading. The Human Development Index (HDI) normalizes each of its three dimensions—health, education, income—to a 0-to-1 scale and then takes the geometric mean. The geometric mean is ruthless about lopsidedness: a collapse in any one dimension drags down the total, and you cannot paper over failure in one dimension by maxing out another. That is precisely the design intent: development cannot be bought with money alone.

Measuring poverty is just as exacting. The World Bank's extreme poverty line is US$2.15 per day—but this is in “international dollars” converted at purchasing power parity (PPP): it measures not dollars at market exchange rates, but whether a household can buy a survival basket of goods where it lives.

Key concepts

Human Development Index (HDI)人类发展指数
The UNDP's index combining health (life expectancy), education (years of schooling), and income (GNI per capita, PPP) as three normalized dimensions, aggregated with a geometric mean. Scores above 0.800 fall in the very high human development group.
Extreme poverty line极端贫困线
The World Bank's survival threshold of US$2.15 per day at 2017 PPP; separate lines of US$3.65 and US$6.85 apply to lower-middle- and upper-middle-income countries. PPP conversion reflects local purchasing power.
Purchasing power parity (PPP)购买力平价
Converts incomes using the actual local prices of the same basket of goods in each country, correcting the way market exchange rates understate differences in living costs. Cross-country comparisons of living standards should use PPP.
Development vs. growth发展对增长
Growth is the rise in output per person; development also encompasses health, education, gender equality, the structure of opportunity, and freedom. Growth is usually development's strongest engine, but the two are not synonymous.

Interactive demo

Try it: drag the “Life expectancy” slider down to its minimum, then max out “Income per capita” alone—can the HDI compensate? The answer is no: the geometric mean punishes lopsidedness.

HDI = ( Ihealth × Ieducation × Iincome ) 1/3 each component is first normalized to 0–1 (life expectancy over 20–85 years, income over ln(100)–ln(75000)); since 2010 the UNDP has used the geometric mean instead of the arithmetic mean, to penalize imbalance among components
The real world

China is among the economies whose development indicators have improved fastest over the past three decades: its HDI rose from 0.484 in 1990 to 0.788 in 2022, and the UNDP calls it the only economy in the world over that period to leap from the “low human development” group into the “high human development” group. Under the current standard, the rural absolute poor fell from 98.99 million at the end of 2012 to zero by the end of 2020—more than 70 percent of the global reduction in poverty over the period. Over the same span, the absolute number of people in extreme poverty in sub-Saharan Africa rose rather than fell: behind the global “average” of poverty reduction, the regional distribution shifted dramatically.

Common pitfalls

(1) High GDP per capita equals developed—wrong: resource economies can have very high incomes alongside lagging education, gender, and structural indicators, which is exactly the lopsidedness the HDI is built to reveal. (2) Moving the poverty line from $1.90 to $2.15 was “raising the standard”—no: it simply re-prices the same survival basket for US inflation. (3) Comparing living standards at market exchange rates—wrong: exchange rates are dominated by capital flows and tradable goods and systematically understate the real purchasing power of poor countries. (4) A low HDI means a country has nothing going for it—the HDI is only one dashboard; the inequality-adjusted HDI, the Gender Development Index, and other multidimensional lenses exist as well. Measurement always serves the question; it does not replace judgment.

Check yourself

Quiz 1.1Two countries have nearly the same gross national income per capita (PPP), but very different HDIs. The most likely explanation is?
Explanation: The HDI is the geometric mean of the health, education, and income dimensions. If the income dimension is nearly identical while the HDI gap is large, the difference must come mainly from health and education—B. A: population size does not affect a per-capita measure; C and D: neither military spending nor exchange rates enter the HDI (the income dimension is computed at PPP, which already strips out exchange-rate volatility).
Quiz 1.2Which statement about changes in global extreme poverty since 1990 is correct?
Explanation: B. Since 1990 the global extreme poverty rate has fallen from about 38% to below 10%, but the decline is heavily concentrated in China and East Asia, while Africa's share has risen. A is wrong: the line is PPP-converted. C is wrong: China accounts for most of the global reduction. D is wrong: improvement in the global average can coexist with deterioration in individual countries (war, epidemic).

Chapter summary

  • Development is multidimensional: growth is the engine, but development also includes health, education, opportunity, and freedom—and the measuring tools themselves shape how we think about development.
  • HDI = the geometric mean of the health, education, and income components; the geometric mean punishes lopsidedness—maxing out one component cannot mask a collapse in another.
  • Cross-country and cross-time comparisons must use PPP; market exchange rates systematically distort comparisons of living costs.
  • The extreme poverty line (2.15 international dollars per day, 2017 PPP) measures a survival floor; the achievement of global poverty reduction is enormous but extremely unevenly distributed.
  • High GDP per capita does not mean developed: structural and distributional information hides behind the aggregates.

Chapter 2The determinants of growth: capital, labor, and TFP

Opening question

After World War II, mainstream economists believed that poor countries lacked only capital: give them money and machines, and they would catch up. Sixty years of international aid and investment later, why did some economies (South Korea, Taiwan (China)) genuinely take off, while many more accumulated capital rapidly yet saw growth stall or even reverse? “The same machines, the same workers—why is output different?” The most important concept of this track hides inside that gap.

The core intuition

Sort growth into three buckets: more machines and plants (capital accumulation), more labor input (hours, human capital), and the residual “more output from the same inputs”—total factor productivity (TFP). Think of a restaurant's revenue growth: it can come from opening more tables (capital) or hiring more staff (labor), or from faster table turnover (TFP). The Solow model tells us that because of diminishing returns to capital, growth from piling up capital alone must taper away—the ultimate engine of sustainable growth is TFP.

So where does TFP come from? Endogenous growth theory answers: knowledge. Ideas are nonrivalrous—you and I can use the same theorem, the same recipe—so accumulating knowledge brings increasing returns, and the growth rate itself can be changed over the long run by policy: R&D, education, openness, and competition are no longer “level effects” but “growth-rate effects.”

Key concepts

Total factor productivity (TFP)全要素生产率
The “residual” in output growth that capital and labor inputs cannot explain: the sum of technology, organization, allocative efficiency, scale economies, and measurement error. The main explanatory variable for long-run growth.
Growth accounting增长核算
The decomposition pioneered by Solow: output growth = TFP growth + capital share × capital growth + labor share × labor growth. The first report for diagnosing the sources of growth.
Endogenous growth theory内生增长理论
Romer, Lucas, and others put technological progress and human-capital accumulation inside the model: nonrival knowledge brings increasing returns, and policy (R&D, education, openness) can affect the growth rate over the long run.
Convergence趋同
The hypothesis that poorer economies grow faster than rich ones and catch up. Absolute convergence fails in world samples; conditional convergence—controlling for investment rates, population, and human capital—has strong evidence; there is also “club convergence.”

Interactive demo

Try it: hold output growth at 6%, drag the capital share α up to 0.45 and capital growth to the 15% maximum—watch the TFP bar flip from positive to negative (6 − 0.45×15 − 0.55×0.5 ≈ −1.0%). This is the mathematical limit of “buying growth with investment.”

gY = gTFP + α·gK + (1−α)·gL g is each variable's annual growth rate; α is capital's income share (equal to capital's output elasticity in competitive equilibrium), commonly set to 1/3; estimates for China mostly fall in the range 0.4–0.5
The real world

China's post-reform growth decomposition continues this debate: most estimates find that capital accumulation contributed about half of growth over 1978–2010, with TFP contributing roughly 2–3 percentage points a year—clearly positive, but modest beside the capital deepening of the Four Tigers at their peak. It follows that as capital deepening approaches the region of diminishing returns, a downshift in growth is nearly unavoidable: after 2012 China's growth slowed while the investment rate stayed high—precisely the macro picture of a narrowing space for “buying growth with capital.” Policy debate has accordingly shifted from “how much more investment” to “how to raise TFP”: market-based allocation of factors, open competition, education, and R&D.

Common pitfalls

(1) TFP is just technology—no: it also includes allocative efficiency (whether money and people actually flow to where productivity is highest), organization and management, scale effects, and even statistical error; it is “a measure of our ignorance.” (2) Once human capital is counted inside labor, the job is done—differences in education quality eventually show up in the residual. (3) “East Asia has no miracle” means East Asian growth was fake—wrong: that is a judgment about the source of growth, not a denial of the fact of growth. (4) Treating the “middle-income trap” as a law—it is a description of a set of stagnation cases; mechanically it is exactly this chapter's story: once accumulation-driven growth runs out, an economy must switch to TFP-driven growth.

Check yourself

Quiz 2.1An economy grows 6% a year, its capital stock grows 9%, labor input grows 1.5%, and the capital share is α = 1/3. TFP growth is approximately?
Explanation: gA = gY − α·gK − (1−α)·gL = 6 − (1/3)×9 − (2/3)×1.5 = 6 − 3 − 1 = 2.0%, so A. B is the result of subtracting only the capital contribution (forgetting labor); C is what you get misremembering α as 1/2; D equates TFP with the whole of output growth. TFP is “what is left after subtracting the factor contributions.”
Quiz 2.2Which statement about the cross-country evidence on convergence is most accurate?
Explanation: B: this is the Solow model's core prediction and a robust fact from Barro regressions—convergence is “conditional.” A confuses this with absolute convergence: in large world samples poor countries on average do not grow faster than rich ones, and the gaps are even widening. C is wrong: convergence within the OECD is relatively visible (though the early evidence drew criticism for sample selection bias). D contradicts the word “conditional”—conditional convergence says precisely that a country must first get the investment rate, human capital, and other conditions right.

Chapter summary

  • Growth accounting decomposes output growth into TFP, capital, and labor contributions: gY = gTFP + α·gK + (1−α)·gL.
  • TFP is a residual: technology, allocative efficiency, organization, and measurement error all live inside it—it measures the “quality” of growth, not merely “technology.”
  • Diminishing returns to capital mean growth from accumulation alone must eventually slow; sustainable growth depends on TFP and human capital.
  • Endogenous growth theory: nonrival knowledge → increasing returns → R&D, education, and openness can change the growth rate over the long run.
  • Convergence is conditional: economies with similar structural variables catch up fastest; “automatic convergence” does not hold.

Chapter 3Poverty traps and the Big Push

Opening question

A Kenyan farmer works hard all year and harvests barely enough to eat: no surplus means no saving, no saving means no investment, no investment means next year is subsistence again. Why does being poor replicate itself? More subtly: why does a transfer to a poor family sometimes lift it for good, and sometimes only buy one breath before it slides back to where it was? The answer hides inside an S-shaped curve.

The core intuition

A poverty trap is a self-reinforcing low-level equilibrium: low income → low saving and low investment → low income. In pictures: the horizontal axis is this generation's income y, the vertical axis the next generation's income y′ = f(y). If this mapping lies below the 45° line (y′ = y) at low incomes and rises above it only in the middle range, there are three equilibria: a low equilibrium (the trap), an unstable critical threshold, and a high equilibrium. Families starting to the left of the threshold slide back into poverty generation after generation; families that cross it climb toward prosperity—initial conditions determine destiny, and that is what “trap” means.

The trap's microeconomic mechanism is coordination failure: investments are complementary—the power plant needs factories to use its electricity, and factories need the power plant before they dare to build. Investing alone loses money, so nobody moves; moving together, everyone profits. On this basis Rosenstein-Rodan proposed the Big Push: use a one-time, coordinated, cross-sector investment to push the economy past the critical threshold. A trap is not destiny: outside aid, public health, and education act like forces that lift the entire S-curve—lift it high enough and the low equilibrium disappears.

Key concepts

Poverty trap贫困陷阱
A self-reinforcing low-level equilibrium: the income mapping and the 45° line form a multiple-equilibrium structure—a stable low equilibrium, a critical threshold, and a stable high equilibrium—and starting points below the threshold converge back into poverty over generations.
Coordination failure协调失灵
Multiple equilibria created by investment complementarity: only simultaneous action by everyone improves outcomes, unilateral action loses money, and so the economy stays at the bad equilibrium. Murphy, Shleifer, and Vishny (1989) formalized it.
The Big Push大推进
Rosenstein-Rodan's (1943) proposal: through a one-time, synchronized, multi-sector coordinated investment (infrastructure + industry + human capital), pass the critical scale and break the low-level equilibrium.
Randomized controlled trial (RCT)随机对照实验
Randomly assign an intervention to a treatment group and a control group, and estimate the causal effect from the difference in the two means. The 2019 Nobel Prize went to Banerjee, Duflo, and Kremer for bringing RCTs into development economics.

Interactive demo

Try it: first keep outside investment at 0 and drag “Initial income” from $2k to $4k—watch the staircase path switch from sliding down to climbing; then push outside investment to the maximum (0.35) and let a starting point of $2k escape too. Whether the threshold exists becomes plain at a glance.

The real world

Two famous pieces of evidence. First: China's 2013–2020 poverty alleviation campaign used a textbook combined push—registered files to identify target households, industry support, relocation from inhospitable areas, and education and health guarantees implemented together—in essence a recognition that cash transfers alone cannot cross the threshold. Second: Banerjee and coauthors ran RCTs on the “graduation” program (a productive asset + training + short-term consumption support for ultra-poor families) across six countries and found household income and consumption still significantly higher years after the intervention ended—a one-time push of “capital + capability” really can push people out of the trap. By contrast, microcredit experiments alone (Banerjee, Karlan, and Zinman's 2015 synthesis of six-country RCTs) produced only modest increases in borrowing and self-employment, with no mass escape from poverty: credit is a lubricant, not an engine.

Common pitfalls

(1) A trap equals destiny—wrong: a trap is the product of parameters (the saving rate, health, returns to education, coordination capacity), and policy can change parameters. (2) “Cash transfers are either a panacea or useless”—wrong: whether a transfer works depends on whether it pushes a family across the threshold; scale and complementary support decide the outcome. (3) The myth and the stigma of microcredit—the RCT verdict is exactly the middle view: real but limited effects. (4) RCT results can be transplanted directly to any country—external validity always asks: are the local markets, institutions, and populations similar?

Check yourself

Quiz 3.1The core logic of the “Big Push” policy proposal is?
Explanation: B: both Rosenstein-Rodan's (1943) original intuition and Murphy–Shleifer–Vishny's (1989) formalization say “complementarity + scale economies → multiple equilibria → coordinated simultaneous investment can select the better equilibrium.” A is wrong about “permanently”: the Big Push stresses a one-time push past the threshold. C reduces the problem to a savings gap and ignores the coordination structure. D is wrong because the price mechanism cannot automatically select among multiple equilibria—which is the very definition of coordination failure.
Quiz 3.2Which is the most accurate summary of the randomized controlled trial (RCT) evidence on microcredit?
Explanation: B: this is the robust conclusion of multi-country RCTs (Banerjee, Karlan, and Zinman's 2015 special issue of the American Economic Journal: Applied Economics)—the effect is real but marginal; microcredit is neither a silver bullet nor a scam. A and C contradict the evidence; D is plainly wrong: randomized expansion of credit access is a classic RCT application. Policy implication: credit must be combined with other interventions (assets, training, insurance) before the threshold can be crossed.

Chapter summary

  • A poverty trap = multiple equilibria: the S-shaped income mapping crosses the 45° line at a low equilibrium, a threshold, and a high equilibrium; the starting point decides the direction of convergence.
  • The micro mechanism is coordination failure: complementary investments require simultaneous action; unilateral action benefits no one.
  • The Big Push: a one-time, cross-sector coordinated investment pushes the economy past the threshold; the value of aid lies in lifting the S-curve, not in permanent transfusion.
  • RCTs are the gold standard for evaluating anti-poverty interventions: the evidence supports combined “asset + capability” pushes, while credit alone has limited effects.
  • A trap is not destiny—health, education, infrastructure, and coordination capacity are all changeable parameters.

Chapter 4Institutions and development

Opening question

The satellite photograph of the Korean Peninsula at night is unambiguous: south of the 38th parallel the lights blaze; to the north it is nearly pitch black. One people, the same language and culture, the same mountains and rivers—two halves artificially divided after 1945—today differ in per-capita income by more than tenfold. If geography and culture are identical, can the remaining variable—institutions—still be denied?

The core intuition

Institutions are the rules of the game: whether property rights are protected, whether contracts are enforced, whether newcomers can compete with incumbents on fair terms. The logic of incentives is plain—if what you painstakingly accumulate can be confiscated, extorted, or defaulted on at any moment, the rational choice is not to invest and not to innovate. Acemoglu and Robinson sort institutions into two great families: inclusive institutions give most people property rights and opportunity; extractive institutions let a small elite extract the surplus of the many. Under extractive institutions, coerced mobilization can deliver high growth for a time (concentrating resources on big projects), but it strangles creative destruction—because what would be destroyed is precisely the elite's own vested interests—so prosperity is hard to sustain.

The rival hypotheses are geography (tropical disease, landlockedness, climate) and culture (religion, trust, work ethic). The way to test them is to find “natural experiments”: samples such as the Korean Peninsula and East–West Germany, where geography and culture are nearly identical while institutions diverged—the variables are held constant, and the remaining difference can only be attributed to institutions.

Key concepts

Institutions制度
The rules of the game in society: formal (laws, property rights, constitutions) and informal (conventions, norms). North stressed that institutions determine the structure of incentives and therefore long-run performance.
Inclusive vs extractive institutions包容性与攫取性制度
Acemoglu–Robinson's dichotomy: the former give most people property rights, opportunity, and political voice; the latter have elites design the rules to extract the surplus. The former accompany sustained growth; the latter struggle to sustain it.
Property rights产权
The bundle of rights to use property, earn income from it, and transfer it, under credible protection. When property rights are not credible, the expected returns to investment and innovation are discounted by “expropriation risk,” and incentives collapse.
Natural experiment自然实验
A “quasi-random assignment” created by historical accident—such as the division of Korea or of Germany—that lets researchers compare the outcomes of different institutions while geography and culture are held nearly constant.

Interactive demo

Try it: click the four quadrants to compare the four social configurations formed by combining “how political power is distributed” with “whom the economic rules serve,” and note which is the most fragile and which the most durable.

The real world

China in 1978 provides a classic case of “change the rules, and every incentive changes”: the household contract responsibility system replaced “work a lot or a little, it's all the same” with “hand in enough to the state, keep enough for the collective, and whatever remains is yours,” and agricultural output surged within a few years in which land, fertilizer, and technology barely changed—in North's sense, institutional change can release productivity without a single new machine. At the global level, Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson used early European settler mortality as an instrument for early institutions, finding that colonies with high settler mortality four hundred years ago tended to establish extractive institutions, which have persisted to this day and are associated with lower incomes—work that earned the 2024 Nobel Prize in Economics.

Common pitfalls

(1) Seeing “countries with good institutions are richer” and asserting causation—institutions and income are mutually causal; identification requires natural experiments or instruments. (2) Treating the geography hypothesis as a straw man—malaria, landlockedness, and climate do affect development; the question is through what channels they work (many effects work precisely by shaping colonial institutions). (3) The unfalsifiability trap of cultural determinism—“low-trust culture” and low income may be mutually causal, and cultural variables are often endogenous. (4) Equating “inclusive” with some particular regime label—what matters is the functions: credible property rights, open opportunity, constrained power, not the form.

Check yourself

Quiz 4.1The division of the Korean Peninsula is often cited in support of the institutions hypothesis because it?
Explanation: B: the two sides of the peninsula shared virtually all geographic, historical, and cultural variables before 1945; after the division, the only systematic difference was institutions—a “quasi-experiment” with confounders controlled, so the gap must be attributed mainly to institutions. A overreaches: it shows only that geography is not the main cause on the peninsula, not that geography is irrelevant elsewhere. C contradicts the evidence. D fails: resource differences are far too small to explain a more-than-tenfold income gap and the contrast in night lights.
Quiz 4.2Acemoglu et al. (2001) used the mortality of early European settlers as an instrument. Its key assumption (the exclusion restriction) is?
Explanation: B: an instrument needs two things—relevance (colonies with high mortality were hard to settle → extractive institutions were built) and the exclusion restriction (the disease environment of that era has been tamed by modern medicine and no longer directly affects today's productivity). A states relevance, not the key assumption; C states the opposite; D has nothing to do with identification—a large sample does not cure bias. This design peels the causal chain “institutions → income” away from reverse causality and omitted variables.

Chapter summary

  • Institutions are the rules of the game: credible property rights, enforceable contracts, and open entry and competition determine the incentives to invest and innovate.
  • Inclusive institutions accompany sustained growth; extractive ones can buy temporary high growth through coerced mobilization, yet strangle creative destruction.
  • The Korean Peninsula and East–West Germany are natural experiments for the institutions hypothesis: similar geography and culture, divergent institutions, vastly different incomes.
  • The colonial-origins research used settler mortality as an instrument to identify the causal effect of institutions; the geography and culture hypotheses must compete through testable channels.
  • Institutions themselves evolve: within path dependence there are critical junctures—China's household contract system is one.

Chapter 5The economics of innovation: Schumpeter and creative destruction

Opening question

When the iPhone launched in 2007, Nokia held forty percent of the global phone market and the strongest manufacturing and distribution; five years later the giant crashed. Why does dominant innovation almost never come from incumbent giants? Why do some technologies (smartphones, mobile payments) sweep the world in a decade, while others (hydrogen fuel-cell vehicles) never break through? Half of the story of growth is not invention but diffusion.

The core intuition

Joseph Schumpeter defined innovation as new combinations: new products, new processes, new markets, new organizations. The essential fact about capitalism is creative destruction: new technologies and new firms continually replace old technologies and old firms from within—growth is not adding more old shops but letting new shops eliminate old ones. Monopoly profit plays a double role here: it is both the trophy for the innovation sprint (without excess returns, who would take the risk) and the target of the next round of competition. This creates the patent trade-off: protect too weakly, and no one will invest in R&D (others copy your result); protect too strongly, and later entrants are locked out by the “patent thicket,” and cumulative innovation actually slows—the optimal patent length is a weighed balance, not “the longer the better.”

Diffusion has its own rhythm: the S-curve. In the early phase there are only innovators and early adopters; after crossing the critical mass (about 16%), word of mouth, network effects, and falling costs ignite one another and takeoff begins; once the mainstream is exhausted comes saturation. The same technology sits at different points of the S-curve in different countries, and the latecomer's opportunity lies precisely in “jumping onto the middle of the curve.”

Key concepts

Creative destruction创造性破坏
Schumpeter's term: innovation destroys old structures from within through new combinations—new entrants replace incumbents. The core mechanism of growth and an important source of TFP; it also explains why incumbents have an incentive to obstruct innovation.
R&D race研发竞赛
Multiple firms compete to invent first: winner-takes-all (patents) pushes effort past the social optimum for “waiting,” while knowledge spillovers can leave total effort too low—two distortions in opposite directions.
Patent trade-off专利两难
Static losses (monopoly pricing, restricted use) traded for dynamic incentives (R&D returns). In cumulative innovation, overly strong patents create an “anticommons” that blocks later improvement—strength and scope must be balanced.
Technology diffusion技术扩散
The process by which a new technology is adopted across people and firms, S-shaped: slow start (nobody can use it), middle takeoff (network effects + learning curves + price declines), late saturation. Rogers's adopter categories are the classic framework.

Think about it: which technologies have an especially large r? (Hint: the stronger the network effects, the larger r.)

The real world

China's high-speed rail is a diffusion showcase of “introduce—digest—absorb—re-innovate”: from 2004 it imported trainsets from several countries by trading technology for market access, rode the world's largest unified market down the learning curve, and ultimately built the world's longest high-speed rail network and began exporting it. It displays the latecomer's S-curve strategy: not inventing from zero, but standing at the middle of the curve and trading market scale for learning speed. The contrast on the patent side is pharmaceuticals: after the TRIPS agreement globalized drug patents, India built a powerful generic-drug industry on the strength of its “transition period before patent grants”; the 2001 Doha Declaration confirmed compulsory licensing in a public health crisis—the patent trade-off being re-weighed again and again in a market where life is at stake.

Common pitfalls

(1) More patents mean stronger innovation—high patent density can also signal thickets and strategic filing; what matters is quality and diffusion. (2) Invention equals innovation—engineering, distribution channels, and business models stand between invention and commercialization; half a century separates Turing from the smartphone. (3) Large R&D spending guarantees strength—the direction of allocation and competitive pressure determine what spending yields; subsidies without exit mechanisms often nourish “professional R&D rent-seekers.” (4) Basic research should be left to firms—the spillovers from basic knowledge are too strong for firms to appropriate the returns; that is the efficiency rationale, not a romantic one, for governments everywhere to fund basic science.

Check yourself

Quiz 5.1Schumpeter's “creative destruction” refers to?
Explanation: B: this is the core concept of Schumpeter's Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy—innovation replaces old structures with new combinations (the iPhone replacing Nokia), so growth is essentially dynamic competition rather than static efficiency; at the same time it explains why incumbents (such as displaced giants) have strong incentives to lobby, litigate, and lock in users to obstruct innovation. A is cartel behavior; C is a cyclical phenomenon, not Schumpeter's original meaning; D is antitrust policy, unrelated to the “creation” side of innovation.
Quiz 5.2Which statement best captures the trade-off over the strength of patent protection?
Explanation: B: the two ends of the patent trade-off—with too little protection, no one invests in R&D (results are imitated immediately); with too much, “standing on the shoulders of giants” costs too much, and patent thicket and anticommons problems follow. A and C ignore the static losses and the blocking effect in cumulative innovation; D is plainly wrong: software, machinery, and biotechnology all depend deeply on the balance between patents and trade secrets.

Chapter summary

  • Innovation = new combinations; creative destruction is the core mechanism of growth: new entrants replace incumbents, and TFP comes from there.
  • Monopoly profit cuts both ways: the trophy for the innovation sprint and the target of the next round of competition; innovation incentives are strongest under moderate competition (inverted U).
  • The patent trade-off: dynamic incentives vs. static monopoly losses; overly strong patents in cumulative innovation block later improvement.
  • Diffusion follows an S-curve: slow start, takeoff after crossing the critical mass of about 16%, saturation at the end; the imitation intensity r sets the steepness.
  • The latecomer's opportunity: stand at the middle of the S-curve and trade market scale for learning speed—high-speed rail and mobile payments are both examples.

Chapter 6Demographic transition and structural change

Opening question

Before 1800, the whole world lived in a Malthusian regime of “many births, many deaths”: income per head barely grew, and the fruits of any technical progress were ultimately shared out among ever more people. Today birth rates are falling in almost every country, and in 2022 China recorded its first negative population growth in more than sixty years. Is population an engine of growth, a burden, or both at once? The answer depends on where a country stands in the demographic transition.

The core intuition

The demographic transition has four stages: high birth rate and high death rate (growth near zero) → the death rate falls first (population explosion) → the birth rate follows it down (growth slows) → both low (growth reaches zero, population ages). The key is the lag: public health and vaccines pull the death rate down first, while fertility norms take longer to change—and that half-beat gap is the baby boom. When the baby-boom generation reaches working age, the dependency ratio is at its lowest and the saving rate at its highest—the demographic dividend; when the window closes, the same cohort grows old, and the dividend turns into pension pressure.

Interwoven with it is structural transformation: employment and output migrate in turn from agriculture → industry → services, driven behind the scenes by Engel's law (the share of food in spending falls once people are fed) and by differences in productivity growth across sectors. Lewis's classic depiction: early in development the countryside holds “surplus labor” whose marginal product is very low, and industry can absorb it “without limit” at a subsistence wage; the moment the surplus is exhausted—the Lewis turning point—wages are set by labor's marginal product and begin to rise faster.

Key concepts

Demographic transition人口转型
The historical process in which the birth rate and the death rate fall one after the other from high levels, in four stages; the lag between the fall in death rates and the fall in birth rates produces the population explosion and later aging.
Demographic dividend人口红利
The growth boost from a rising share of working-age population and a falling dependency ratio during the transition. The dividend has a window, and it takes education and job creation to cash it.
Lewis turning point刘易斯拐点
The point at which rural surplus labor shifts from “unlimited supply” to scarcity: agriculture's marginal product rises, industrial wages accelerate upward with the marginal product, and the labor-intensive export model faces restructuring.
Structural transformation结构转型
The long-run shift of the center of gravity of output and employment along agriculture → manufacturing → services, jointly driven by the structure of demand (Engel's law) and sectoral productivity differences.

The real world

Around 2010, persistent “labor shortages” appeared in coastal Chinese manufacturing and migrant workers' wages rose by double digits for years on end; the economist Cai Fang and others judged from this that the Lewis turning point had arrived—the “unlimited supply” of surplus labor was over, labor-intensive industry had to move inland or upgrade, and over the same period investment in robotic substitution and automation visibly accelerated. The contrast on the other side is India: in 2023 it overtook China as the world's most populous country, with a median age about ten years lower, standing inside the dividend window—but the dividend does not cash itself; it depends on whether enough jobs can be created each year for the vast flow of new workers, which is exactly the core question of structural transformation policy.

Common pitfalls

(1) A big population means poor—scale alone does not decide wealth or poverty: the most densely populated regions contain both the richest and the poorest economies; what matters is human capital and structure. (2) The demographic dividend cashes itself—without education and job absorption, a young population is merely unemployment pressure. (3) Aging is only a rich-country problem—China is “growing old before growing rich,” its transition running faster than its income level. (4) Fertility falls with economic development alone—access to contraception, women's education, and child mortality all play causal roles. And do not forget Baumol's warning: productivity rises slowly in services, so a servicifying economy is not necessarily “degenerating,” but it does slow overall growth.

Check yourself

Quiz 6.1The “population explosion” in stage two of the demographic transition is directly caused by?
Explanation: B: the transition's lag is the key—vaccines, clean water, and antibiotics cut the death rate first, while fertility, driven by norms, education, and economic motives, adjusts far more slowly; the gap between the two lines widens to its maximum and the natural growth rate spikes. A need not hold: in stage two the birth rate can even be slightly higher than in stage one (better nutrition and medicine raised survival rates). C and D contradict the historical record—the explosion occurred in developing countries with no immigration and no pro-natalist policies.
Quiz 6.2The “Lewis turning point” refers to?
Explanation: C: in the Lewis (1954) model, early in development agriculture holds surplus labor with marginal product near zero, and industry can hire “without limit” at a wage slightly above subsistence; once the surplus is exhausted, industry must bid above agriculture's marginal product to hire more—the wage curve bends from flat to steep. A and B are statistical thresholds, not economic mechanisms; D is a stage-four event of the demographic transition, not synonymous with this labor-market turn.

Chapter summary

  • The demographic transition has four stages: high birth and high death → the death rate falls first (population explosion) → the birth rate follows down → both low, with aging.
  • The lag between the falls of the death rate and the birth rate creates the baby boom; when the boom reaches working age, that is the demographic dividend; when the window closes, aging.
  • The Lewis turning point: surplus labor is exhausted, wages switch from subsistence-level to marginal-product pricing, and the labor-intensive model faces restructuring.
  • Structural transformation proceeds along agriculture → industry → services, jointly driven by Engel's law and sectoral productivity differences.
  • Whether population is a burden or an engine depends on the stage of the transition and on policy's capacity to turn people into human capital.
