Political Economy & Institutional Economics

政治经济学与制度经济学

Prices allocate resources—but what allocates prices? Every market exchange happens inside rules: who owns what, whether promises bind, and whose side the state is on. This track studies where these "rules of the game"—institutions—come from, how they change, and why some societies ride them to prosperity while others are locked into poverty by them.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • Distinguish formal from informal institutions, and explain how property rights and contract enforcement determine a society's "radius of exchange"
  • Use transaction costs to draw the line between "make" and "buy," and answer Coase's question: why do firms exist?
  • Use increasing returns and critical junctures to explain path dependence, and judge which reforms get locked in place and which can still change course
  • Use Olson's logic of collective action to predict which groups win in policy competition, and compute the social cost of rent-seeking
  • Explain how the median voter, the political business cycle, and state capacity jointly shape a country's policy quality
  • Compare planning with markets and limited access with open access social orders, and understand the different fates of transition
Prerequisites: 01 Micro (market failure, marginal analysis) 04 Game Theory (Nash equilibrium, prisoner's dilemma)

Chapter 1Institutions: Rules, Property Rights, and Contracts

Opening question: The same vending machine stands unguarded and untouched on a Tokyo street, yet in certain other cities it must be welded inside a steel cage and watched by cameras. The machine has not changed, the drinks have not changed—so what has? Push the thought further: why can a slip of paper (a bill of lading) persuade two merchants ten thousand miles apart, who have never met, to entrust cargoes worth hundreds of millions of yuan to an ocean-going freighter? The answer lies not in the goods but in the rules behind them.

The core intuition

Douglass North gave the classic definition: institutions are the rules of the game in society—humanly devised constraints that shape how people interact. Economics usually assumes that "people optimize subject to constraints"—price constraints, budget constraints; institutional economics asks the prior question: where do the constraints themselves come from?

The key to understanding institutions is uncertainty. If I cannot be confident that goods ordered today arrive tomorrow, that money lent out comes back, that what I sow on my land I will reap, then every long-range transaction, investment, and collaboration shrinks. Good institutions do not create resources; they stretch the radius of cooperation among strangers—they let people ten thousand miles apart dare to sign contracts, and people who have never met dare to lend. Bad institutions (or their absence) compress that radius until people dare do business only with acquaintances.

Key concepts

Institutions
制度
The humanly devised rules of the game in society: the constraints and incentives that govern political and economic activity, comprising formal rules (laws, regulations) and informal constraints (customs, norms, reputation), together with their enforcement characteristics.
Property Rights
产权
The bundle of rights to decide how a resource is used, who collects its returns, and how it is transferred. Clear and enforceable property rights are the precondition for price signals to work—what belongs to no one is neither well cared for nor efficiently traded.
Contract Enforcement
契约执行机制
The whole set of arrangements that make promises credible: courts, arbitration, collateral, reputation networks, platform ratings. The radius of exchange ≈ the radius over which contracts can be enforced.
Transaction Costs
交易成本
All the costs of reaching and completing a transaction: finding partners, negotiating terms, monitoring performance, punishing breach. Coase identified them as the key to understanding firms, markets, and economic organization at large.
Formal / Informal Institutions
正式 / 非正式制度
Formal institutions are rules written down and enforced by dedicated bodies; informal institutions are behavioral norms upheld by social pressure and reputation. The two can complement—or undermine—each other.

Unpacked, three pillars hold up the market:

  • Defining property rights—only once "whose is this?" is settled is exchange possible. Poorly defined resources (open-sea fisheries, the atmosphere) get overused (see the tragedy of the commons in Track 12, Environmental and Resource Economics).
  • Enforcing contracts—promises are credible only when breach carries a foreseeable price. Enforcement can run through state courts, or through reputation networks, merchant guilds, or platform ratings.
  • Stable rules—a rule set today still counts tomorrow. Constant policy reversals amount to disguised expropriation; people shorten investment horizons and hide assets.

Note an important symmetry: markets need the state, and the state can destroy markets. Without a state, property rights rest on private protection (costly and narrow in reach); with a state that is too strong and unconstrained, property rights may be violated by the state itself. This dilemma runs through the whole track and is confronted head-on in Chapter 6.

The real world

Shanxi piaohao and the bill of lading: contract enforcement in two eras. The Shanxi piaohao banks of the Qing dynasty remitted funds "across the realm" relying not on courts but on the apprenticeship system, native-place networks, and "body-share" (shengu) incentives that made employees part-owners—a clerk caught cheating was expelled from the entire trade, and reputation did the work of law. In today's international trade, a bill of lading plus a letter of credit lets buyer and seller rest their trust on a chain of institutions built from banks and shipping custom. The technology has changed; the logic has not: whoever can make promises credible can do big deals.

Common pitfalls

"Institutions = the laws written on paper."—Many countries have beautifully drafted constitutions and corporate codes aligned with international standards, yet court queues run a decade and judgments are never enforced. The institutions that actually work are the rules that are expected and enforced; the paper text is only their shadow. To judge a country's institutions, do not look at what has been proclaimed—look at whether defaulters pay a price, and whether investors dare keep their money onshore. Another common misreading treats institutions as pure "shackles": good institutions are precisely the scaffolding of cooperation—traffic rules constrain you, and they let everyone else drive with confidence.

Quiz 1.1A merchant is willing to ship goods on credit to a customer ten thousand miles away whom he has never met. The most critical institutional support for this is:
Explanation: Selling on credit is in essence the business of making the promise "goods go out first, money comes back later" credible, and what does that is the cost of breach. A is price control, which usually wrecks incentives instead; C affects only the convenience of exchanging currencies and has nothing to do with trust. Remember this chapter's motto: the radius of exchange ≈ the radius over which contracts can be enforced.
Quiz 1.2Which statement about informal institutions—customs, reputation, relationship networks—is most accurate?
Explanation: The piaohao enforced contracts through reputation networks, which shows informal institutions are the real thing (A is wrong); but reputation mechanisms rely on repeated interaction and identifiable identities, so they have a natural boundary—a modern market economy networks billions of strangers and must have formal institutions such as courts, registries, and credit bureaus (B is right). C is the opposite of reality: when law conflicts with custom, the frequent outcome is "the law stands still, custom carries on"—precisely why many institutional transplants fail.

Chapter summary

  • Institutions = society's rules of the game; their function is to bring uncertainty down and stretch the radius of cooperation.
  • Markets stand on three pillars: defined property rights, contract enforcement, and stable rules; lose any one and exchange shrinks.
  • Formal and informal institutions can complement or fight each other; the institutions that matter are the rules that get enforced.
  • Markets need the state to protect property rights yet fear the state violating them—this dilemma is the through-line of the whole track.

Chapter 2Transaction Costs and the Boundaries of the Firm

Opening question: If markets really work as well as the textbooks say, why is not everyone a sole proprietor, with all production stitched together by market purchases? Inside the company you work for is plainly a small patch of "central planning"—the boss gives orders, reports climb layer by layer, resources move through administrative procedures—so why has market competition not eliminated it? That is the question 21-year-old Ronald Coase asked in his 1937 article "The Nature of the Firm" (The Nature of the Firm), and the answer won him a Nobel Prize.

The core intuition

When you buy something on the market, the price is only part of the bill: first you must find a reliable supplier (search), then haggle and sign a contract (negotiation), after delivery you must guard against being fobbed off with inferior goods (monitoring), and when disputes arise you go to court (enforcement)—all of this is transaction cost. When transaction costs run too high, people simply move the transaction inside the firm: nothing needs to be negotiated and priced item by item, one "go do it" suffices—authority replaces price.

But the firm is not free. As hierarchies multiply, information distorts at every layer, employees earn the same however much they do (weakened incentives), and departments fight for budgets and dodge blame (bureaucratization). Coase's answer in one sentence: the boundary of the firm is drawn where "the marginal cost of organizing one more transaction inside the firm" catches up with "the marginal cost of organizing the same transaction through the market." Oliver Williamson refined it: the key variable is asset specificity—a highly specialized investment customized for one counterparty leaves the investor exposed to being squeezed after the fact (hold-up), so people protect such investments with long-term contracts, equity stakes, and mergers.

Key concepts

Boundaries of the Firm
企业的边界
Which transactions live inside the firm (make) and which are left to the market (buy). Coase: the boundary sits at the marginal balance between market transaction costs and the costs of internal organization.
Asset Specificity
资产专用性
The degree to which an asset is customized to a particular trading partner. The more specific the asset, the greater the value lost by redeploying it and the greater the risk of being price-squeezed by the other side after the fact.
Hold-up Problem
要挟问题
Opportunistic behavior in which, after one party has made a specific investment, the other "holds it up" and grabs the returns of that investment. Anticipating hold-up, specific investment is under-supplied.
Vertical Integration
纵向一体化
Buying an upstream or downstream transaction out of the market and into the firm, replacing repeated negotiation with authority and long-term employment—the classic response to the hold-up problem (at a price in managerial overhead).

Write Coase's logic as a formula. Let t be the transaction cost per purchase on the market (negotiating, inspecting, guarding against breach), m the marginal cost of internal management per transaction inside the firm, and F the fixed organization cost the firm must amortize up front. Then the point where the two total-cost lines cross is the optimal scale:

q* = F / (t − m) q*: the volume of transactions the firm should internalize (= firm size); F: the excess of the firm's fixed organization cost over the fixed cost of buying on the market; a solution exists only when t > m

This simple expression generates a string of testable predictions: cheaper market transactions (better courts, e-commerce platforms, standard contracts) shrink the boundaries of the firm—outsourcing and specialization flourish; higher asset specificity (dedicated equipment, custom software) expands them—more integration.

The real world

Fisher Body and General Motors: the textbook case of integration. In the early twentieth century GM purchased closed car bodies from Fisher Body, with dies and production lines customized to GM's models—textbook specific assets. However the contract was written, one side felt exposed: when demand boomed, Fisher could plead "rising costs" and raise prices (hold-up); when demand slumped, GM could squeeze its orders. In 1926 GM simply bought Fisher and moved the transaction inside the firm. The modern contrast is Apple and Foxconn: Apple outsources manufacturing (the market) yet "contractualizes" the specific investments through exclusive capacity agreements, resident engineers, and prepayments for equipment—integration is not the only answer, just the heaviest tool in the box.

Common pitfalls

"Since markets have transaction costs, the bigger the firm the better."—This sees the thief eating his meat but not the beatings he takes. Inside the firm there are costs too: hierarchical distortion, weakened incentives, big-company disease; once internal management cost exceeds market transaction cost, expansion is burning money. In the other direction, digitalization (e-commerce platforms, online price comparison, cloud SaaS, cross-border payments) has pushed t sharply down, and we see firm boundaries contract: brands outsource manufacturing, then design, then IT—not because firms have grown weaker, but because the market has grown stronger. Coase's 1937 framework predicted the gig economy of ninety years later with uncanny accuracy.

Quiz 2.1What is the core of Coase's answer to "why do firms exist"?
Explanation: Coase's insight is a cost comparison between two modes of governance: the market uses price, the firm uses authority, and each has its own cost curve. B is the precise statement of that marginal condition. A treats the firm as a tax shelter and cannot explain firms with no taxes to dodge; C has the direction backwards—incentives inside firms are usually weaker than in the market (pay is flat however much you do), which is itself one of the costs of firm expansion.
Quiz 2.2To produce custom parts for you, a supplier invests in a production line that can only be used for your product. What does the Williamson framework predict will most likely happen next?
Explanation: The direction of hold-up depends on who is locked in: once the supplier has built the dedicated line, the asset is worthless in any other use, and the bargaining table tilts toward you—you can squeeze the price. But look one step ahead in the game: foreseeing the squeeze, the supplier dare not invest in the first place, or demands a premium up front—that is under-investment in specific assets. The Fisher Body case was caught in exactly this two-way bind, and GM ultimately chose integration. B has the direction reversed.

Chapter summary

  • The firm = the "island of planning" where authority replaces price; it exists because markets have transaction costs.
  • Coase's boundary condition: marginal cost of organizing a transaction in-house = marginal cost of organizing it through the market.
  • Williamson's three ingredients: bounded rationality, opportunism, asset specificity → hold-up → long-term contracts / integration.
  • Technologies and the rule of law that lower transaction costs make the market stronger and firm boundaries contract—the institutional account of outsourcing and the platform economy.

Chapter 3Institutional Change and Path Dependence

Opening question: The QWERTY keyboard layout was born in the age of typewriters, allegedly designed on purpose to slow typing down and keep the keys from jamming—the mechanical typewriter long since went to the museum, so why does the whole world still use it? Scale up to a country: why do many economies "know" perfectly well what better institutions look like, yet spin in place for decades, while others can change course overnight in a crisis?

The core intuition

North's framework of institutional change has just two beats: gradual change and rupture. Most of the time, institutions shift course slowly, like a river—organizations (firms, unions, bureaucracies, parties) learn, invest, and evolve under the existing rules, and the rules are worn bit by bit into new shapes. Occasionally, a critical juncture—war, revolution, great crisis—suddenly blows a window open, and the choices a society makes within a few years set the tone for decades to come.

Why is change so hard once the window closes? Because institutions carry increasing returns: the more people use them, the cheaper, more practiced, and more credible they become—coordination effects, learning effects, and network effects pile up in layers into path dependence and lock-in. At that point, the total cost of switching to the "better" institution may be so high that no one is willing to move first. Small events, compounded by the interest of increasing returns, grow into large configurations—that is why historical detail matters, and why "photocopying the list of optimal institutions" so often fails.

Key concepts

Path Dependence
路径依赖
Today's feasible choice set is locked in by yesterday's choices: small early differences, amplified by increasing returns, determine the long-run equilibrium. It is not "historical determinism," but "history with very high switching costs."
Increasing Returns
报酬递增
The more people adopt an institution or technology, the larger the net benefit of continuing to adopt it (four mechanisms: network, learning, coordination, adaptive expectations).
Critical Juncture
关键节点
A brief window in which an old equilibrium is broken by an exogenous shock—war, crisis, revolution—when the choices of a small group can reset the long-run path (the Meiji Restoration, postwar reforms, China's 1978 reform and opening-up).
Institutional Drift
制度漂移
The environment changes slowly while institutions stand still; the gap between them keeps accumulating until some shock tears it open into change.
Historical footnote

The QWERTY puzzle and its critics. Paul David used the QWERTY keyboard in 1985 to teach path dependence: a mechanical anti-jamming design from 1873 plus the training investments of typing schools locked in the layout; ever after, even though layouts such as Dvorak claimed to be more efficient, the switching cost (retraining the fingers of billions) was unreachably high, so the "second-best" won. This is the most famous parable of path dependence, but it is empirically contested—some studies hold that Dvorak's advantage was exaggerated. Use it as a fable of the mechanism, not a measured fact: what matters is not the keyboard but the logic "small early event × increasing returns = large long-run outcome."

Institutional change has one more source of stickiness: vested interests. Rules shape the organizations that grow up inside them, and those organizations in turn spend resources defending the rules. Ministries and state enterprises under the planned system, property chains under land institutions, incumbents under licensing regimes—even when a new institution is better for society as a whole, the moment it touches some powerful organization's "rents," change meets systematic resistance. This is precisely Olson's territory, the subject of the next chapter.

The real world

The dual-track system: gradual change straight out of the textbook. After 1978, China did not free prices overnight; it opened a market track alongside the plan track: the same product was allocated at official prices inside the plan and could trade at market prices outside it. Household contracting in the villages, special economic zones, and the pilot–evaluate–roll-out cycle were all ways of "growing the new institution beside the old one." The virtue was that nobody was smashed at once (incumbents had no reason to fight to the death), while new stakeholders (self-employed households, foreign investors, township and village enterprises) kept growing, until the market track swallowed the plan track. The price was a window of arbitrage and corruption between the tracks. Contrast Soviet shock therapy: privatization overnight, but the enforcing institutions (rule of law, statistics, banking supervision) never caught up, and the old bureaucrats transformed themselves into oligarchs—tearing down the old institutions does not make new ones grow automatically.

Common pitfalls

"Path dependence = fatalism: backward countries can only accept their fate."—This misreads "high switching costs" as "impossible." History offers plenty of counterexamples: Japan's Meiji Restoration, the transformations of South Korea and Taiwan, China's reform and opening-up—all cases of deliberately changing course at a critical juncture. The correct use of path dependence is as a reminder: reform must price the switching costs, design compensation, and leave vested interests a way out, not declare the problem unsolvable. Another pitfall is "cultural determinism"—ascribing everything to "national character," when in fact the same culture can produce radically different institutional outcomes (compare the two Koreas, the two Germanys, or China before and after reform).

Quiz 3.1What is the core mechanism by which increasing returns lock in an institution?
Explanation: Lock-in needs no ban, only "whoever moves first loses": when everyone uses the same set of rules, coordination effects make the unilateral deviator pay a huge price—even when an alternative better for society exists (QWERTY, left-hand traffic, and plug standards are all examples). A is the coercive case, a special instance; C has nothing to do with lock-in—what gets locked in may be precisely an inferior equilibrium, and that is exactly what makes lock-in frightening.
Quiz 3.2In the debate between "shock therapy" and "gradual reform," what is the most insightful conclusion from institutional economics?
Explanation: The post-Soviet lesson is exactly B: prices were freed, but without antitrust and the rule of law privatization became an insiders' carve-up; the currency was freed, but without reliable banking supervision savings were looted. The relative success of China's dual track confirms B too—it was not "optimal" at every step, but the pace of each step roughly matched the capacity to execute it. A treats "the market" as a switch rather than an ecosystem; C ignores how much sequencing and packaging matter in transition.

Chapter summary

  • Institutional change = gradual evolution most of the time + discontinuous choice at critical junctures.
  • Increasing returns (coordination, learning, network, adaptive expectations) produce path dependence and lock-in.
  • Organizations with vested interests defend the rules that shaped them; reform must design transition paths and compensation.
  • The divide between gradualism (dual track) and shock (big bang) is whether institutional support can keep pace with the speed of change.

Chapter 4Collective Action and Rent-Seeking

Opening question: A tariff costs 1.4 billion consumers 10 yuan each per year (14 billion yuan in total), yet delivers extra millions to the shareholders and workers of each of a few thousand protected firms. The losing side outnumbers the winners by a factor of ten thousand and loses more in total—so why does the "majority" keep losing the policy fight? Why can't "the people" beat "the special interests"? Mancur Olson's 1965 The Logic of Collective Action still gives the best answer.

The core intuition

Olson's starting point is disarmingly simple: winning a collective good for a group is itself the production of a public good. Once the tariff is repealed, the 10 yuan each consumer saves does not grow by a single cent because "you marched"—the gains are spread thin across every head, while the costs of organizing, lobbying, and petitioning land on you alone. So everyone waits for somebody else to do the fighting and free rides. The larger the group and the smaller the per-capita stake, the more rational free riding becomes—and the less possible collective action.

In the other direction, a trade association of a few thousand firms carries an enormous per-capita stake, members can easily watch one another, and organization is cheap—small "privileged groups" act in unison by nature. The policy market thus develops a systematic mismatch of supply and demand: concentrated benefits versus diffuse costs, which is why protectionism, subsidies, licenses, and entry barriers never die. Gordon Tullock drove the blade home: when people scramble for such artificially created transfers (rents), the lobbying, public relations, queuing, and bribery all burn real resources—this is rent-seeking; it creates no output, it only decides who gets the output.

Key concepts

Collective Action Problem
集体行动问题
The predicament in which rational individuals will not contribute to the group's interest, so an outcome better for everyone fails to materialize (an N-person prisoner's dilemma).
Free Riding
搭便车
The strategy of sharing in the gains of collective action without bearing any of the cost. The larger the group and the harder contributions are to observe, the more widespread free riding.
Selective Incentives
选择性激励
Olson's remedy: bundle the collective good with private benefits only members enjoy (journals, insurance, networks, punishment of defectors), so that even large groups can organize.
Rent-Seeking
寻租
Activity that spends real resources competing for artificial transfers (monopoly, tariffs, subsidies, licenses). Proposed by Tullock, named and popularized by Krueger.
Rent Dissipation
租金耗散
When rent-seekers compete with each other, the total resources spent contesting the rent approach—and in equilibrium may equal—the rent itself, so society's net loss widens from the "triangle" to "triangle + rectangle."

First, compute free riding in the simplest "volunteer's dilemma": n people all want the public good provided (worth B to each), and it takes at least one person stepping forward to bear the cost c (B > c). Everyone's private calculation is "let someone else do it"—in equilibrium each person steps up only with probability p, and p plummets as group size n grows:

p = 1 − (c/B)1/(n−1) in the mixed-strategy equilibrium each person is indifferent between "stepping up" and "free riding"; p approaches 0 as n grows, and the probability that the public good goes unprovided approaches c/B

The rent-seeking half can be computed just as cleanly. When a monopolist lifts its price, the traditional analysis counts only the "Harberger triangle" (deadweight loss); but Tullock asked: where does the monopoly position itself come from? If it was won by lobbying, then under free entry into rent-seeking competition, the resources spent contesting it swell until they have eaten the entire "Tullock rectangle" (monopoly profit):

dissipation share = (n − 1) / n → the larger n, the closer dissipation to 100% n evenly matched rent-seekers (Tullock's lottery model); as n → ∞, total social loss = the deadweight-loss triangle + the entire monopoly-rent rectangle (the Posner proposition)
The real world

The sugar quota: Olson and Tullock bound in one volume. The United States has long protected its sugar growers with import quotas; the standard textbook estimate puts the cost to consumers at roughly 3–4 billion dollars a year—spread over every consumer, a dozen-odd dollars apiece, and no one organizes a lobbying campaign or pores over voting records for a dozen dollars. The benefiting sugar mills and cane growers, by contrast, are concentrated in a handful of states, number in the tens of thousands, and gain tens of thousands of dollars per head—they have a trade association, a lobbying budget, and votes to bargain with. The result: this protection has stood for decades, weathering every change of party in power. Taxi medallions are another example: the capped license supply manufactures "rents" worth millions, and around them flourish queuing, speculation, lobbying, and a rent-seeking boom—passengers pay more while society's taxi service improves not at all.

Common pitfalls

First, "monopoly's social loss is just that triangle."—If the monopoly position comes from regulation and protection, the rectangular rent is dissipated by rent-seeking competition, and the true loss far exceeds the triangle. Second, "lobbying at least transmits information, so it is not waste."—The information-transmitting part may carry value, but when the reward structure pays for "contesting transfers" rather than "creating output," the brightest people queue, glad-hand, and game the approval process instead of doing R&D and production—that misallocation of human capital is an invisible loss. Third, "rent-seeking = corruption."—Rent-seeking can be entirely legal: compliant lobbying, campaign contributions, and maneuvering over standards are all rent-seeking; legality does not change the essence of "spending resources to contest transfers."

Quiz 4.1By Olson's logic of collective action, which kind of group is most likely to win in policy competition?
Explanation: Victory in the policy market depends not on headcount or merit but on the balance between organization costs and per-capita stakes. Consumers number in the tens of millions but lose only a dozen-odd yuan each—free riding is rational, and they cannot organize; the trade association is small but each member's stake is huge, members are visible to one another and defectors easy to punish—so it acts in unison. That is the mechanism of "concentrated interests defeating diffuse costs." A overrates headcount; C conflates normative analysis with positive prediction—"should win" is not "will win."
Quiz 4.2In the Tullock–Posner framework, the total social loss caused by monopoly should include:
Explanation: The crucial distinction is transfer versus dissipation: the rectangle passes from consumers to the monopolist, and if the monopoly position fell from the sky it really is only a transfer (under that assumption A is not wrong); but real-world monopoly positions usually have to be contested and defended by rent-seeking, and Tullock's lottery model shows that n competitors' equilibrium total outlay is (n−1)/n times the rent—those outlays are real resources, and burning them is a net loss. C denies even the triangle (output that could have been produced, gone forever), which is the furthest wrong of all.

Chapter summary

  • Winning a collective good = producing a public good: free riding paralyzes large groups while small, concentrated groups win.
  • The remedies: selective incentives, representation, compulsory taxation—turning "should" into "worth my while."
  • Rent-seeking spends real resources contesting artificial transfers; competition drives rents toward full dissipation.
  • Monopoly's social cost = the triangle + the dissipated rectangle; when evaluating regulation, first ask "who gets the rent, and how much gets burned contesting it."

Chapter 5Politics and Economic Performance

Opening question: With universal elections alike, why do some countries vote their way to prosperity and stability while others vote their way to triple-digit inflation? Why are policies "everyone knows are wrong"—subsidies showered before the vote, money printed to build roads—nearly impossible to defeat before election day? Politics is not the backdrop of the economy; it is itself a policy machine. This chapter opens the casing and inspects its three gears: votes, cycles, and state capacity.

The core intuition

Gear one: the median voter. In Downs's model of two-party competition, voters line up by policy preference; both parties want to win, so both court the voter in the exact middle—equilibrium policy converges on the median voter's most-preferred point, not the mean and not the optimum. This seemingly humdrum result is astonishingly powerful: whoever moves the median voter (through immigration, education, media, demographics) moves policy. Substitute the income distribution and you have the Meltzer–Richard model: when the income distribution is right-skewed, the median voter is poorer than the average and votes for progressive taxes and transfers—the more unequal the distribution, the greater the pressure for redistribution.

Gear two: the political business cycle. The rhythm of stimulus before the election and tightening after comes from the short-run Phillips curve (see Track 05, Money and Banking) plus a commitment problem: the politician's objective function (reelection) is time-inconsistent with voter welfare, so the expansion and inflation of the "election economy" become institutionally periodic. The prescription is institutional too: an independent central bank, fiscal rules, term limits. Gear three: state capacity. Even the best policy needs someone who can actually tax, count, and enforce—an incompetent state is as lethal as an unconstrained one; the ideal is a state that is "strong and leashed."

Key concepts

Median Voter Theorem
中位选民定理
Under single-peaked preferences and two-party (or majority-runoff) competition, equilibrium policy converges to the most-preferred position of the median voter.
Political Business Cycle
政治商业周期
Output–inflation fluctuations created by incumbents steering macro policy for electoral gain: expansion before the election, tightening after (Nordhaus's opportunistic model).
State Capacity
国家能力
The state's actual ability to carry out its policy intentions: the capacity to tax and to compile statistics, a bureaucracy that can execute, and the capacity to uphold the rule of law and protect property.
Time Inconsistency
时间不一致性
The predicament of a policy promise that is optimal ex ante yet abandoned once ex post incentives take over. One root of the political cycle; the remedy is institutions that bind your future self (rules, independent agencies).
Key point

The state-capacity dilemma: capability × constraint in four combinations. Incapable and unconstrained (Somalia-style disorder), capable and unconstrained (a predation machine), incapable but constrained (wants to act and cannot), capable and constrained (the modern fiscal state). Recent development economics keeps finding that the poor country's problem is often not that "the government does bad things" but that the government cannot do things—it cannot collect taxes, cannot count its population, cannot enforce its judgments. Without capability, the finest institutional design stays on paper; without constraint, capability itself is a hazardous good. "Strong and leashed" is the narrow road the Prussian–Scandinavian line of fiscal-military states walked over two hundred years.

The real world

Argentina: a century-long specimen of the political business cycle. In 1913 Argentina's income per person ranked among the world's highest, and Buenos Aires was called "the Paris of South America." For the century since, it has replayed the same drama: fiscal expansion, subsidies, and wage hikes before elections; inflation out of control, default and devaluation after. Since Peronism, the "redistribution–inflation–crisis–austerity" loop has become institutionalized path dependence (Chapter 3's lock-in): the central bank has long lacked independence, and the time-inconsistency problem has never been solved institutionally. Australia and Canada, equally resource-rich large countries, took the other road that rests on the rule of law and the central bank. A century later, that institutional difference is a several-fold gap in income levels.

Common pitfalls

"Democracy automatically brings growth" or "authoritarianism automatically brings growth."—Both simple stories are wrong. Authoritarian developmentalism has produced the strong records of South Korea and Singapore and the disasters of Zaire and Haiti—the difference lies not in authority itself but in whether authority is used to protect property rights or to grab them; democracy has produced both India's long inefficiency and postwar Western Europe's miracle. Empirical work (such as Acemoglu et al., 2019, "Democracy Does Cause Growth") finds that democracy's long-run growth effect is positive on average, but the mechanisms are complex and initial conditions matter. Asking whether "democracy / authoritarianism," as a single variable, is good is like asking whether "medicine" is good—without asking about dose, disease, or what it is combined with.

Quiz 5.1The median voter theorem predicts that two parties' policies converge toward the middle. What is the key premise for this to hold?
Explanation: Single-peaked preferences guarantee each person a unique optimum and make "the middle" attractive to both ends; when both parties can position freely, whichever drifts from the median sees the middle ground seized by its opponent, so both converge. A affects only the technical detail of ties; C is simply irrelevant. One real-world caution: when policy is multidimensional (economics plus cultural issues), or when primaries, strategic vote-dropping, or turnout differences enter, convergence loosens—the theorem is a benchmark, not an iron law.
Quiz 5.2What is the root cause of the recurring opportunistic political business cycle (pre-election expansion, post-election tightening)?
Explanation: The cycle needs two conditions to hold at once: a short-run macro trade-off to exploit (with expectations unadjusted, stimulus buys growth and jobs) and a political motive to exploit it (the votes are counted now, while the inflation bill is mailed after the election). This is a textbook case of time inconsistency: the ex ante promise "never a pre-election stimulus" is not credible ex post. That is why the prescription is institutional—an independent central bank, fiscal rules, transparent statistics. A underestimates the power of incentives; C has it exactly backwards: central bank independence is the medicine for this disease.

Chapter summary

  • Under two-party competition, policy converges on the median voter; moving the median voter = moving policy.
  • The more right-skewed the income distribution, the further the median falls below the mean, and the greater the redistribution pressure.
  • The political business cycle = short-run trade-off + time inconsistency; the antidote is rules and independent institutions.
  • State capacity is the physical carrier of policy: strong without constraint is predation, constrained but weak is empty talk.

Chapter 6Comparative Systems and Social Orders

Opening question: The twentieth century ran a social experiment across half the planet: abolish the market and allocate all resources by central plan. Early on, industry surged; in the middle decades, shortage was universal; at the end, one system after another transitioned—why could planning mobilize at first yet ultimately lose to the market? And why did some transition countries join the developed economies (Poland, the Baltic states) while others sank into oligarchic economies (Russia in the 1990s)? Pull the camera back to the widest angle: on a peninsula whose people share the same language and ancestry, the night-time lights on one side blaze like a river of stars while the other side fades to near darkness—what lies between the light and the dark?

The core intuition

The planned economy has two fatal weak points. The information problem: Hayek pointed out that the knowledge of "which knowledge should allocate the resources" is itself dispersed across hundreds of millions of hands and cannot be gathered and reported upward—the price is humanity's invented information compressor, one number carrying the weight of everything; abolish prices, and the planning commission faces astronomically large product vectors and true preferences. The incentive problem: János Kornai pointed out that enterprises under a planned economy face a soft budget constraint—losses will be bailed out—so they scramble for targets and investment, understate capacity, and hoard inputs, and shortage becomes an institutional regularity rather than an accidental breakdown.

So what kind of social order can prosper over the long run? Two complementary vocabularies. North–Wallis–Weingast (NWW) distinguish the limited access order (the natural state: rents are created by restricting competition, so powerful elites profit from order and therefore support it) from the open access order (everyone may enter and form organizations, competition is not restricted by personal status, and order rests on neutral institutions); Acemoglu and Robinson distinguish inclusive institutions from extractive institutions: the former allow broad participation and creative destruction, the latter lock power and wealth inside a small circle. The two vocabularies point at the same thing: long-run prosperity = a state capable of keeping the peace + the openness and checks that keep that state from turning into a machine of predation.

Key concepts

Soft Budget Constraint
软预算约束
Kornai's term: the enterprise expects that losses will be covered (by the ministry, the bank, or the budget), the budget constraint never hardens, and incentives warp accordingly—grab inputs, understate output, never stop losses.
Limited Access Order / Natural State
有限准入秩序
The NWW framework: rents are created by limiting access to scarce goods (trade, land, office), and the rents make powerful coalitions profit from order, so peace is held together by interests rather than pure violence.
Open Access Order
权利开放秩序
Everyone may freely form organizations and hold impersonal legal and civil rights; competition is coordinated by neutral rules, and peace no longer depends on the distribution of rents.
Inclusive / Extractive Institutions
包容性 / 攫取性制度
Acemoglu and Robinson's pairing: inclusive institutions secure broad property rights and freedom of entry and allow creative destruction; extractive institutions are built so the few can extract from the many.
Historical footnote

The socialist calculation debate (1920–1945). Mises opened fire in 1920: without a market in the means of production there are no true prices, and rational calculation is impossible. Lange replied: let the planning commission "simulate the market by trial and error" and solve for general-equilibrium prices. Hayek's 1945 "The Use of Knowledge in Society" struck deeper: even if a solution existed in theory, the knowledge required is dispersed, tacit, and changing by the minute—the true function of prices is not "solving" but communication. Half a century later, the practice of the planning bureaus and the big-data-era debate (is an algorithmic planned economy feasible?) still run along this same line—and most people still have not finished reading the full rebuttals of Hayek and Kornai.

The real world

Peninsula lights and two transitions. In night-time satellite photographs, the Korean peninsula splits dark and bright along the 38th parallel—institutional economics' most famous "natural experiment": the same people and language, similar resources and starting points, yet decades of institutional divergence have produced an order-of-magnitude gap in income per person. The spectrum of transitions is just as instructive: Poland, with prior rule-of-law construction and the "institutional anchor" of joining the EU, quickly recovered its growth; Russia in the 1990s ran voucher privatization across a wasteland without the rule of law or competition, and managers and officials used their informational edge to carry off the public's shares—property rights without enforcement are not property rights, Chapter 1's lesson replayed in transition.

Common pitfalls

First, "planning failed because its leaders were stupid or greedy."—This demotes a structural problem to a character problem: even leaders both honest and brilliantly gifted would still face the twin mountains of information and incentives; conversely, the market needs no one to be a genius. Second, "copying good institutions into the constitution completes the reform."—Institutional transplants fail at a remarkable rate: many Latin American constitutions closely resemble that of the United States while their outcomes differ wildly; enforcement agencies, behavioral norms, and the balance of political forces (Chapter 3's path dependence) are an institution's flesh and blood. Third, "inclusive institutions are a wonder drug—one dose and done."—Institutions are a layered, nested ecosystem; forcing the seedlings up usually buys a backlash. The reformer's real problem is sequencing and fit, not ticking boxes on a list.

Quiz 6.1Kornai's "soft budget constraint" refers to:
Explanation: The "soft" in the soft budget constraint lies in the ex post expectation: the budget line is supposed to be a hard line between life and death, but under the planned system the enterprise knows that overspending and losses will ultimately be covered, so the rational strategy is to demand as much investment and as many materials as possible and hide true capacity. That is why shortage is institutional. A is a question of pricing rights; C says exactly the reverse—hardening the budget constraint (letting money-losers actually go bankrupt) is the core prescription of transition and state-enterprise reform.
Quiz 6.2In North–Wallis–Weingast theory, the main mechanism by which the "natural state" (limited access order) maintains internal peace is:
Explanation: The depth of the natural state is that order rests not on morality but on a structure of interests: the king grants the nobles monopolies and offices (rents), so the nobles back the crown rather than civil war; whoever stirs up trouble loses his rents. This explains why restricting access (inefficient) can buy peace (orderly), and also why the transition to the open access order is hard: to get elites to give up rents, they must first be convinced that the order after opening is safe for them too. A describes the open access order; C is utopia—historically, attempts to abolish the rent-distribution mechanism have usually brought crueler struggles over redistribution.

Chapter summary

  • Planning's two fatal weak points: information (prices are the communication system of dispersed knowledge) and incentives (the soft budget constraint).
  • Transition succeeds or fails on institutional support and execution, not on the speed of privatization itself.
  • The natural state buys peace with rent distribution; the open access order holds competition within neutral institutions.
  • Long-run prosperity = state capacity × open checks; missing either, a country slides into disorder or predation.
