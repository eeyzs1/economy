Track 04 · Core theory

Game Theory & Information Economics

博弈论与信息经济学

When your best choice depends on what others choose, and their best choices depend on you—that is a game. Game theory gives you a language for analyzing strategic interaction; information economics studies the distortions that arise in markets when the parties hold different information.

By the end of this track, you will be able to
  • Read and build payoff matrices and game trees, and identify dominant strategies
  • Use the best-response way of thinking to find Nash equilibria, and understand that equilibrium ≠ collective optimality
  • Explain the difference between adverse selection and moral hazard and their real-world consequences (the market for lemons, the insurance trap)
  • Analyze how signaling (education, warranties) and screening (deductibles, contract terms) mitigate information problems
  • Describe how mechanism design, as in auctions, "makes truth-telling the best strategy"
Prerequisites: 01 Microeconomics (marginal thinking)

Chapter 1The Language of Games

Opening question: Two suspects are interrogated separately: if both stay silent, each gets 1 year; if one confesses while the other stays silent, the confessor goes free and the silent one gets 10 years; if both confess, each gets 3 years. The detective leaves the room smiling—why is he so sure both will talk?

The core intuition

Put yourself in the other player's shoes, and then think one more round about their "thinking"—that is strategic reasoning. Game theory's standard assumptions: everyone is rational (maximizing their own payoff), and that fact itself is common knowledge (you know I am rational, I know you know…). In the Prisoner's Dilemma, whether the other player stays silent or confesses, confessing is better for me—this is a dominant strategy. When both players have a dominant strategy, the ending is all but sealed.

Key concepts

The Three Elements of a Game 博弈三要素
The players, their available strategies, and the payoffs under every combination of strategies. Write these three down clearly and the game is "modeled."
Dominant Strategy 占优策略
A strategy that is optimal no matter what the opponent does. With a dominant strategy in hand, a rational player need not guess about the opponent.
Payoff Matrix 收益矩阵
A table for a two-player simultaneous-move game: rows are one player's strategies, columns are the other's, and each cell lists both payoffs (row player, column player).
The real world

The advertising arms race: Coca-Cola and Pepsi both pour money into advertising versus neither spending (higher profits, but each fears losing market share); national tariff wars: mutual tariffs leave both sides worse off, yet cutting tariffs unilaterally feels like getting played; rat-race overtime: everyone would be better off if nobody worked late, but the one employee who stays late alone gets ahead. The shadow of the Prisoner's Dilemma falls everywhere.

Common pitfalls

"Game theory assumes people are cold-blooded and selfish, so it can't be realistic."—The "payoffs" in a payoff matrix can be anything you care about: a sense of fairness, reputation, other people's welfare. In experiments, many people reject an "unfair 90/10 split" in the ultimatum game, preferring mutual ruin—this does not overturn game theory; it shows that fairness sits inside their payoff functions (see Track 15, Behavioral Economics).

Quiz 1.1In the Prisoner's Dilemma, confessing is a dominant strategy because it…
Explanation: A dominant strategy is one that is "at least as good against every one of the opponent's strategies." If the partner stays silent: confess (released) > silent (1 year); if the partner confesses: confess (3 years) < silent (a 10-year sentence). Confessing is strictly better in both cases.
Quiz 1.2The outcome (Silent, Silent), with 1 year each, compared with (Confess, Confess), with 3 years each…
Explanation: Pareto superior: no one is worse off and at least one player is better off. (Silent, Silent) leaves both better off—but each has an incentive to deviate unilaterally, so it is not a Nash equilibrium. Individual rationality ≠ collective rationality: this is game theory's deepest lesson.

Chapter summary

  • A game = players + strategies + payoffs; rationality and common knowledge are the standard assumptions.
  • A dominant strategy is optimal regardless of what the opponent does; the Prisoner's Dilemma = dominant strategies on both sides producing an inferior outcome.
  • Payoffs can be anything players care about (fairness, reputation, long-run interests).

Chapter 2Nash Equilibrium

Opening question: Many games have no dominant strategy—my best choice depends on what you do. What, then, is "the solution" of a game? John Nash's answer: look for a strategy profile that no one wants to change unilaterally.

The core intuition

Nash equilibrium = a set of strategies in which each player's strategy is a best response to the equilibrium strategies of the others. The role-reversal check: hold everyone else's choices fixed—can I still do better on my own? If not, the profile is "pinned down." Equilibrium guarantees neither fairness nor optimality, only stability—it is the game's "center of gravity," not a moral ideal.

Key concepts

Nash Equilibrium 纳什均衡
A profile of mutual best responses: no one can gain by changing strategy unilaterally.
Best Response 最佳响应
Your optimal choice given the opponents' strategies. The universal way to find equilibria: for each of the opponent's strategies, map out your best response and look for the combination where they "hit each other."
Multiple Equilibria and Coordination 多重均衡与协调
When there is more than one equilibrium, "which one we play" depends on expectations, conventions, and communication—keyboard layouts, technology standards, and social platforms are all coordination games.
Common pitfalls

"The Nash equilibrium is a game's 'best result.'"—Flat wrong, and remarkably common. Equilibrium means only stability (no one wants to deviate alone). The Prisoner's Dilemma's equilibrium is mutual betrayal; the tragedy of the commons' equilibrium is overfishing. Precisely for this reason, institutional design often works by changing the game's payoff structure so that good outcomes become equilibria (Chapter 6).

Quiz 2.1To check whether a strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium, the standard question is…
Explanation: This is the operational version of the "mutual best response" check. If anyone can improve unilaterally, it is not an equilibrium; if no one can, the profile is stable. Maximizing total payoff (A) is a Pareto/social-optimum concept, orthogonal to equilibrium.
Quiz 2.2When a coordination game gets stuck at a "bad equilibrium" (think QWERTY keyboards or an outdated technology standard), the most plausible economic explanation is…
Explanation: In a coordination game, "what others use" determines "what I should use." When everyone expects everyone else to stay on the old standard, staying is each person's best response—the inferior equilibrium fulfills itself. This has nothing to do with irrationality; it is an expectations-coordination problem, and it foreshadows the "network effects" of Track 10, Industrial Organization, and the "path dependence" of Track 16, Institutional Economics.

Chapter summary

  • Nash equilibrium = mutual best responses = no one wants to deviate unilaterally.
  • Equilibrium explains "stable," not "best"; bad equilibria are everywhere.
  • With multiple equilibria, expectations, conventions, and communication decide which one prevails.

Chapter 3Classic Games and Applications

Opening question: If the Prisoner's Dilemma is played only once, betrayal is inevitable. But if the same two people will be dealing with each other for a lifetime—why do a "one-shot deal" and a "long-term relationship" give completely different answers?

The core intuition: the shadow of the future

In a repeated game, today's betrayal invites tomorrow's retaliation. The famous tit-for-tat strategy: cooperate in the first round, then mirror whatever the other player did in the previous round. As long as both sides care enough about the future (a large enough discount factor), "cooperate forever" can be an equilibrium—and reputation is born. The flip side: with someone you will never meet again, the optimal strategy is often to "take the money and run"—the mediocre food at tourist-trap restaurants is a property of the game's structure, not a moral slide.

Three classic structures

GameStructureThe tragedy at equilibriumReal-world counterparts
Prisoner's DilemmaBetrayal is a dominant strategyIndividual rationality → collective irrationalityTariff wars, advertising wars, carbon emissions
Tragedy of the CommonsAn N-player Prisoner's DilemmaThe resource is overused until it collapsesOverfishing, congestion, rat races
ChickenWhoever swerves loses; if neither swerves, both crashThe standoff escalatesDebt-ceiling brinkmanship, price wars
The real world · OPEC

OPEC is a cartel: members jointly restrain output to prop up prices. But each member is tempted to quietly pump more (the others shoulder the restraint that keeps prices high, while you sell more and earn more)—a classic multi-player Prisoner's Dilemma. That is why OPEC periodically shows "production-cut agreements" coexisting with "members pumping above quota." What holds a cartel together is precisely the repeated game: betray once, and a price war awaits in the future. The oil-price collapses of 2014 and 2020 can both be read as the start of a "punishment phase."

Common pitfalls

"As long as an agreement is signed, the Prisoner's Dilemma is solved."—The agreement itself does not change the game: if breach is costless, a signing ceremony does nothing to the temptation to betray. What actually works is a credible punishment/enforcement mechanism (repeated dealings, collateral, legal coercion, reputation records). Which yields a deep insight: institutions = the rules of the game changed by enforcement (see Track 16, Political Economy & Institutional Economics).

Quiz 3.1Cooperation is easier to sustain in a repeated Prisoner's Dilemma when…
Explanation: The condition for cooperation is roughly "value of future cooperation > temptation to betray today," that is, a discount factor large enough. Conversely, a game with a known end unravels cooperation by backward induction: betrayal is certain in the last round → reputation is worthless in the second-to-last → … a chain reaction of collapse (end-of-term clearance sales, soon-to-quit employees coasting out their notice).
Quiz 3.2The essence of the tragedy of the commons (overgrazing a shared pasture) is…
Explanation: Add one more sheep: the weight it gains is entirely mine, while the cost of pasture degradation is spread across everyone. Privatized benefits + socialized costs = individually optimal choices aggregating into a collective disaster. The directions for a fix: delineating property rights (private contracting), tradable quotas, community self-governance (Ostrom; see Track 12, Environmental & Resource Economics).

Chapter summary

  • Repeated play lets "future retaliation" discipline today's betrayal—reputation is capital.
  • Tragedy of the commons = an N-player Prisoner's Dilemma: private benefits, shared costs.
  • Agreements alone are useless; credible enforcement mechanisms are what matter.

Chapter 4Asymmetric Information: The Market for Lemons and Moral Hazard

Opening question: In a used-car market, a good car is worth 100,000 yuan and a lemon only 40,000, yet neither buyers nor sellers can size up a car's condition. Buyers will offer only 70,000 for "average quality"—what happens next? Nobel laureate George Akerlof's 1970 answer shook economics: the market may simply vanish.

The core intuition: the adverse-selection death spiral

Sellers know the car's condition; buyers do not. Buyers offer the expected value → good-car sellers feel shortchanged and withdraw → the share of lemons in the market rises → buyers offer even less → even better cars withdraw… bad money drives out good, until only lemons are left. What asymmetric information destroys is not one particular trade but the market's very existence. Insurance works the same way: one-size-fits-all premiums → healthy people drop out → the pool fills with the sick → premiums rise again → even more healthy people leave.

Key concepts

Information Asymmetry 信息不对称
One party to a transaction holds key information the other does not have. Before the contract is signed it breeds adverse selection; after the contract is signed, moral hazard.
Adverse Selection 逆向选择
The "bad types" on the informed side self-select into the transaction (lemons, the seriously ill buying insurance), and the price mechanism squeezes the good types out of the market.
Moral Hazard 道德风险
Shirking or risk-taking that emerges because behavior after signing cannot be observed: fully insured drivers drive more recklessly, guaranteed banks dare to make risky loans.
The real world · Moral hazard in the 2008 financial crisis

One main thread of the subprime crisis: the originate-to-distribute model. Banks issued mortgages and immediately securitized and sold them off, so default risk was no longer theirs—the gains from monitoring borrowers accrued to someone else, while the commissions from lending accrued to them. Asymmetric information + misaligned incentives = collapsing loan quality. The post-crisis wave of global regulatory reform (the Dodd–Frank Act) is in large part an "anti-moral-hazard design manual": risk retention, deferred compensation, stress tests.

Quiz 4.1The root cause of the used-car market's collapse is…
Explanation: Prices are supposed to convey scarcity, but under asymmetric information the price conveys an expectation of "average quality," which in turn shapes the mix of goods that stay in the market—that is exactly what "adverse" means: the selection mechanism selects the bad types. Good cars do not cease to exist; they simply cannot fetch what they are worth, so they exit.
Quiz 4.2Which of the following is moral hazard (rather than adverse selection)?
Explanation: The mnemonic: hidden type before the contract → adverse selection; hidden action after the contract → moral hazard. A and C are both "type" problems (choices driven by hidden health status) that occur before contracting, while the pool is forming; B is a change in behavior after the contract.

Chapter summary

  • Adverse selection: hidden types; the price mechanism selects against good risks (the market for lemons).
  • Moral hazard: hidden actions; misaligned incentives invite shirking and risk-taking.
  • Asymmetric information erodes the market's very existence, giving rise to warranties, mandatory disclosure, regulation, and other institutions.

Chapter 5Signaling and Screening

Opening question: If what universities teach is never used on the job, why do employers still favor graduates of elite schools—and why do students still fight so hard to get in? Michael Spence's signaling theory offers an unsettling yet deeply explanatory answer.

The core intuition

An effective signal must be costly, with costs that vary by type: able people earn a degree more easily than less able ones (they learn fast and suffer less), so the very act of "being willing to pay the price for a degree" conveys information. Conversely, the uninformed side can proactively design a menu that makes the other side reveal itself—an insurer offering a "high-deductible, low-premium" plan alongside a "low-deductible, high-premium" plan lets the healthy self-select into the former and the sickly into the latter. This is screening. A signal is sent voluntarily by the informed side; screening is elicited by the uninformed side.

Key concepts

Signaling 信号
The informed side communicates its type through costly actions. Key point: the cost of the signal must be negatively correlated with type, or it will be mimicked and lose its force.
Screening 筛选
The uninformed side designs a menu of options (contracts, prices, terms) so that people of different types self-select and reveal their type.
Separating / Pooling Equilibrium 分离均衡与混同均衡
Separating: different types choose different actions, so information is revealed; pooling: everyone chooses the same action, so information stays hidden.
The real world · Signals everywhere

Warranty promises: a maker bold enough to promise "free replacement for three years" is saying "my return rate is low"—a maker with a high return rate cannot afford to mimic that. Bride prices (caili, China's customary betrothal gifts) and diamond rings: costly, functionally useless commitment spending, given a cool-headed reading by signaling theory. Blind-box collaborations and luxury logos: consumption as a status signal (Veblen goods). Voluntary financial disclosure: good companies volunteer more, bad ones stay silent—the economic rationale behind mandatory-disclosure regulation.

Common pitfalls

"Signaling theory says education is useless and college is a waste."—Not accurate. Education can be simultaneously human capital (genuinely raising productivity; see Track 09, Labor Economics) and a signal (displaying ability). Both effects are real; the dispute is only over the proportions. Even a pure-signaling model has social implications: when everyone stays in school one more year to "prove themselves," the signaling arms race is individually rational but socially wasteful (compare the advertising war in Chapter 1).

Quiz 5.1For a signal to separate types, the key is…
Explanation: If good and bad types face the same cost of signaling, bad types mimic effortlessly and the signal degenerates into noise. "Expensive" is not the point; the type-dependent cost gap is. This also explains why certificates "anyone can easily earn" depreciate fast.
Quiz 5.2An insurer setting up deductible tiers for customers to choose from is an example of…
Explanation: Who is the uninformed side? The insurer (it does not know your health). It designs two contracts: high deductible + low premium attracts those confident in their health; low deductible + high premium attracts those who know their risk is high. The menu makes types surface automatically—the textbook posture of screening.

Chapter summary

  • Signaling = initiated by the informed side, costly, with type-linked costs.
  • Screening = the uninformed side designs a menu so types self-select.
  • A separating equilibrium reveals information; a pooling equilibrium preserves ignorance; signaling arms races can be socially inefficient.

Chapter 6Mechanism Design: Game Theory in Reverse

Opening question: Auctioning the same object: an English ascending auction, a Dutch descending auction, a first-price sealed-bid auction, a second-price sealed-bid auction (highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid)—which one gets the seller the highest price? Which one gets buyers to tell the truth?

The core intuition

Game theory says: "given the rules, predict behavior." Mechanism design runs it in reverse: "given the behavior you want, work backward to the rules." William Vickrey's insight: in a second-price auction, bidding your true valuation is the optimal strategy—bidding higher never makes you pay more (you pay the second-highest bid), while bidding lower can needlessly lose you an auction that would have been profitable. Truth-telling becomes a dominant strategy: the Holy Grail of mechanism design, incentive compatibility.

Three touchstones of mechanism design

Incentive Compatibility 激励相容
Under the mechanism, reporting honestly (or taking the desired action) is each participant's optimal choice. The rules and self-interest point in the same direction.
Individual Rationality 个体理性
Participating beats not participating; otherwise no one shows up. A good mechanism satisfies both IR and IC.
Revelation Principle 显示原理
Whatever outcome a mechanism can achieve, some "direct, truth-telling" mechanism achieves as well—so the analysis can restrict itself to truth-telling mechanisms.
The real world · Spectrum auctions and matching markets

Since 1993, countries have allocated radio spectrum by auction in place of administrative licensing—work that won Stanford's Milgrom and Wilson the 2020 Nobel Prize in Economics: their "simultaneous multi-round auction" lets telecom operators coordinate package bids across thousands of licenses, selling a public resource at a fair price. Kidney paired exchange: incompatible donor–patient pairs swap with one another, and the designed chain matchings have saved tens of thousands of lives. School assignment: reforms of the school-matching mechanisms in Boston and New York (the deferred-acceptance algorithm) sharply reduced strategic rank-order gaming—mechanism design lives inside your school preference form.

Common pitfalls

"Second-price auctions shortchange the seller (they collect only the second-highest price)."—A trick of intuition. Precisely because truth-telling is a dominant strategy, every buyer bids full value, and competition pushes that "second-highest price" close to the equilibrium revenue of a first-price auction—the rough meaning of the celebrated revenue equivalence theorem. Different rules can deliver the same expected revenue, but the second-price format's simplicity (no need to second-guess rivals) makes it the first choice in many settings.

Quiz 6.1In a second-price (Vickrey) auction, your optimal bid is…
Explanation: Bid your true value v: if you win, you pay the second-highest bid (< v, a gain); if you lose, the second price was ≥ v, so winning was never profitable for you. Underbid: you might lose when the second price is < v, sacrificing (v − second price) for nothing. Overbid: you might win a money-losing auction when the second price is > v. Truth-telling is therefore weakly dominant—the mechanism turns honesty into self-interest.
Quiz 6.2The relationship between "mechanism design" and "game theory" is…
Explanation: "Game theory = given the rules of the game, how will players play? Mechanism design = wanting the players to play this way, what rules should be set?" Hurwicz, Maskin, and Myerson won the 2007 Nobel Prize for this "reverse engineering." It is the theoretical foundation for the design of auctions, regulation, voting, and platform rules.

Chapter summary

  • Mechanism design: target behavior → work backward to the rules; check IR + IC.
  • The second-price auction makes truth-telling a dominant strategy.
  • From spectrum to kidney exchange, mechanism design is rewriting the rules of real markets.

Wrap-upNext steps & further reading

Game theory and information economics are the "second pillar" of modern economics: 10 Industrial Organization (oligopoly pricing, platform strategy), 16 Political Economy & Institutional Economics (institutions = the rules of the game made binding by enforcement), and 12 Environmental & Resource Economics (international climate negotiations = a multi-stage Prisoner's Dilemma) all rest on its foundations.

  • Dixit & Nalebuff, Thinking Strategically—the best popular entry point: case-driven intuition for games.
  • Avinash Dixit, Games of Strategy / Robert Gibbons, A Primer in Game Theory—the standard textbook and the concise primer.
  • Akerlof (1970), "The Market for Lemons"—the original is only 14 pages long, and it changed economics.
  • Alvin Roth, Who Gets What—and Why—a popular tour of matching markets and mechanism design.
